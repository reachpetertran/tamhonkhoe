Stoicism 4 – Week 5: The circles of concern

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: draw your circles of concern, from the inside out. Each day, do one small thing for someone in an outer circle, as if drawing them one circle closer: a neighbour you rarely greet, a colleague in another department, a stranger who needs directions.

Measurable goal: one small thing for someone in an outer circle each day, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Stoicism teaches that all people share one rational nature, so we belong to the community of humankind, not only to our family or our city.

Hierocles, a Stoic of the 2nd century CE, left a famous image for this idea. His work survives only in fragments copied by Stobaeus. In tamhonkhoe’s own summary: each person stands at the centre of many circles. The innermost circle is one’s own mind; next comes close family; then relatives; then the community one lives in; then people of neighbouring towns; then one’s fellow countrymen; and the outermost circle is the whole human race. Hierocles advises drawing the circles closer to the centre: treating people in the outer circles a little more closely than their place would suggest.

Reading it in context

  • The description of Hierocles’ circles above is tamhonkhoe’s summary, not a verbatim translation. The order of the circles follows the fragment preserved by Stobaeus; English readers can look for Thomas Taylor’s translation (1822).
  • "Antoninus" is Marcus Aurelius’s name as emperor. His line does not tell anyone to give up their homeland: he still carried out the duties of a Roman emperor, while remembering that he was one person among all people.
  • [Expert judgement] Drawing the outer circles closer does not mean neglecting the inner ones. Hierocles starts from oneself and one’s family; caring for distant people does not replace looking after yourself and those at home.
  • This exercise is about small everyday actions; it does not discuss politics or nations.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A systematic review of volunteering (40 papers): cohort studies found volunteers had less depression and greater life satisfaction; pooling 5 cohort studies, volunteers had a lower risk of death (RR = 0.78; 95% CI 0.66–0.90). This is a correlation: randomised trials have not confirmed these benefits. (Jenkinson et al. 2013)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experimental studies with 4,045 people: doing kind things for others raised the well-being of those who did them by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] Neither study measured Hierocles’ "circles" exercise. The main aim of the exercise is that the other person is cared for, not a benefit to the practitioner.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes the first time, a few minutes each day.

  1. Read (first time, 3 minutes). Read the summary of Hierocles and Marcus’s passage.
  2. Draw your circles (10 minutes, once). Draw 5–7 concentric circles on a sheet of paper. Write names or groups in each: yourself; family; relatives and close friends; colleagues and neighbours; people in your city; people far away.
  3. One action each day. Choose someone in an outer circle and do one small thing: greet the building’s security guard and learn their name; help a colleague from another department; give directions to a stranger; pick up litter in a shared walkway; message a relative you have not contacted for a long time.
  4. One line (2 minutes, evening). Whom did I draw one circle closer today? Draw a small arrow on your sheet.
  5. Keep the inner circles. Still make time each day for yourself and your family. If you are tired this week, a greeting is enough.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "My circles" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Draw 3 circles, one inside another. In the middle the child draws themselves; in the second circle the people at home; in the outer circle the people they often see: their teacher, classmates, the shopkeeper, the security guard.
  2. Ask: "In the outer circle, who would you like to do something kind for?"
  3. The child chooses one small thing: greeting the security guard, drawing a picture for their teacher, inviting a new friend to play.
  4. The adult goes along when the child does it. Don’t make the child talk to strangers if they feel shy.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have traumatic flashbacks, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", many sleepless nights, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Caring for others is wearing you out or making you neglect yourself and your family. Then go back to the inner circles first.
  • Someone in an outer circle behaves suspiciously or makes you feel unsafe. Staying safe matters more than the exercise.
  • In that case stop practising and see Get help now.
