Stoicism 2 – Week 1: What belongs to us

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read two passages of Epictetus on the three areas of training slowly, then each evening spend 20–25 minutes on one page of a three-column journal: Desire, Action, Judgement.

Measurable goal: fill all three columns on at least 5 of 7 evenings.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Stoicism teaches that living well has to be practised daily, like an athlete trains. Epictetus (about AD 50–135) divides this training into three areas: what we desire or avoid, what we do towards others, and what we accept as true. This course goes through all three in 8 weeks, each week leaning towards one area.

Reading it in context

  • [Verified] The scholar Pierre Hadot read Marcus Aurelius’ Meditations through three "disciplines" taken from Epictetus: the discipline of desire, of action, and of judgement (or assent). This course takes its name from that reading; Epictetus himself speaks of three "topics" (topoi).
  • "Not free from affects like a statue": Epictetus says plainly that a Stoic learner still keeps the roles of child, parent and citizen. The training here is not about cutting off feelings or withdrawing from life.
  • The second passage is addressed to someone threatened with chains and exile. The man saying it had once been enslaved. [Expert judgement] "Going with smiles" is the ideal of a long-time practitioner, not a demand placed on someone who is struggling.
  • "I must die" appears only as an example. Death is the subject of Stoicism 3 and is not discussed here.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 146 randomised studies of writing about one’s experiences and feelings (expressive writing) found a positive but very small overall effect (r = 0.075) on mental and physical health. (Frattaroli 2006)
  • [Inference] This week’s three-column journal is not the same as the writing tasks in those studies: here we record and sort, rather than write deeply about feelings. The result only suggests that regular writing may help a little; it does not prove this course’s method.
  • [Verified] A pre-registered study of 1,307 people found that a common misreading of Stoicism (wanting to show fewer emotions, wanting to feel less) goes with lower well-being. This is correlational. (Karl et al. 2022)

Limits: we have not found a peer-reviewed review that evaluates a programme based on Epictetus’ three disciplines as such.

3. Practice for adults

20–25 minutes a day in total.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes, morning). Read the first passage. Remember three words: Desire – Action – Judgement.
  2. Three-column journal (15 minutes, evening). Draw three columns on a page and write a few lines in each: - Desire: what did I want today, what did I fear? Was it up to me or not up to me? - Action: what did I do today for someone, in one of my roles (child, parent, friend, colleague)? - Judgement: was there a thought I believed too quickly?
  3. Choose one thing for tomorrow (3 minutes). Underline one line you want to do better tomorrow.
  4. Tick it. At the end of the week, count the evenings with all three columns filled; the goal is at least 5 of 7. If a column is empty, leave it empty; there is no need to invent something.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Three questions before bed" (5 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The adult asks three questions, one finger for each: - "What did you want today?" - "What good thing did you do for someone today?" - "Was there a time you thought something and then found you were wrong?"
  2. The adult answers the three questions too, briefly and honestly.
  3. There are no right or wrong answers. If the child can’t remember, move on to the next question.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Journaling makes you blame yourself more or go over the same thing again and again. Then cut down to one column, or pause.
  • People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising for more than 20 minutes a day.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Tonight write three lines, one per column: one desire, one action, one thought you believed too quickly.
