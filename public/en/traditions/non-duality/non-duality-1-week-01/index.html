Introduction to the Gita and the Upanishads – Week 1: Work without clinging to fruits

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: getting to know the Bhagavad Gita, and a slow reading of its most quoted lines: working without clinging to the fruits of the work.

Measurable goal: each day choose one task, do it as well as you can, let go of the result and write one line; on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

What this course is and is not

The course reads two kinds of text that many Hindu traditions hold as scripture: the Bhagavad Gita and the Upanishads. It describes what these texts teach; learners do not need to be Hindu, and the course does not ask anyone to change their beliefs.

The name of this section, "Non-duality", comes from one well-known reading of these texts (Advaita Vedanta, usually linked with Shankara). It is not the only reading. Other schools, for example those of Ramanuja or Madhva, read the same texts but understand the relation between human beings and God differently. The course reads the texts and does not take the side of any school; where readings differ, the lesson says so.

What the Bhagavad Gita is

[Traditional teaching] The Bhagavad Gita ("Song of the Lord") has 18 chapters and is part of the epic Mahabharata. It is a conversation between prince Arjuna and Krishna, his charioteer, whom the text presents as the Divine. The conversation takes place on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, before a war between members of the same family. Arjuna lays down his bow and does not want to fight; Krishna answers with teaching on duty, on the soul, on action and on devotion.

Many readers, Gandhi among them, understand the battlefield as an image of the struggle inside each person; others read the book within its epic setting. This course does not discuss war. It only reads a few passages about how to live and work day to day.

The lesson uses Edwin Arnold's English verse translation (1885). Arnold does not number the verses; the verse numbers in the lesson are matched by content.

Work without clinging to fruits

[Traditional teaching] The passage below is in chapter 2, where Krishna speaks of the path of action.

A note on context: [Inference] Verse 2:47 is usually understood as: a person has a share in their actions, not in their fruits; so do not make the fruit your motive, but do not stop acting because of that either. The teaching does not say to work carelessly or not to care about results; it says to work wholeheartedly without letting craving or fear about the result steer you. The tradition calls this path karma yoga, the path of action. The verses that follow (2:48) were read in Convergence 1 – Week 9: Letting go of the ego.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A review of 20 meta-analyses found that Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), in which people practise living by what they value rather than by their feelings or short-term results, works for anxiety, depression and chronic pain, about as well as cognitive behavioural therapy. (Gloster et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] ACT is guided therapy, not a self-study exercise, and the research does not measure the Gita's teaching. The lesson only borrows a related idea: choose a task by what you value, then let go of the part you do not control. No study has evaluated this exercise directly.

3. Practice for adults

About 10–12 minutes a day.

  1. Read slowly (2 minutes). Read the three lines of the Gita twice.
  2. Choose one task (2 minutes). A small task in your day: cooking a meal, writing an email, teaching your child a lesson, fixing something broken.
  3. Split a sheet of paper (2 minutes). Left: "my part" (my attention, my care, the time I give). Right: "not mine to decide" (whether others praise or criticise, the final result, luck).
  4. Do the task as well as you can. While you work, if you notice you are worrying about the right-hand column, gently come back to the left.
  5. Write one line (3 minutes) at the end of the day: "Today I did my part by …; what was not mine, I let go of …".

Letting go of the result does not mean not learning from it. If the task went wrong, add one line: "Next time, my part will change by …".

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The tower and the wind" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The child builds a tower of wooden blocks or Lego for 3 minutes, as carefully as possible.
  2. The adult praises what the child did: "You placed every block so carefully."
  3. Together, make a "wind" (blow gently or fan with a hand) at the tower. It may stand or it may fall.
  4. Ask: "Which part was yours? Which part was not up to you?" The child's part was building carefully; the wind was not up to them.

If the child is sad when the tower falls, stop blowing; take a photo of the tower and put it away. The aim is for the child to see what they did well, not to make them happy about losing what they built.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "Letting go of the result" leads you to neglect something important (health, money, the safety of people close to you). Those things need you to watch results and to ask people with the right expertise.
  • You are using this verse to endure work or a situation that is harming you. The teaching does not tell anyone to stay where they are exploited or abused.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
