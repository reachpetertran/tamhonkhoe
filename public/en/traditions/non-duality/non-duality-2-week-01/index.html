Karma yoga and meditation in the Gita – Week 1: Even-minded in success and failure

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Before you start this course: this is a Level 2 course, about 20–30 minutes a day for 8 weeks, with sitting time growing from 15 to 25 minutes. If you have never read the Gita, it is best to take Non-duality 1 first. If you have ever had psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask your doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

This week: a slow reading of two passages from chapter 2 of the Gita about evenness of mind, and the start of a daily habit of sitting quietly.

Measurable goal: sit quietly for 15 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; each evening note one thing that went well, one that did not, and how stirred up you were (0–10).

1. Traditional teaching

What this course is and is not

The course reads three chapters of the Bhagavad Gita (chapters 2, 3 and 6) that speak of two paths the text calls yoga: the path of action (karma yoga) and the path of meditation (chapter 6, often called dhyana yoga). "Yoga" here does not mean physical exercise; in the Gita the word broadly means a discipline, a path that joins a person to what is highest.

The course describes what the Gita teaches; learners do not need to be Hindu, and the course does not ask anyone to change their beliefs or to be devoted to anyone. As in Non-duality 1, the section name "Non-duality" comes from one reading (Advaita Vedanta); other schools, such as those of Ramanuja and Madhva, read the same text differently. The course does not discuss war in the Gita's setting and does not teach ritual, mantras or Sanskrit.

The meditation part (from Week 3) follows closely how the Gita describes the seat, the posture and the gaze, but that is tamhonkhoe's reading of the text, not a substitute for a teacher within the tradition.

The lessons use Edwin Arnold's verse translation (1885). Arnold does not number the verses and writes "Yog" for yoga; verse numbers in the lessons are matched by content.

Evenness is yoga

[Traditional teaching] In Non-duality 1 – Week 1 we read verse 2:47: work without clinging to the fruits. The very next verse says that one who works this way stays even towards gain and loss.

A note on context: [Inference] The Sanskrit word in 2:48 is samatva, usually translated "evenness" or "equanimity": keeping the mind the same in success and in failure. Arnold renders it "equability". Evenness does not mean having no feelings; verse 2:56 says such a person is not carried away by sorrow or joy. A "Muni" is a quiet one, a sage.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A systematic review of 47 randomised trials with 3,515 people: meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, but are not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] A review of 20 meta-analyses found that Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), in which people practise letting feelings come and go without being run by them, works for anxiety, depression and chronic pain, about as well as cognitive behavioural therapy. (Gloster et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] Neither body of research measures the Gita's teaching, and ACT is guided therapy. No study has evaluated this course directly.

3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

  1. Read slowly (3 minutes). Read both passages. The second time, read only the line "equability Is Yog".
  2. Sit quietly (15 minutes). Sit on a chair or a cushion, back upright but not stiff. Let your attention rest on the breath. When a thought pulls you away, notice it and come back. This week the posture need not be exact; Week 3 reads closely how the Gita describes the seat.
  3. Evening note (2 minutes). Three lines: "Today … went well; … did not. When it went well I felt …; when it did not I felt …. My highest level of being stirred up today: …/10."

Step 3 is not about forcing yourself to feel the same; it is about noticing how far you were carried. At the end of the week, look back: did what went well or what went badly stir you more?

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The paper boat and the wave" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Fold a paper boat and put it in a shallow basin of water.
  2. The adult stirs gently to make waves: the boat rocks, then settles. The child says "wave coming" and "wave gone".
  3. Tell one small happy thing and one small sad thing from the child's day (for example: being praised, dropping a pencil). Ask: "Was there a wave inside you then? Did the wave pass?"
  4. The adult tells one of their own first, so the child sees that everyone has waves.

Never tease a child for being sad or angry; tell them that waves are normal. Use a shallow basin and dry the floor afterwards.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "Evenness" turns into forcing yourself not to feel sad or angry. Feelings still come; the exercise is only to notice them.
  • You use this teaching to put up with a situation that is harming you. "Even towards gain and loss" does not mean staying where you are exploited or abused.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have ever had psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask your doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
