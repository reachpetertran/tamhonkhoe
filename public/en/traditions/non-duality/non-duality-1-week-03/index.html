Introduction to the Gita and the Upanishads – Week 3: Lifting yourself up

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: a slow reading of the Gita passage saying that each person can be their own friend or their own enemy, and practice in speaking to yourself as you would to a friend.

Measurable goal: each morning write or say one encouraging sentence to yourself, on at least 5 of 7 days; at the end of the week, count how many times you turned a self-critical sentence into a friend's sentence.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 6 of the Gita is about meditation and self-mastery. Before describing the seat and the way of sitting, Krishna speaks about a person's relation to themselves.

A note on context: Arnold's version is hard to read because of a play on words. His footnote explains that the Sanskrit uses one word, Atman, in two senses, so he translates it sometimes as "Self" and sometimes as "Soul". His footnote marker after the last line is left out of the quotation. [Inference] The main idea is usually summed up like this: raise yourself up, do not drag yourself down; you are your own friend, and you can also be your own enemy. For someone who has mastered themselves, they are their own friend; for someone who has not, they harm themselves like an enemy.

[Inference] In the Gita, "mastering oneself" is tied to meditation and to not being ruled by desire. This lesson uses only a small part of that idea: how you speak to yourself day to day. Schools read the word Atman here differently (the everyday self, the mind, or the deepest Self); the reviewer from the tradition will say more.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 21 trials of loving-kindness and compassion meditation in the workplace found lower burnout and stress and higher self-compassion; results vary a lot between studies. (Wang et al. 2024)
  • [Verified] 33 studies of self-control training: a small effect, smaller still after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017)
  • [Inference] No study has evaluated the "one encouraging sentence each morning" exercise directly. The self-control research suggests not expecting large or quick change; the exercise aims at a small, kind habit towards yourself.

3. Practice for adults

About 8–10 minutes a day.

  1. Read slowly (2 minutes) each morning. Read the Gita passage once, then the summary: "you are your own friend".
  2. Write one encouraging sentence (2 minutes). A specific sentence for today's tasks, the kind a good friend would say. For example: "Today I only need to finish the first step of the report"; "Yesterday I was tired and still picked up the kids on time".
  3. Say the sentence once, aloud or silently.
  4. During the day, catch one self-critical sentence. When you hear something like "I'm useless" in your head, ask: "What would a good friend say to me right now?" Write the friend's sentence next to it.
  5. End of the week (5 minutes). Count the sentences you changed. Choose the encouraging sentence you like best to keep using.

The encouraging sentence has to be true. There is no need to overdo it ("I'm the best"); a true, kind sentence is stronger than a big one you do not believe.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Cheering myself on" (5 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The adult asks: "When your friend drops something, what would you say to them?" The child thinks of a kind sentence.
  2. "And when you drop something, could you say that same sentence to yourself?"
  3. Together, choose a short cheering sentence for the child (for example: "It's OK, I'll try again"). Write it on paper; the child adds a drawing.
  4. Stick it where the child often looks. The adult says their own cheering sentence aloud too.

Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: Kind to myself (in Vietnamese).

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The self-critical voice in your head is very harsh, lasts for days, or you feel worthless. That is a time to talk to a professional, not only to encourage yourself.
  • The exercise makes you blame yourself more for "not managing to think positively". That is fine; stop and switch to something simpler.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
