Introduction to the Gita and the Upanishads – Week 5: All is covered by the Lord

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: a slow reading of the opening verse of the Isha Upanishad, one of the shortest Upanishads, and noticing the moments when you want what someone else has.

Measurable goal: slow reading for 10 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; each day note one moment when you noticed wanting something of someone else's, and one thing you already have.

1. Traditional teaching

The Isha Upanishad

[Traditional teaching] The Isha Upanishad has only 18 verses. According to Paramananda's introduction, it is the closing chapter of the Shukla (White) Yajur Veda, and its title comes from its opening words Isa-vasya, "God-covered". The word Isa ("Lord") is a more personal name than those usually found in the Upanishads, such as Brahman or Atman.

A note on context: the words in brackets are the translator's, added to explain the meaning. Paramananda explains "covering all things with the Lord" as perceiving the divine presence everywhere. He also notes that Shankara takes the word "enjoy" here to mean "protect" as well.

[Expert judgement] Schools read verse VI differently. The non-dual (Advaita) reading takes it to mean that the Self in each person and the deepest reality are one. Theistic readings, such as Ramanuja's, take it to mean that all beings live in God and God is in all beings, but that human beings and God are not simply one. The lesson does not take sides; it uses only what the readings share: seeing all things as worthy of respect, and not coveting what belongs to others.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] Meta-analysis of 753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples: people who put strong weight on money and possessions had slightly lower well-being (r = −0.19 for measures of materialistic values and beliefs). This is a correlation. (Dittmar et al. 2014)
  • [Inference] A correlation does not show which causes which: less happy people may turn more to possessions. No study has evaluated slow reading of the Upanishads. The exercise does not promise to make you happier; it only helps you notice a habit of the mind.

3. Practice for adults

About 10–12 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (10 minutes). - Read verse I three times, each time more slowly. On the third reading, stop at whichever phrase catches your attention. - Sit quietly for one minute with that phrase. - Read verse VI once. - Write one line: "Today the phrase … reminds me …".
  2. During the day: notice one moment of wanting. When you find yourself wanting what someone else has (a new phone, a house, a trip, success), write it down without blaming yourself.
  3. Next to it, write one thing you already have that relates to it. For example: "Wanted a big house like A's; I have a quiet corner where my child can study."

The exercise does not ask you to give up goals or stop working for a better life. It only separates "wanting to have" from "craving what is someone else's".

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Look really closely" (5 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Choose an ordinary thing in the house: a leaf, an orange, a pebble.
  2. Look at it really closely together for one minute without talking.
  3. Each person names three things they had not noticed before (a vein, a scratch, a smell).
  4. Ask: "Is there something in our home you've just noticed is beautiful?"

There is no need to talk to the child about the Lord or about doctrine; the game only practises looking closely and valuing ordinary things.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You begin to feel that everything around you is "unreal" or strange. "The unreal" in the text does not mean that the world or the people you love are not real; stop reading and talk to someone you trust.
  • The exercise makes you feel guilty for wanting enough for your life. Wanting enough food, shelter and schooling for your family is not "coveting".
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Write down one thing you have wanted of someone else's, and one thing you already have.
