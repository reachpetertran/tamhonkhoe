Presence in ordinary tasks – Week 4: The meal

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: the ordinary task everyone does every day is eating. The Gita sees a meal as a small offering that links the person eating with rain, earth and other people.

Measurable goal: one meal a day: pause for 3 breaths before eating, say a word of thanks (or the prayer of your own tradition), eat the first 5 mouthfuls slowly, on at least 5 of 7 days. No counting calories, no weighing, no eating less.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In chapter 3 the Gita speaks of yajña, offering, and of the circle that links people and the world: food feeds people, and rain feeds the food.

In chapter 4 the Gita says that for one who has let go of craving, every act is an offering, and every part of the offering is Brahman.

A note on context: [Inference] "Brahm" is Arnold's spelling of Brahman, the ultimate reality of the Upanishads. Verse 4:24 describes a fire offering: ghee and grain poured into the fire, and "the flesh it eats" is what the fire consumes, the offering itself. Many Hindu websites say this verse is often recited before meals, treating eating as offering food into the "fire" within. The lesson only describes this; it does not teach reciting the verse in Sanskrit or performing a ritual.

"Eat sin and drink of sin" is about a person who thinks only of themselves and spreads a feast without sharing. It is not a reproach to anyone who eats; eating enough is good for the body.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 24 experimental studies: eating while distracted increased intake moderately at that meal (SMD 0.39) and more at a later meal (SMD 0.76); remembering the meal just eaten reduced later intake. (Robinson et al. 2013)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 22 studies: eating slowly went with lower intake in the meal (SMD 0.45); hunger at the end of the meal and up to 3.5 hours later did not differ. (Robinson et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 17 studies, 182,836 children and adolescents: eating 3 or more family meals a week went with healthier eating habits. This is a correlation. (Hammons and Fiese 2011)
  • [Inference] The lesson uses these studies to talk about being present at a meal, not to help anyone lose weight. No study has looked at saying thanks or reciting verse 4:24 before eating.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes, during one meal a day. If you have done Body 1, this is the next step: the same slow eating, with the Gita's view added.

  1. Read slowly (2 minutes, before the meal). Read both passages. Pause at "food comes of rain".
  2. Pause for 3 breaths. Sit down and put your chopsticks or spoon down. Look at the plate.
  3. Remember three sources. Think of three things that made the meal: rain and soil, the grower or seller, the cook.
  4. A word of thanks. Believers say the prayer of their own tradition. Others might say: "Thank you to the rain, the soil and the people who made this meal." This sentence is tamhonkhoe's suggestion.
  5. Five slow mouthfuls. Put your chopsticks or spoon down between mouthfuls. Notice taste, warmth, the sound of chewing. After five mouthfuls, eat as usual.
  6. Share first (when eating with others). Serve a good piece to someone at the table before serving yourself.

There are no "good" or "bad" foods here, no eating less and no skipping meals. Eating until satisfied is fine.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Where did this rice come from?" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Before the meal, the adult asks: "Where did this bowl of rice come from?"
  2. Trace it back together: rice pot → kitchen → market → field → farmer → rain and soil. The child can draw a quick row of pictures.
  3. The whole family says: "Thank you, rain; thank you, soil; thank you, everyone who grew our rice."

Do not make the child finish everything, and do not mention weight. For more slow-eating games, see Đi chậm, ăn chậm (Walk slowly, eat slowly; in Vietnamese).

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The exercise makes you preoccupied with how much you eat, makes you want to eat less or skip meals, or makes you feel guilty about eating. If you have ever had an eating disorder, ask your doctor before doing this lesson.
  • You have trouble swallowing, or choke when eating slowly. Eat in the way that is safe for you and ask your doctor.
  • Shared meals at home are times of shouting or violence. Your safety matters more than the exercise.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

Thanking the people who make our food and the things we use: Cảm ơn người lao động (Thanking working people; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Choose the meal you will use for tomorrow's exercise, and write down the words of thanks you will say.
