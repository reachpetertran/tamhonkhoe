Karma yoga and meditation in the Gita – Week 3: The seat and posture

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: a slow reading of the passage in chapter 6 of the Gita that describes how to sit in meditation, and setting up your seat, posture and gaze according to it.

Measurable goal: sit for 20 minutes in the same place on at least 5 of 7 days; after each sitting, rate how still your body, neck and head were (0–10).

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 6 opens with the idea of the last two weeks: one who works without seeking gain is both a renouncer and a yogi. Then Krishna describes concretely where and how the meditator sits.

Reading it in context

  • [Inference] In the usual numbering, verse 6:11 says the seat is spread with kusa grass, a deerskin and a cloth, one over the other; Arnold renders these as the meditator's "goods". Kusa grass is a grass regarded as pure in Vedic ritual.
  • [Inference] Arnold writes "The One". The Sanskrit in verse 6:12 is usually translated "one-pointed", that is, the mind gathered on a single point. The two renderings differ considerably; a reviewer within the tradition will comment.
  • Arnold's footnote on "nose-end" says "So in original." [Expert judgement] Many teachers take this to mean lowering the eyes and letting the gaze rest around the tip of the nose, not staring or crossing the eyes; commentators differ on this point.
  • "Brahmacharya" is a vow of chastity, usually understood as sexual continence, linked in the tradition with the student stage of life. The lesson only describes it and does not ask anyone to take this vow.
  • "Me" is Krishna. The tradition teaches the meditator to turn the mind towards the Divine; schools understand "Me" here differently. The course does not ask anyone to be devoted to anyone.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A review of 83 studies of adverse experiences in meditation: about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression; this can happen to people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020)
  • [Verified] A systematic review of 47 randomised trials with 3,515 people: meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, no more than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Inference] None of these studies compares sitting postures or ways of placing the gaze. The posture below rests on the text, not on science.

3. Practice for adults

About 25 minutes a day.

  1. Read slowly (3 minutes). Read the passage once. Underline every word about the seat and the body.
  2. Set up the place (day 1, 5 minutes). Following the text:
    • Secluded, still, clean, fixed: choose a corner where few people pass, clean it, and use the same corner all week.
    • Not too high, not too low: a cloth or mat on the floor, plus a cushion so your hips are a little higher than your knees. If your knees or back hurt, sit on a chair with both feet on the floor. You do not need a deerskin or kusa grass.
  3. Take the posture (2 minutes). Body, neck and head in one straight line, as if lightly suspended from the crown. Hands on the thighs. Relax shoulders and jaw. Then keep still.
  4. The gaze. Eyes half closed and lowered, letting the gaze rest around the tip of the nose. Do not stare and do not cross your eyes. If your eyes tire or your head aches, lower the gaze to the floor in front of you or close your eyes.
  5. Where to place the mind (20 minutes). The text says to gather the mind on one point and turn it towards "Me". Hindu practitioners may follow what their teacher and community teach. Others can use a neutral point: the feeling of the breath at the tip of the nose, just where the gaze rests. When the mind runs off, come back to that point. No chanting or repeating any word is needed.
  6. Rate (1 minute). How still were body, neck and head, 0–10.

For the first three days you may sit for 15 minutes, then go up to 20. Keeping still does not mean bearing pain: if a leg goes numb or hurts, change position slowly and carry on.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The mountain tree" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The child sits on a low stool or cushion, back straight "like a tree". The adult places a small beanbag or folded cloth on the child's head.
  2. Count slowly to 10 together. If the bag does not fall, the tree has stood tall.
  3. The second time, the child looks down at the tip of their nose, then up at the ceiling, then at the floor. Ask: "Where do you look when it is easiest to sit still?"
  4. Swap roles: the child puts the bag on the adult's head.

Use only a soft, light bag. Do not make the child sit for long; when they want to get up, the game is over.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Keeping the gaze at the nose-tip gives you headaches, dizziness or lasting eye strain. Lower your eyes to the floor or close them; if it continues, see a doctor.
  • Sitting still makes back or knee pain grow over several days. Move to a chair; if pain lasts, ask a doctor.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have ever had psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask your doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
