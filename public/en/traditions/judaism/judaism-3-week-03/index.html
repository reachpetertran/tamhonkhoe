Judaism 3 – Week 3: The friends

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Before you start: this Level 3 course deals with suffering and loss. If you are newly bereaved, seriously ill, or having thoughts of dying or harming yourself, please save the course for another time and see Get help now straight away.

This week: slow reading of the words of Eliphaz, the first friend to speak, and of Job's reply to his friends. Sit for 25 minutes each day. Each day, when someone tells you about something, listen for at least 3 minutes without advice or explanation.

Measurable goal: a full session of reading and sitting on at least 5 of 7 days; one 3-minute moment of listening without advice on at least 5 of 7 days; each evening, note one explanation you wanted to give but held back.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The three friends came to comfort Job and sat in silence for seven days. When Job laments, Eliphaz speaks first. He begins by recalling the good Job has done, then offers an explanation: the innocent do not perish; those who sow evil reap evil.

After many rounds of speeches, Job replies:

Reading in context

  • [Inference] Eliphaz's words sound reasonable and well-meant. The difficulty is that he uses a general rule ("you reap what you sow") to explain one particular person's suffering; the reader knows from chapter 1 that Job is not someone who "sows mischief".
  • [Traditional teaching] At the end of the book (42:7), God tells Eliphaz that the friends "have not spoken of Me the thing that is right", as Job has. Week 6 reads this verse.
  • [Inference] In 16:4–5, Job says he too could speak like his friends if they changed places, and names what he needs: words that strengthen and ease grief, not explanations.
  • "Sorry comforters" means miserable, poor comforters. "Upholden", "affrighted" and "assuage" are old English for "held up", "frightened" and "ease".
  • The course does not discuss the speeches of the fourth friend, Elihu (chapters 32–37); a reviewer may explain how the sages read them.
  • "Do not explain another person's suffering" does not mean staying silent when someone is in danger. If the person speaks of thoughts of self-harm, violence or abuse, help them find support at once: Get help now.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any study that evaluates this week's exercise of "listening without explaining".
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 49 studies (105 effect sizes) on how people draw on religious faith in difficult times: "positive" forms (such as seeking support from God or from a community) went with better adjustment, to a moderate degree (r = 0.33); "negative" forms (such as feeling punished by God) went with poorer adjustment, to a small degree (r = 0.22). These are correlations and do not show which causes which. (Ano & Vasconcelles 2005)
  • [Inference] The study used questionnaires; it does not judge theology and says nothing about the Book of Job. It only suggests that the explanation "you suffer because you did wrong" may not help a suffering person, even when it is well meant.
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 people): those with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation. (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010)

Limits: the evidence is indirect and correlational.

3. Practice for adults

About 35 minutes a day, plus one moment of listening during the day.

  1. Settle (1 minute).
  2. Read slowly (5 minutes). On odd days read Eliphaz, on even days read Job. Pause at the verse in which you recognise yourself (sometimes we are Eliphaz, sometimes Job).
  3. Sit for 25 minutes.
    • First 10 minutes: notice your breath.
    • Middle 10 minutes: recall a time someone explained your suffering to you. Notice your body as you remember. There is no need to judge that person.
    • Last 5 minutes: recall a time you explained someone else's suffering to them. Just notice.
  4. Come back to the ground (2 minutes).
  5. During the day: listen for 3 minutes. When someone tells you about something hard (even something small), listen for at least 3 minutes. No advice, no comparing with your own story, no "it's probably because…". Ask just one more question: "And then what?" or "How do you feel about it?".
  6. Evening note (2 minutes). One explanation you wanted to give but held back.

4. Kids' corner 6+

The game "Rabbit ears" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Adult and child put their hands on their heads as "rabbit ears".
  2. The adult tells a small unhappy thing from the day (for example: "Today Dad got stuck in traffic and was late"). The child "pricks up their rabbit ears" and listens to the end without interrupting.
  3. The child says back one thing they heard: "Dad got stuck in traffic." Then asks one question: "How did you feel?"
  4. Swap roles: the child tells, the adult is the rabbit.
  5. To finish: "Rabbit ears listen to the end before they speak."

Use only small everyday things.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself or of dying.
  • Remembering how someone explained your suffering brings up anger or hurt that rises high and does not ease after a few minutes. If so, open your eyes, come back to the ground, and for the rest of the week just sit with the breath.
  • You begin to think that your suffering is a punishment you deserve. Stop and talk with someone you trust.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for more than 20 minutes.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
