Judaism 1 – Week 6: Justice, kindness, humility

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: slow reading of three verses from the prophet Micah, choosing one of three things (justice, kindness, humility) to practise this week, and reviewing the six weeks of the course.

Measurable goal: do and note one action for the thing you chose, on at least 5 of 7 days; at the end of the week, rate the course's six practices and keep one or two.

1. Traditional teaching

Micah and the question "what shall I bring?"

[Traditional teaching] Micah is one of the twelve "minor" prophets of the Hebrew Bible, living in the kingdom of Judah around the 8th century BCE. In chapter 6, God is pictured as bringing a case against His people; in verses 6–7 someone asks what offering would be enough, and verse 8 answers.

A note on context: the question moves from an ordinary offering (calves a year old), to an excessive one (thousands of rams, rivers of oil), to the unthinkable (one's own child). The answer asks for no offering at all. "Mercy" here translates the Hebrew chesed, a word rich in meaning (faithfulness, steadfast kindness). The quotation keeps the JPS punctuation as published. [Traditional teaching] The Talmud (Makkot 23b–24a) says the prophets "gathered" the commandments into fewer principles, and names Micah as the one who gathered them into three: the three of this verse.

[Inference] Verse 8 does not do away with worship; in Jewish tradition ritual and ethics go together. The verse is a reminder that offerings cannot replace how one treats other people. The Jewish reviewer will say more about how the sages read it.

Looking back over six weeks

The six weeks of the course have touched all three: listening and love (Week 1), loving the neighbour and the stranger (Week 2), rest for workers too (Week 3), caring for yourself but not only for yourself (Week 4), doing your share without demanding to finish (Week 5).

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experiments (4,045 people) found that doing kind acts for others raises the doer's own well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests (more than 8,000 people) found that "if… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006)
  • [Inference] The research measures kind acts and a planning method; it does not measure "justice" or "humility" in Micah's sense, nor a life of faith.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes a day.

  1. Read slowly (3 minutes). Read the three verses twice. On Day 1 just read; from Day 2 you can read only verse 8.
  2. Day 1: choose one (3 minutes). Choose one of the three and write one concrete action for the whole week: - Justice: pay something you owe on time; share the housework fairly; hear both sides before deciding. - Kindness: one small kind act a day for someone who asks nothing of you. - Humility: admit one mistake; let someone else speak first once; do one good thing without telling anyone.
  3. Make an "if… then…" plan (1 minute). For example: "If I have just got home, then I will ask my partner whether there is something I can do for them today."
  4. Do it and write one line each evening (3 minutes): "Today I …; I noticed …".
  5. End of the week: course review (10 minutes). Rate from 0 to 3 how far you still keep each practice:
Week Practice Score (0–3)
1 Slow reading and one line
2 One kindness to someone near
3 One intentional rest period
4 Doing something put off, right now
5 Ten minutes on a long-term task
6 One act of justice, kindness or humility
  1. Keep one or two practices, put them in your calendar for the next four weeks, each with an "if… then…" sentence.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Three cups" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Set out three cups with three pictures: scales (fairness), a heart (kindness), a bending tree (humility).
  2. The adult tells small situations one by one, and the child drops a bean or marble into the cup that fits best: sharing a cake equally between brothers; helping a friend who fell; saying "sorry" after spilling water; letting a younger sibling choose first.
  3. There are no wrong answers. Ask: "Why did you choose that cup?"
  4. Together choose one action from the three cups for the whole family to do this week.

Keep beans and marbles out of reach of babies.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "Humility" turns into putting yourself down, or "admitting a mistake" into taking blame that is not yours. If so, switch to one of the other two.
  • "Justice" touches a major dispute (money, land, legal matters). The exercise does not replace someone with the right expertise.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
