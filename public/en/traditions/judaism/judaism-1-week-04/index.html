Judaism 1 – Week 4: If not now

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: slow reading of three short questions by Hillel in Pirkei Avot, and finishing one thing you have put off.

Measurable goal: write one line answering one of the three questions, on at least 5 of 7 days; finish one thing you have put off before the week ends.

1. Traditional teaching

What Pirkei Avot is

[Traditional teaching] Pirkei Avot, usually translated "Ethics of the Fathers" or "Sayings of the Fathers", is part of the Mishnah, the collection of oral law written down around the year 200. Unlike the rest of the Mishnah, it consists almost entirely of the sages' advice on how to live. Many Jewish communities have the custom of reading a chapter of Pirkei Avot on Shabbat afternoons during the summer weeks.

This lesson uses Joseph I. Gorfinkle's English translation. His numbering of sections sometimes differs from other editions; in chapter I it is the same.

Hillel's three questions

[Traditional teaching] Hillel is one of the sages mentioned most often in the Talmud, living around the turn of the Common Era. The tradition remembers him as gentle and patient. Section 12, just before, records his teaching to love peace, pursue peace and love all people.

A note on context: Gorfinkle's footnote sums the saying up as: be self-reliant, but not selfish. [Inference] The three questions form a chain: responsibility for oneself, not stopping at oneself, and not letting what is right drift into "later". Later sages read the saying in deeper ways too, for example about Torah study; the Jewish reviewer will say more. Gorfinkle's footnote number (33) and the full stop printed after it are left out of the quotation.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests (more than 8,000 people) found that "if… then…" plans (for example: "if I have finished breakfast, then I will call the doctor") helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65). (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006)
  • [Inference] The goals and measures in the studies vary widely, and an average promises nothing for any one person. The research does not measure Hillel's teaching; the lesson only borrows a planning method to make the "now" part easier.

3. Practice for adults

About 10–12 minutes a day.

  1. Read slowly (2 minutes). Read the three questions twice.
  2. Choose one question (1 minute). Each day choose one of the three, in turn.
  3. Write one line (3 minutes). For example: "Today, what I need to take care of for myself is …"; "Today, beyond myself, I can take care of …"; "Something I have put off that I can do today is …".
  4. Day 1: make a list (5 minutes). Write down three things you have put off for a long time. Circle the smallest, one you can do in 30 minutes.
  5. Day 1: make an "if… then…" plan (1 minute). "If [time, place], then I will [first step]." For example: "If I have finished lunch on Wednesday, then I will open the form and fill in the first page."
  6. Do it this week. When it is done, mark it and write one line: "Now it's done, I feel …".

If the thing you put off is too big, make its first step the "thing", not the whole task.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Two minutes, right now" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Together, find a small task the child often says "later" to: putting slippers away, putting pens in their box, hanging up a towel.
  2. Set a timer for two minutes. The child does the task straight away before the timer rings.
  3. Ask: "How did it feel to do it now? Was it as hard as you thought?"
  4. The adult names a small task of their own that they will also do right away today.

Do not rush the child or criticise them for being slow. The aim is for the child to see that "starting" is easier than it looks.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "If not now, when?" leaves you feeling more pressured, rushed or self-critical. If so, switch to the first or second question, and choose a smaller task.
  • The thing you have put off is a major decision (leaving a job, divorce, an investment, a health matter). The exercise does not push you to decide quickly; ask someone with the right expertise.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Write down one thing you have put off and an "if… then…" sentence for its first step.
