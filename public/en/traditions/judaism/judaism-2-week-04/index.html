Judaism 2 – Week 4: Honouring others

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: slow reading of Rabbi Eleazar son of Shammua's saying about the honour of a pupil, a companion and a teacher. The trait of the week: honouring others, in Hebrew kavod.

Measurable goal: each day, one specific act of respect for someone below or alongside you, on at least 5 of 7 days; keep the evening notebook on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

Three levels of honour

[Traditional teaching] Rabbi Eleazar son of Shammua lived around the year 150, according to Gorfinkle's footnote. His saying goes through three relationships: teacher to pupil, companion to companion, pupil to teacher.

A note on context: Gorfinkle numbers this section IV, 15; many other editions number it 4:12. "Heaven" is the sages' way of referring to God without saying His name directly. [Inference] Each level is raised one step: a teacher should not merely avoid shaming a pupil, but hold the pupil's honour as dear as their own; companions of equal standing are treated with the reverence usually kept for a teacher. The teaching begins with those below us, not those above. Gorfinkle's footnote number (25) is left out of the quotation.

"Who is honored?"

[Traditional teaching] In Week 1, Ben Zoma's fourth question (Pirkei Avot IV, 1) answered: "He who honors others." [Inference] The two teachings meet: honour is not something we demand from others, but something we give. The Jewish reviewer will say how the sages explain kavod.

2. What science finds

  • We have not verified any review that measures practising respect for others as such.
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experimental studies (4,045 people): doing kind things for others raised the doer's own happiness by a small-to-medium amount (δ = 0.28); the studies were often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] A thank-you by name, or giving someone credit, is a small kindness. The research measures how the doer feels, not how the person honoured feels, and it does not measure Rabbi Eleazar's teaching.

3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). Read Rabbi Eleazar's saying twice. The second time, stop at "as dear to thee as thine own".
  2. Reminder phrase (1 minute). "Other people's honour is as dear as mine."
  3. Reflection (8 minutes). Write a few lines: "Who works for me, learns from me or works alongside me, and rarely hears me thank them by name? What in them deserves respect?"
  4. During the day: one specific act of respect. Choose one of these, for someone below or alongside you:
    • Thank someone by name, saying exactly what they did ("Thanks, Lan, for checking the figures again").
    • Give credit to whoever had the idea, in front of others.
    • Let someone finish what they are saying, without interrupting or looking at your phone.
    • Ask the opinion of someone younger or less experienced, and use it if it makes sense.
    • Give feedback in private, without making anyone lose face in public.
  5. Evening: trait notebook (5 minutes). Mark ✓ or ✗, then write two lines: "Today I honoured … by …"; "The moment I nearly made someone lose face was …".

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Praise the real thing" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Sit around the table. Each person praises someone else in the family, but must praise exactly what that person did: "You lined up all the shoes neatly", "Mum made soup even though she was tired", not just "well done".
  2. The person praised says "Thank you" and looks at the one who praised them.
  3. The adult praises the child first, then the child praises an adult or a brother or sister.
  4. Ask the child: "How did it feel to be praised for exactly what you did?"

No comparisons like "you're better than your brother". Each piece of praise is about one person only.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You are somewhere where others regularly belittle or insult you. Honouring others does not mean accepting insults; look for someone who can help you there.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
