Judaism 1 – Week 5: Not to finish, but not to quit

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: slow reading of two short sayings of Rabbi Tarfon in Pirkei Avot, and ten minutes each day on one large, long-term task that you cannot finish in a week.

Measurable goal: spend 10 minutes on your chosen long-term task and tick it off on a chart, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

Rabbi Tarfon

[Traditional teaching] Rabbi Tarfon was a sage of the Mishnah period, living after the destruction of the Temple in Jerusalem in the year 70, a contemporary of Rabbi Akiva. The two sayings below come at the end of chapter II of Pirkei Avot.

Gorfinkle numbers them sections 20 and 21; many other editions number them 2:15 and 2:16.

A note on context: according to Gorfinkle's notes, "the day" is a human life, which is short, and "the Master of the house" is God. He sums up the second saying: no one can finish the work of the world, yet one must not give in to idleness and despair, but do one's share as well as one can. In the tradition, "the work" is first of all the study of Torah and keeping the commandments. [Inference] The saying lifts two burdens at once: "I must finish everything" and the excuse "I can't finish it, so why start". Gorfinkle's footnote numbers and italics are left out of the quotation.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally et al. 2010)
  • [Inference] The research shows that a steady practice takes a long time and survives a missed day; it does not measure Rabbi Tarfon's teaching, and says nothing about reward "in the time to come", which is a matter of faith.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes a day: 3–5 minutes reading and writing, 10 minutes of work.

  1. Day 1: choose the task (5 minutes). Choose one large thing you want to pursue over months: learning a skill, reading a difficult book, gradually clearing out the house, tending a garden, writing a family journal. Write one line: "I will not finish this in a week, but each day I will give it 10 minutes."
  2. Draw a chart of seven boxes for the seven days.
  3. Each day read slowly (2 minutes) Rabbi Tarfon's two sayings, then work for 10 minutes. When the 10 minutes are up, stop, even if you are in the flow.
  4. Tick the box and write one line: "Today I managed …".
  5. If you miss a day, do not make up double. The next day just do your 10 minutes as usual.
  6. End of the week (3 minutes): write one answer: "What is my share of this task, and what part is not mine?"

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "One storey a day" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Together, choose a big project with building blocks or Lego, or a large picture on paper, that cannot be finished in one day.
  2. Each day the child adds a small part in 5 minutes: a few blocks, one animal in the picture.
  3. Keep the project somewhere safe; do not tidy it away.
  4. At the end of the week ask: "What did it look like on the first day? What does it look like now? Does a little each day work?"

Do not push the child to do more once they are bored, and do not criticise the project for being unfinished. Keep small pieces out of reach of babies.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "Not free to desist" leaves you feeling trapped or blaming yourself for things you have given up. The exercise is about a task you chose; you are free to choose another, or to stop something that no longer suits you.
  • You are exhausted. Then the ten minutes can be ten minutes of rest; go back to Week 3: The day of rest.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Choose your long-term task, draw the seven-box chart, and do the first 10 minutes.
