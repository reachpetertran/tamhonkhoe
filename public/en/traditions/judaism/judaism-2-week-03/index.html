Judaism 2 – Week 3: Do not judge too quickly

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: slow reading of two teachings in Pirkei Avot about how we see other people. The trait of the week: not judging quickly, and judging people favourably.

Measurable goal: each day, write "two other explanations" once for something that annoyed or upset you, on at least 5 of 7 days; keep the evening notebook on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

"Until thou art come into his place"

[Traditional teaching] Hillel is the sage we met in Judaism 1, Week 4, remembered as gentle and patient. The passage below has five short pieces of advice; this week practises only the third.

A note on context: Gorfinkle numbers this section II, 5; many other editions number it 2:4. His footnote explains "trust not in thyself" as being constantly on guard against oneself, since even a good person can fall. [Inference] The second and third pieces of advice shed light on each other: if we cannot be sure of ourselves until the end of our lives, we can be even less sure of someone else before standing in their situation. And in truth no one ever fully stands in another's place.

The scale of merit

A note on context: Gorfinkle's footnote says the "companion" here is a fellow-student. [Inference] "Judging in the scale of merit" means that when an action can be read in several ways, we lean towards the reading that is good for the other person. The teaching comes together with having a teacher and a study companion: one learns to see others fairly within a community, not alone. The Jewish reviewer will say where the tradition sets the limits of this teaching. Gorfinkle's footnote numbers (10)–(13) and (19) are left out of the quotations.

Not judging people quickly does not mean ignoring wrongdoing

[Inference] Both teachings are about how we see a person when we do not understand enough. They do not tell us to call wrong right, to stay silent in the face of injustice, or to stay close to someone who is harming us. We can still say clearly "this is not acceptable", and still report it when needed, without jumping to conclusions about the whole person.

2. What science finds

  • We have not verified any review that measures practising "judging people favourably" as such.
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests (more than 8,000 people) found that "if… then…" plans helped people do what they intended more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65). (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006)
  • [Inference] The exercise uses one such plan ("if I am annoyed with someone, then I look for two other explanations"). The research measures reaching goals, not how we see other people, and it does not measure Hillel's teaching.

3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). Read both passages. The second time, stop at "until thou art come into his place".
  2. Reminder phrase (1 minute). Write the week's plan: "If I am annoyed with someone, then I look for two other explanations."
  3. Reflection (8 minutes). Remember a time when you yourself were misunderstood. Write a few lines: "What did I wish they had known about my situation?"
  4. During the day: two other explanations. When you notice you have just reached a verdict about someone ("he's lazy", "she looks down on me"), write three short lines:
    • What happened (only what you saw and heard).
    • What I just thought about that person.
    • Two other explanations that could also be true.
  5. Evening: trait notebook (5 minutes). Mark ✓ or ✗, then write two lines: "Today I jumped to a conclusion about …"; "Thinking further, I see …".

You do not need to believe the kinder explanation straight away. It is enough to see that you do not know enough yet.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "Swapping shoes": the adult and the child each draw a shoe and swap drawings, "walking in the other person's shoes", and think about how that person feels in a small everyday situation. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 3.

Afterwards you can ask: "Did anyone annoy you today? What might have been going on for them?" If the child tells you about being bullied or hurt, do not tell them to "think kindly of the other child": listen, and tell the teacher.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You are in a relationship with violence, threats or control. The exercise is not for finding excuses for someone who is harming you. Look for someone who can help.
  • The exercise turns into blaming yourself: "they must have done that because of me". If so, pause the writing for a few days and keep only the slow reading.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

Quick judgements often come when emotions are running high. daynentang has a lesson on pausing for a few breaths before reacting: Anchoring when emotions run high (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Write the week's "if… then…" plan on a slip of paper and put it where you will often see it.
