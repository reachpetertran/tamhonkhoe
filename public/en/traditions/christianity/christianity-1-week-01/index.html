Christianity 1 – Week 1: The shepherd

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: get to know the way of slow reading that the Christian tradition calls lectio divina ("divine reading"), with one short poem: Psalm 23.

Measurable goal: about 10 minutes of slow reading in four steps, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

What lectio divina is

[Traditional teaching] The Christian tradition teaches that Scripture is not only for study, but also for slow reading, listened to as a word spoken to oneself. This way of reading is called lectio divina. It began in the life of monasteries and today is used by many Catholics, Orthodox, Anglicans and Protestants, each in their own way.

[Expert judgement] The four familiar steps of lectio divina are usually traced to a letter by the twelfth-century Carthusian monk Guigo II, known as the Scala Claustralium ("The Ladder of Monks"). The four steps are:

Step Latin name What you do
1. Reading lectio Read slowly, read again, notice a word or phrase that touches you
2. Meditation meditatio Repeat that word and ask: what does this passage say to me today?
3. Response oratio For believers: pray in your own words
4. Stillness contemplatio Sit quietly, with nothing more to think about

[Verified] In 2010, in the apostolic exhortation Verbum Domini (section 87), Pope Benedict XVI summed up these steps as questions, and added that lectio divina is complete only when it leads to action (actio): living what one has read for others. (vatican.va) This course uses the four steps, and from Week 4 adds one small action each week.

Psalm 23

[Traditional teaching] The Psalms are a book of poems and prayers shared by Jews and Christians. Psalm 23 likens God to a shepherd who leads the flock to green grass and still water, and stays with the sheep even in the dark valley.

Reading it in context

  • "The LORD" in capitals stands for the personal name of God in the Hebrew Bible. Catholic Bibles that follow the Greek and Latin numbering call this Psalm 22. This is a difference of translation and numbering, not of content.
  • The rod and the staff are a shepherd's tools: to guide and to drive off wild animals. [Inference] The image here is protection, not punishment.
  • [Expert judgement] "The valley of the shadow of death" is often read at funerals. If you are grieving, this line may touch deeply; you can read more slowly, or stay with verses 1–3 this week.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any review that evaluates lectio divina as such.
  • [Verified] The closest evidence is a meta-analysis of 97 studies (7,181 people) on psychotherapy that integrates the client's religious faith. In the most rigorous studies (the same therapy with and without the religious part), the added part reduced distress about as much as standard therapy, but increased spiritual well-being. (Captari et al. 2018)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 23 randomised trials of religious or spiritual interventions in mental health care found that they reduced anxiety; for depression the results were weaker. The programmes varied widely and study quality was limited. (Gonçalves et al. 2015)
  • [Inference] These studies were guided therapy, usually for people of faith. Reading slowly on your own for 10 minutes a day has not been tested; any benefit is only inferred.

Limits: slow reading is spiritual practice, not treatment.

3. Practice for adults

About 12–15 minutes a day. Each day, read the same poem again; rereading is the way of lectio divina.

  1. Settle (1 minute). Sit still and take a few breaths. Believers may ask for the grace to listen.
  2. Read (3 minutes). Read the whole poem slowly once. Read it a second time very quietly aloud. Notice a word or phrase that makes you pause.
  3. Meditate (4 minutes). Repeat that word several times. Ask: today, what does this line bring up for me?
  4. Respond (2 minutes). If you are a Christian: speak to God in your own words about what you have noticed. If not: write one sentence in response to the poem. [Expert judgement] The second option is tamhonkhoe's adaptation for people outside the faith, not a form of the tradition itself.
  5. Be still (2–3 minutes). Sit quietly, with nothing more to think or do.
  6. Tick it off. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days. On the first two days you can do steps 1–3 only.

4. Kids' corner 6+

The game "Bringing the sheep home" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Use a soft toy or a toy block as the "sheep", and a box as the "sheepfold".
  2. With wool or a few slippers, lay out a winding path on the floor, with a "meadow" (a green towel) and a "stream" (a blue towel) along the way.
  3. Your child is the shepherd and leads the sheep slowly along the path, stopping at the meadow for the sheep to "eat" and at the stream for it to "drink", then brings it into the fold.
  4. Ask: "What does the sheep need to feel safe?" The adult says: "A good shepherd goes slowly, lets the sheep rest, and never leaves it behind."

Christian families can read verses 1–2 of Psalm 23 to their child. Other families can just play the shepherd game.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Reading Scripture makes you feel guilty or afraid of punishment, or you keep rereading for fear of "not doing it right". Then stop and talk with a trusted friend, your pastor or priest, or a mental health professional.
  • The line about "the valley of the shadow of death" stirs up grief that feels too much to bear.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
