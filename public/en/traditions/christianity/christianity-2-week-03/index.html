Christianity 2 – Week 3: Go into your room

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: choose a fixed place and time, sit in silence there for 15 minutes each day, and count how many times you come back without blaming yourself.

Measurable goal: sit in silence for 15 minutes in the same place on at least 5 of 7 days; at the end of each session, note how many times you came back (a rough guess is fine).

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In the Sermon on the Mount, just before the Lord's Prayer (read in Christianity 1, Week 3), Jesus tells his disciples to pray in a hidden place, not to be seen by others, and without many words. The Christian tradition teaches that God sees what is hidden, and knows what the one who prays needs before they ask.

More than a thousand years later, Brother Lawrence wrote to someone whose thoughts often wandered in prayer:

Reading it in context

  • Jesus was a Jew speaking to Jews. The synagogue was the place of shared Jewish prayer. [Expert judgement] The criticism in verse 5 is aimed at praying in order to show off, not at shared prayer or at Judaism; Christians themselves pray together in church.
  • "Gentiles" (verse 7) are people outside the Jewish people. The verse is about the idea that "many words get you heard"; it is not a ranking of religions, and tamhonkhoe does not use it to compare traditions.
  • The "inner room" is an image. Without a room of your own, a corner, a familiar chair or an early-morning moment will do.
  • The image of a "dumb or paralytic beggar" is seventeenth-century language. His point: coming before God needs no fine words, only being there.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any review of silent prayer or "contemplative prayer" (for example centering prayer).
  • [Verified] The nearest evidence is on meditation. A systematic review of 47 randomised trials (3,515 people): meditation programmes reduced anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, but were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] Long silent sitting is not always pleasant. A review of 83 studies found about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression, including people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] Silent prayer differs from meditation in being turned towards Someone, so these figures are only indirect. That is why the "Stop practising if" section matters more as the sittings grow longer.

3. Practice for adults

About 22 minutes a day.

  1. Choose a place (day 1). A quiet spot, at the same time each day. Tell your household so that no one calls you for 20 minutes.
  2. Read slowly (4 minutes). Read both passages. The second time, read only verses 6 and 8, very slowly.
  3. Sit in silence (15 minutes). Set a timer.
    • Christians: pray silently with few words, as Brother Lawrence advises. You might say a single sentence, then stay in silence before God.
    • People who are not Christian: sit in silence, attending to the breath or a short word of your choice (for example "here"). This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation.
    • For both: when thoughts wander, notice, and bring the mind back "in tranquility", without annoyance.
  4. Count (1 minute). At the end, estimate how many times you came back. A higher number does not mean a worse sitting: each return is one repetition of the practice.
  5. Note (1 minute). The number of returns, and one word for today's sitting.

If 15 minutes makes you restless, sit for 10 minutes for the first three days and build up. You can keep your eyes open, looking at the floor.

4. Kids’ corner 6+

This week uses the activity "The breathing statue": in the middle of moving about, your child freezes like a statue and notices their body and breathing. See the full activity at Kids’ corner – Activity 1.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Sitting in silence keeps bringing up painful memories that do not settle, even after opening your eyes and looking round the room.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting in silence for longer than 20 minutes.

7. Practice for today

Find the place where you will sit tomorrow, and put a chair or a cushion there ready.
