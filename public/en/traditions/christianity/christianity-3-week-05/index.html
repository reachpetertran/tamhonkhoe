Christianity 3 – Week 5: Silence before mystery

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

A reminder: this is a Level 3 course, and this is the longest week of practice. If you are depressed, newly bereaved, going through a major upheaval or having thoughts of harming yourself, please pause the course and see Get help now. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe PTSD or long-lasting feelings of being detached from themselves should not study this course alone. If earlier weeks left you feeling unsteady, stay at 30 minutes; do not increase.

This week: read one passage from chapter 3 of The Mystical Theology and two from chapter 46 of The Cloud of Unknowing slowly. Each day, one 40-minute session (40 minutes of silent sitting, or 30 minutes of sitting and 10 of slow walking). Once in the week, read these three passages with someone you trust, then each say one sentence to the other.

Measurable goal: a 40-minute session on at least 5 of 7 days; one reading with another person during the week.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The author of The Mystical Theology says that when speaking of God from below upwards, words become fewer and fewer, and at the summit there is only silence. The Cloud of Unknowing adds practical advice: that silence is not reached by straining, but by gentleness, humility and patient waiting.

Reading it in context

  • The chapter 3 passage is about how one speaks of God: as the text rises, it uses fewer words. "Dumbness" here is silence before something greater than words, not ceasing to talk with other people.
  • [Inference] The Cloud's words "list" and "listily" refer to an easy, unforced desire, not tension. The text between the two passages says that straining "hurts" the soul and leads to delusions; the lesson only summarises that. Just after the passage, the author says playfully that one should behave as if one did not want God to know how much one longs to see Him, and compares this to the happy play between a father and a child.
  • "Beastly" is a medieval way of describing the roughness of the body. The line does not despise the body; the next passage speaks of a "soft and a demure behaviour as well in body as in soul".
  • The original course outline had a week for "Longer practice" and a week for "Rereading with a reviewer". Since there is not yet a reviewer from within the tradition, this week combines the two: a longer session and one reading with someone you trust.
  • The 1922 scan's text has "Hst" by an OCR error; the 1912 edition and the context show it is "list".

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] The nearest evidence is on meditation. A systematic review of 47 randomised trials (3,515 people): meditation programmes reduced anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, but were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] An interview study of Western meditators recorded 59 kinds of experience in 7 domains, including difficult ones; their impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting, with 26 categories of influencing factors. (Lindahl et al. 2017)
  • [Inference] The 14th-century advice "do not strain" and the records of difficult experiences in long practice point to the same thing: longer practice does not mean better practice.

3. Practice for adults

About 45 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages. The second time, read only "snatch not over-hastily, as it were a greedy greyhound."
  2. A 40-minute session. Choose one of two ways:
    • Sit in silence for 40 minutes as in earlier weeks, using your short word from Week 3 if you wish; or
    • Sit in silence for 30 minutes, then walk slowly around the room for 10 minutes, noticing each foot touching the floor.
    • Christians may begin with a short prayer, then be silent. Those who do not pray: simply sit and walk. This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation.
    • If you notice you are trying to "reach" some state, remember "snatch not", relax your shoulders and jaw, and carry on sitting.
  3. Back to the ground (2 minutes). Open your eyes, put your feet on the floor, name three things in the room.
  4. Reading with someone (once in the week, 15 minutes). Choose someone you trust. Each of you reads one passage aloud, then each says one sentence: "The line I remember most is… because…". Do not argue about right and wrong. If you are a Christian, this person could be a priest, a pastor or a guide in your community.
  5. Tick it off. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days for the session, and one shared reading.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "Listening to the bell fade": the family listens to a bell until it can no longer be heard, and notices the silence afterwards. See the full activity in Kids' corner – Activity 11.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have traumatic flashbacks, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself or of dying.
  • A feeling that you or the world are "not real", or of being detached from your body, stays after you stand up. If it lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • You skip meals or sleep, or spend less time with your family, in order to sit longer, or you find it hard to talk to people after sitting. Then go back to 20–30 minutes and talk to someone you trust.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for more than 20 minutes.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

If you feel unsteady while sitting, daynentang has a lesson that brings your attention back to your body and surroundings: Neo lại (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

In a conversation today, when the other person stops speaking, wait two more breaths before you reply.
