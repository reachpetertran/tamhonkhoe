Christianity 1 – Week 3: The Lord’s Prayer (described)

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read the Lord's Prayer slowly as a text, pausing each day on one line, and write one line.

Measurable goal: read and write one line on at least 5 of 7 days. Praying the passage is optional, not required.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus tells his disciples not to pray at length to be seen by others, and then gives them a short prayer. The Christian tradition teaches that this is the model prayer: calling God Father, asking that his will be done, asking for enough bread for today, asking forgiveness as we forgive, and asking to be kept from evil. Almost all Christian churches say this prayer.

Reading it in context

  • The World English Bible says "debts" and "debtors", as the Greek does; many church versions say "trespasses" or "sins". The meaning is close.
  • [Expert judgement] The last line ("For yours is the Kingdom…") is missing from many of the oldest manuscripts of Matthew. Protestant churches usually say it with the prayer; in the Catholic Mass a similar line is said separately, after another short prayer. Luke 11:2–4 has a shorter version.
  • Verses 14–15, right after, stress forgiveness again: God forgives those who forgive others.
  • [Expert judgement] This lesson describes the prayer and reads it slowly; it does not invite anyone to pray in a faith they do not hold. People outside Christianity can read it as an important text of a major tradition.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any study that evaluates slow reading of the Lord's Prayer as such.
  • [Verified] Religious or spiritual interventions in general (23 randomised trials) reduced anxiety, with weaker results for depression; the programmes varied widely and study quality was limited. (Gonçalves et al. 2015)
  • [Verified] On "as we also forgive": a meta-analysis of 53 studies (2,323 people) found that guided forgiveness programmes clearly increased forgiveness compared with no treatment, and reduced depression and anxiety. These were guided programmes over several sessions, not the reading of one line of Scripture. (Wade et al. 2014)

3. Practice for adults

About 12–15 minutes a day.

  1. Settle (1 minute).
  2. Read (3 minutes). Read the whole passage once. The second time, read slowly the line for the day: day 1 "Our Father… kept holy"; day 2 "Let your Kingdom come…"; day 3 "daily bread"; day 4 "Forgive us…"; day 5 "Bring us not…"; days 6–7 the whole passage.
  3. Meditate (4 minutes). What does today's line ask for? With "daily bread": today, what is "enough" for me?
  4. Respond (2 minutes, optional). Christians may pray the whole prayer slowly. Others: write one sentence in response to today's line.
  5. Be still (2 minutes).
  6. Write one line (1 minute).

On day 4 ("Forgive us…") think only of a small hurt that is over. Do not use this line for harm from violence or abuse, or when the other person is still harming you. In this tradition, forgiveness does not mean going back to someone who is causing harm.

4. Kids' corner 6+

"Daily bread" is a chance to thank the people who make our meals. This week uses the activity "Who helped me today?". See the full activity at Kids’ corner – Activity 2.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "Forgive us… as we forgive" makes you feel pushed to forgive someone who hurt you badly, or makes you blame yourself again and again.
  • The image of "Father" brings up painful memories of your own father. You can stop this lesson; that is not your fault.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

Before dinner, pause for 10 seconds and think: this meal is enough for today.
