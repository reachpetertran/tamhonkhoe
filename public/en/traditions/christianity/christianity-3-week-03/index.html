Christianity 3 – Week 3: One short word

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

A reminder: this is a Level 3 course. If you are depressed, newly bereaved, going through a major upheaval or having thoughts of harming yourself, please pause the course and see Get help now. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe PTSD or long-lasting feelings of being detached from themselves should not study this course alone.

This week: read the end of chapter 7 of The Cloud of Unknowing slowly. Choose one very short word and keep it all week. Sit in silence for 30 minutes each day; whenever you notice you have been pulled away, gently return to that word. After each sitting, note a rough count of how many times you came back.

Measurable goal: sit in silence for 30 minutes with one word on at least 5 of 7 days; note the number of returns after each sitting.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 7 answers the question: when thoughts come during prayer, even devout ones, what should one do? The author advises wrapping one's whole intent towards God in a very short word of one syllable, and using that word as something to hold on to.

Reading it in context

  • Just before this passage, the author says that anyone who hopes to reach contemplation without many meditations on God's goodness and on the Passion of Christ coming first "shall err and fail of his purpose". The short word is a step for someone who has prayed for a long time, not a shortcut.
  • "Thou shall" is as printed in Underhill's 1912 and 1922 editions.
  • "Smite down", "beat on", "shield and spear" are the strong images of 14th-century writing. Chapter 46 of the same book advises doing this work "listily" (gently, with ease), without straining (Week 5). So this week's practice uses a gentle return, not chasing thoughts away.
  • [Expert judgement] This book's way of praying with one short word is one source of today's Centering Prayer.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of controlled studies: trying to suppress a thought makes it come back more often, by a small-to-moderate amount; the effect varied with the kind of thought and how it was measured. (Abramowitz et al. 2001)
  • [Inference] So this week's practice does not ask you to "beat down" thoughts. The short word is only a place to return to; thoughts come and go on their own.
  • [Verified] In a 28-day trial of Centering Prayer with 702 Christians, well-being one week after the practice period did not differ between the group with a religious framing, the group without it, and a group that simply reflected on the day; all three improved. (Rohde et al. 2024)

Limits: no study has measured this course.

3. Practice for adults

About 35 minutes a day.

  1. Choose a word (first day only). Christians: a short word turned towards God, such as "God" or "Love". Those who do not pray: a neutral word, such as "still" or "here". This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation. Keep the same word all week; do not change it halfway.
  2. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the two passages. The second time, read only "ever the shorter it is the better it accordeth with the work of the Spirit."
  3. Silent sitting (30 minutes).
    • Say the word inwardly once, very softly, then be silent.
    • When you notice you are thinking of something else, say the word once again, as lightly as setting down a feather. Do not repeat it continuously like a chant, and do not speed up.
    • There is no need to count while sitting.
  4. Back to the ground (2 minutes). Open your eyes, put your feet on the floor, name three things in the room.
  5. Note (1 minute). A rough count of returns: "a few", "about ten", "very many". Many returns does not mean a poor sitting; each return is the practice.

4. Kids' corner 6+

"Just one word" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Adult and child sit facing each other and pass a small ball or a ball of wool.
  2. Whoever holds the ball describes something (dinner, the cat, yesterday's rain) in exactly one word, then passes the ball on.
  3. Play a few rounds. Ask: "Can one word say everything about dinner? What is missing?"
  4. Finish with: "Some things are bigger than all the words we have. One short word just helps us remember them."

Do not criticise the word the child chooses.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have traumatic flashbacks, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself or of dying.
  • A feeling that you or the world are "not real", or of being detached from your body, stays after you stand up. If it lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • Saying the word becomes compulsive: you feel you must say it enough times, feel guilty or afraid if you forget, or the word keeps repeating in your head all day. Then stop this practice and talk to someone you trust.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for more than 20 minutes.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

If you feel unsteady while sitting, daynentang has a lesson that brings your attention back to your body and surroundings: Neo lại (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Once today, when you are in a hurry, stop just long enough to say your word inwardly once, then carry on.
