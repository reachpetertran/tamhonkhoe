Islam 4 – Week 2: Ramadan, the month of the Qur'an

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: learn about Ramadan as Muslims live it: not only a fast but a month of reading the Qur'an, prayer and giving. The three verses on fasting (2:183–185) were read closely in Body 4, Week 1; this week reads chapter 97, on the night the Qur'an was revealed.

Measurable goal: read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; keep a 10-minute slow reading in the evening, with no entertainment screens, on at least 5 of 7 days; write in your own words four things the lesson describes about Ramadan. The light restraint exercise is optional and does not count towards the goal.

1. Traditional teaching

The Night of Power

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 97 is called Al-Qadr. The tradition teaches that the Qur'an began to be revealed to the Prophet Muhammad on a night in the month of Ramadan, called Laylat al-Qadr. Verse 2:185 also says that Ramadan is the month in which the Qur'an was revealed.

The month of Ramadan (described)

[Traditional teaching] Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. The calendar is lunar: the month begins and ends with the sighting of the new crescent moon, so each year Ramadan comes about 10–12 days earlier in the Gregorian calendar and passes through every season.

  • The fast (sawm). Healthy adults neither eat nor drink from dawn until sunset. The sick, travellers and some others are exempt or make up the days (Week 3).
  • Two meals. Suhur is the meal before dawn; iftar is the meal that breaks the fast at sunset, often shared with family and friends, sometimes with the whole community at the mosque.
  • Reading the Qur'an and prayer. Many people read the whole Qur'an during the month; many Sunni communities add evening prayers called tarawih.
  • The Night of Power. It is sought in the last ten nights of the month; many Sunni Muslims look especially to the 27th night, many Shi'a Muslims to the 19th, 21st and 23rd.
  • The end. Ramadan ends with the festival of Eid al-Fitr ("the festival of breaking the fast"). Before it, each household gives zakat al-fitr, a share of food or money for the poor, so that everyone can celebrate.

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955. "We" is Allah speaking.
  • Qadr can mean "decree, destiny" or "worth, power"; Pickthall uses both "Predestination" and "Power", and the Vietnamese translation uses "Destiny".
  • [Inference] The scripture places the centre of the month in the revelation of the Qur'an. For Muslims the fast is part of that month, not the whole of it.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A review of 22 papers on Ramadan and mental health: the effects are generally small and mixed; people with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder have a higher risk of relapse. (Heun 2018)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 11 studies, 1,436 people, on fasting interventions in general: in randomised trials at low risk of bias, fasting groups had lower anxiety and depression scores than controls. The authors call the results preliminary; no study included people with psychiatric conditions. (Berthelot et al. 2021)
  • [Verified] Self-control training programmes have a small effect, smaller still after correcting for publication bias (33 studies). (Friese et al. 2017)
  • [Inference] No study has measured this week's light restraint exercise. Do not expect it to improve your mental health; its only purpose is to notice a habit.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). Read 97:1–5.
  2. Write four lines (days 1–2, 5 minutes). In your own words: what calendar Ramadan follows; from when to when Muslims fast; the names of the two meals of the day; the festival that ends the month.
  3. Evening: slow reading (10 minutes). Turn off entertainment screens. Read one page of the book you are reading, or of your own tradition's scripture, slowly. Note one sentence you want to remember.
  4. If you have friends or colleagues keeping Ramadan (optional): ask them one thing they love most about the month. Listen; do not argue about faith.

Light restraint exercise (optional, healthy adults only)

Do not do this exercise if you: are under 18; are pregnant or breastfeeding; have or have had an eating disorder, or are often preoccupied with weight or body shape; have diabetes treated with medication, or a chronic illness under treatment; are unwell. If unsure, ask a doctor first.

  1. Choose one snack or sweet drink you often have without really needing it (an afternoon bubble tea, sweets between tasks).
  2. Leave it out on 3 days of the week. Still eat your three main meals and drink enough water. Do not skip meals, do not go without drinks, do not swap in something smaller instead.
  3. When you want it, pause for one minute: breathe, notice whether you are hungry, tired or bored. Note one word in your notebook.
  4. From the fourth day, eat and drink as usual.

This is not the Ramadan fast and does not replace it. It is not for weight loss; no calorie counting.

4. Kids' corner 6+: A calendar of seven good deeds

About 5–10 minutes, with an adult. Children do not fast or give up any food. They eat and drink as normal.

  1. Draw a strip of seven boxes with the child, with a new crescent moon in the first box.
  2. Explain: "Some of our Muslim friends have a special month that begins when they see the new moon. In that month they read their holy book more and share with poor people."
  3. Each evening the child tells you one small good deed they did today (pouring water for grandma, lending a friend a pen) and draws it in that day's box.
  4. On the seventh day, look at the whole strip together. Ask: "Which one made you happiest?"

Do not praise or criticise anyone's faith. Do not talk about fasting as something children should try.

5. Stop practising if

  • The restraint exercise makes you want to cut out more foods, skip meals, think more about food or weight, or feel guilty when you eat. Stop the restraint part at once and talk to a doctor.
  • You feel dizzy, faint or shaky. Eat and drink normally; if it does not pass, see a doctor.
  • You keep Ramadan as part of your faith and have a chronic illness, diabetes treated with medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder: ask a doctor before Ramadan; this course does not replace medical advice or the teaching of your religious leaders.
  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

Eating slowly, noticing hunger from boredom: Đi chậm, ăn chậm (Walking slowly, eating slowly; in Vietnamese). Trouble sleeping: Giấc ngủ khi lòng không yên (Sleep when the mind is unsettled; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Choose a time for your 10-minute evening reading, and put the book where you will see it.
