Islam 2 – Week 4: Dhikr in the hadith

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: learn about dhikr through the hadith, the sayings and deeds of the Prophet Muhammad as reported by later generations, and through a verse of the Qur'an saying that God is very near. Sitting still rises to 15 minutes.

Measurable goal: sit still for 15 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; keep the three pauses on at least 5 of 7 days; retell the main point of the two hadith in your own words.

1. Traditional teaching

"Surely I am nigh"

[Traditional teaching] Verse 186 of chapter 2 (Al-Baqarah) sits in the middle of the passage about the fasting month of Ramadan. The tradition teaches that Allah is near and hears prayer, with no need for an intermediary.

The living and the dead (hadith, summarised)

[Traditional teaching] As narrated by Abu Musa, the Prophet Muhammad said that the one who remembers and praises his Lord, compared with the one who does not, is like the living compared with the dead. The hadith is in the chapter "The superiority of dhikr of Allah" in Sahih al-Bukhari.

Read the full text at sunnah.com, Sahih al-Bukhari 6407.

"I am with him when he remembers Me" (hadith, summarised)

[Traditional teaching] As narrated by Abu Hurairah, the Prophet reported the words of Allah (a kind of hadith called hadith qudsi): Allah is to His servant as the servant thinks of Him, and He is with the servant when the servant remembers Him. Whoever remembers Him within themselves, He remembers them within Himself; whoever remembers Him among a group of people, He remembers them among a better group. Whoever comes a little closer to Him, He comes much closer; whoever comes to Him walking, He comes running.

This is tamhonkhoe's summary, not a translation, because the English on sunnah.com is in copyright. Read the full text at sunnah.com, Sahih al-Bukhari 7405.

Dhikr in daily life (described)

[Traditional teaching] Many Muslims say short phrases of dhikr after each prayer, in the morning and evening, before sleep and when beginning a task. Some use prayer beads (misbaha or subha) to count. The Sufi tradition also has gatherings for shared dhikr; the course Islam 3 "Rumi and the Sufi tradition" covers it. This course does not teach reciting or counting.

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955.
  • Verse 2:186 places prayer beside duty: believers must also "hear My call" and trust. [Inference] Nearness here goes with responsibility; it is not a promise that every request will be granted.
  • [Expert judgement] Muslim theologians understand phrases such as "He comes running" in different ways. A common point is that Allah's mercy comes faster and in greater measure than human effort. The course does not choose between readings.
  • [Inference] In the second hadith, remembrance can be private, within oneself, or shared with others. This leads on to Week 6 on Friday and community.

2. What science finds

  • We have not been able to verify any review that evaluates dhikr itself.
  • [Verified] The closest is research on meditation: 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people, found that mindfulness meditation programmes reduced anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, and were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] Long sitting is not always comfortable: a review of 83 studies found that about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience, most often anxiety or depression, including people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] Mindfulness meditation and dhikr differ in purpose: dhikr is directed to Allah, while the mindfulness in this research is not. These figures only show that sitting still may help modestly, and may also cause discomfort.

3. Practice for adults

About 25–27 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). On odd days read 2:186; on even days read one of the two hadith summaries.
  2. Sitting still (15 minutes). Up from 10 to 15 minutes. If 15 minutes is too hard, sit for 12 minutes all week.
    • Muslims: keep dhikr as your community teaches.
    • Those who are not Muslim: sit still with the out-breath. In the last minute, open your eyes, look around the room and feel both feet on the floor before you stand up. This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation, not dhikr.
  3. Three pauses (1–2 minutes each). As last week.
  4. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). "When today did I feel fully 'alive' to what I was doing?"
  5. Once in the week (5 minutes). Retell the main point of the two hadith to someone, or write it down.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "The breathing statue": the child stands as still as a statue, letting only their tummy rise and fall, then the "statue" slowly comes back to life. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 1.

The adult can say: "Sometimes all we need to do is stand still and remember that we are here."

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Sitting for 15 minutes keeps bringing up painful memories or thoughts. Then go back to 5–10 minutes, open your eyes, or walk slowly instead of sitting.
  • You start to feel you must recite or count a great deal to have done "enough", or you feel afraid when you miss once. This is not what the hadith teach. Muslims should talk to a teacher in their community; anyone can talk to a mental health professional.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for longer than 20 minutes.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

When sitting still brings up strong feelings, use Neo lại khi cảm xúc dâng cao (Anchoring when emotions run high; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Set a 15-minute timer for tomorrow's sitting, and choose your spot in advance.
