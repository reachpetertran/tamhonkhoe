Islam 2 – Week 2: Remember Me

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read two passages of the Qur'an that call for the remembrance of Allah, and learn about the short phrases of dhikr that Muslims often say. The daily rhythm gains one more pause, and each pause comes with one thing to be grateful for.

Measurable goal: sit still for 10 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; pause twice a day (morning and midday) on at least 5 of 7 days, each time thinking of one thing you are grateful for; write the meaning of four common phrases of dhikr in your own words.

1. Traditional teaching

Remembering and being remembered

[Traditional teaching] Verse 152 of chapter 2 (Al-Baqarah) places remembrance beside gratitude. The tradition teaches that when people remember Allah, Allah remembers them too.

Much, early and late

[Traditional teaching] Two verses of chapter 33 (Al-Ahzab) call believers to remember Allah "much", early and late in the day.

Common phrases of dhikr (described)

[Traditional teaching] The tradition has many short phrases used for remembrance. The four below are very common; the table is for understanding their meaning, not instruction in reciting them.

Name Phrase (transliterated) Meaning (summary)
Tasbih Subhan Allah Glory be to Allah; Allah is pure, free of any imperfection
Tahmid Alhamdulillah All praise belongs to Allah; also an everyday way of saying "thank God"
Takbir Allahu akbar Allah is greater than all
Tahlil La ilaha illa Allah There is no god but Allah

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955.
  • In verse 33:42, the Vietnamese translator on QuranEnc adds in brackets that "morning" and "afternoon" are the Fajr and 'Asr prayers; Pickthall simply has "early and late". [Expert judgement] This is one translator's reading; commentators read it in several ways.
  • Verse 2:152 comes after the passage about the change in the direction of prayer (qiblah). The course does not go into that passage.
  • [Inference] In 2:152, remembrance and gratitude go together. That is why this week's exercise pairs each pause with one thing to be grateful for.
  • The table of phrases is tamhonkhoe's summary. In many places Muslims use these phrases in everyday speech, not only at prayer.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 studies, 3,675 people: gratitude exercises reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety by a small amount; the reduction was smaller against control groups doing another activity. (Cregg & Cheavens 2021)
  • [Inference] The gratitude exercises in this research are psychological exercises, not set within faith. They do not measure dhikr; they only suggest that regularly stopping to notice something good helps a little.

Limits: the effect is small, and most of the studies rely on self-report.

3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). On odd days read 2:152; on even days read 33:41–42.
  2. Sitting still (10 minutes). As last week.
    • Muslims: keep dhikr as your community teaches.
    • Those who are not Muslim: sit still with the out-breath. This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation, not dhikr.
  3. Two pauses (1–2 minutes each). Keep last week's midday pause and add one in the morning, tied to a point you already have (after brushing your teeth, before leaving home). Each time: stop what your hands are doing, take three slow breaths, and think of one specific thing you are grateful for at that moment.
  4. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). Note the two things from your two pauses.
  5. Once in the week (10 minutes). Write the meaning of the four phrases in the table in your own words, then cover the table and compare.

The pauses do not follow prayer times, involve no bowing and use no Arabic words. If you are Muslim, a pause does not replace Salah.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "Who helped me today?": in the evening the child names one person who helped them that day, and the family says thank you or draws a card. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 2.

Muslim families can teach their child to give thanks in their own family's way. Other families simply say "thank you".

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The gratitude exercise makes you feel guilty ("I'm not grateful enough") or pushes you to feel happy when you are sad. You do not need to feel happy; just notice one small thing, or skip that day.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

The pauses still build on Neo lại (Anchoring; in Vietnamese). Families with young children can also see Ba điều cảm ơn (Three thank-yous; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Choose the point for your morning pause and write it next to last week's midday one.
