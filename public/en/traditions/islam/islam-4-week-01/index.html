Islam 4 – Week 1: Who zakat is for

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

What this course is and is not. Islam 4 describes three parts of Muslim life: giving (zakat, sadaqah), the month of Ramadan, and how to treat parents, relatives and neighbours. Learners do not need to be Muslim. The course does not teach ritual, does not teach how to calculate zakat, does not teach the Ramadan fast and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs. The exercises are everyday actions set by tamhonkhoe; they are not zakat or fasting and do not replace them. Muslims keep zakat and Ramadan as their own community and teachers teach them. This course is not financial advice.

This week: read the verse of the Qur'an that names who may receive zakat, and two verses on how to give. The exercise is one act of giving within your means, not talked about and never brought up again.

Measurable goal: read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; write the eight groups of 9:60 in your own words; make one act of giving in the week and mention it to no one; say at least one deliberate kind word each day, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

What zakat is (described)

[Traditional teaching] Zakat is one of the five pillars of Islam (see Islam 1, Week 2). The word is usually explained as "purifying" and "growing". An adult Muslim whose wealth is above a threshold called the nisab, held for a full lunar year, sets aside a share for the people the scripture names. For money and accumulated wealth the customary rate is 2.5% (one fortieth); for crops, livestock and some other kinds of property it differs. Scholars differ on how the threshold is reckoned and on some kinds of property. There is also sadaqah: voluntary giving, with no set amount and no set time.

[Traditional teaching] Shi'a tradition also has khums ("one fifth"): Shi'a scholars understand it as one fifth of the income left over after a year's expenses, in addition to zakat. This lesson only names it.

Eight groups

Giving without reproach

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955.
  • Verse 9:60 uses the word sadaqat, which Pickthall renders "the alms". The tradition reads the verse as the list of those who may receive obligatory zakat.
  • Some groups in the verse belong to a historical setting (captives, those newly drawn to the faith). Scholars today discuss how to apply them in different ways; the lesson does not choose between readings.
  • [Inference] Verses 2:262–263 do not say how much to give but how: without reminding, without shaming the receiver. A kind word is placed above a gift that hurts.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A survey, a follow-up and a randomised experiment: spending money on others went with, and in the experiment raised, the giver's happiness. The survey part is a correlation; the amounts in the experiment were small; one research group. (Dunn et al. 2008)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experiments, 4,045 people: acts of kindness to others raise the doer's well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] These studies measure giving in general, not zakat. They say nothing about its religious meaning.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes a day, plus one act of giving in the week.

  1. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). On odd days read 9:60, on even days 2:262–263.
  2. A map of the eight groups (days 1–2, 10 minutes). Write the eight groups of 9:60 in your own words. Next to each, note whether anyone around you is in a similar situation (short of food, in debt, far from home…). Do not write real names anywhere others can read them.
  3. One act of giving (once in the week). Set yourself a small amount whose loss would not touch your family's essential spending, or give time and effort instead. Give through a trustworthy channel (a charity kitchen, a school fund, a neighbourhood group), or directly if it will not embarrass the person receiving it.
  4. Never bring it up. After giving, tell no one, do not remind the receiver, do not post it online.
  5. One deliberate kind word a day. To a shop assistant, a delivery driver, a colleague: thank them for something specific, ask after them, use their name.
  6. Evening: write one line (2 minutes). "What did I give today, and did I want it to be noticed?"

Very important. This exercise is not zakat and does not replace it. The lesson does not teach how to calculate zakat and is not financial advice. Do not borrow, or cut your family's money for food, school or medicine, in order to give. Muslims who want to know how much zakat they owe should ask their own teacher or religious organisation.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "A small gift": the child chooses something in good condition (a book, a toy) or makes a gift to give to someone else. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 12.

The adult can say: "Some of our Muslim friends set aside part of what they have each year to help poor people. They teach each other not to boast about it when they give."

The child does not need to give money. Do not press the child to give away their favourite thing.

5. Stop practising if

  • Giving makes you more anxious about money, or you feel guilty that you "have not given enough". Then stop giving money and keep only the kind words and help with your time.
  • Someone uses teaching about giving to press you for money, or demands more than you can afford. You have the right to say no.
  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

One act of giving close to home: Cả nhà giúp cộng đồng (The whole family helps the community; in Vietnamese). Doing good without telling: Việc tốt bí mật (Secret good deeds; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Write the eight groups of 9:60 in your own words, then choose a day this week for your act of giving.
