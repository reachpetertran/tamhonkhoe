Islam 3 – Week 1: The reed flute

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: get to know Rumi and the Masnavi, and read slowly its sixteen opening lines, "The song of the reed". Sit still for 20 minutes a day and practise listening.

Measurable goal: read the passage slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; sit still for 20 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; write, in your own words and in 3 sentences, what the flute is lamenting.

What this course is, and is not

  • The course introduces Rumi and the Sufi tradition (tasawwuf, the inward path within Islam) through the Masnavi. It is a Level 3 course, about 30–45 minutes a day.
  • The course describes: it does not teach the dhikr of any Sufi order, does not teach the whirling sema, does not bring anyone into an order, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs.
  • The exercises (slow reading, sitting still, listening, writing, doing good) are neutral rhythms set by tamhonkhoe. They are not Sufi ritual and do not replace it.
  • Muslims, and people already following a Sufi order, keep their practice as their own community and teachers teach it.

1. Traditional teaching

Who Rumi was

[Verified] Jalāl al-Dīn Rūmī, whom Muslims call with respect Mawlānā ("our master"), was born in 1207 in Balkh (now in Afghanistan) and died in 1273 in Konya (now in Turkey). He was a Muslim scholar who taught law and religion, and became the Sufi poet whom the Encyclopaedia Britannica calls the greatest in the Persian language. His longest work is the Masnavi-yi Ma'navi ("Spiritual Couplets"), a teaching poem of some 25,000 lines, composed from about 1262 until the end of his life.

The song of the reed

[Traditional teaching] The Masnavi opens with the lament of the ney, a flute made from a reed stem. The Sufi tradition often reads the flute as an image of the human soul: separated from its source, God, and always longing to return.

Reading in context

  • Whinfield's version (1898) is a translation and abridgement: he left out many passages of the Persian original. Readers of Persian may find differences.
  • The "osier bed" is where the reed grew before it was cut to make a flute. [Traditional teaching] Many Sufi commentators understand it as the spiritual source of human beings, where they come from and where they will return.
  • The line "each interprets my notes in harmony with his own feelings" is also a reminder to readers: it is easy to hear Rumi's poetry as saying what we already want. [Expert judgement] The course tries to read Rumi within his Islamic context, without separating the poetry from the faith.

2. What science finds

  • We have not been able to verify any review that evaluates reading Rumi or Sufi practice itself.
  • [Verified] The closest is research on meditation: 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people, found that mindfulness meditation programmes reduced anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, and were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] A review of 83 studies found that about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience, most often anxiety or depression, including people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] Sitting and listening in this course is neither the mindfulness meditation of the research nor a Sufi practice. These figures only show that sitting still may help modestly, and may also cause discomfort.

3. Practice for adults

About 30 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (5 minutes). Read the passage once, aloud if you can. The second time, stop at whichever line catches you and read it three times.
  2. Sitting and listening (20 minutes). If you are new to sitting, start with 10 minutes and build up through the week.
    • Sit upright, eyes open looking down, or closed. Notice sounds near and far: traffic, a fan, voices. No need to name or judge them; just hear each sound begin and fade.
    • When your mind wanders, gently come back to the sounds.
    • Muslims: keep dhikr as your community teaches. This listening exercise is tamhonkhoe's adaptation, not dhikr.
  3. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). "What did I miss, or long for, today?"
  4. Once in the week (5 minutes). Write 3 sentences in your own words: what the flute is lamenting, and why "not one fathoms" it.

4. Kids' corner 6+: The bottle flute

About 5–10 minutes, with an adult.

  1. One clean, empty plastic bottle each (no glass). The adult shows how: rest the bottle's mouth just under the lower lip and blow gently across the opening until the bottle "hoots".
  2. Pour a little water in and blow again: is the note higher or lower? Try three water levels.
  3. Everyone sits still and listens until the sound has completely gone before the next blow.
  4. Ask: "The bottle can only sing when it's empty inside. Why do you think that is?"

Safety: blow gently, one breath at a time; if the child feels dizzy, stop and breathe normally. Everyone uses their own bottle.

The adult can say: "There's a very old poem about a flute made from a reed. It sings well because it is empty inside."

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • A poem about separation and homesickness brings up your own grief (losing someone, being far from family) more than you can carry. Then read a different passage, sit for less time, open your eyes, or talk with someone you trust.
  • Sitting for 20 minutes keeps bringing up painful memories or thoughts. Go back to 5–10 minutes, or walk slowly instead of sitting.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for longer than 20 minutes.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

When sitting still brings up strong feelings, use Neo lại khi cảm xúc dâng cao (Anchoring when emotions run high; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Read the passage once aloud, then sit still for 10 minutes and count how many different sounds you can hear.
