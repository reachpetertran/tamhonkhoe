Islam 3 – Week 6: Review

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read the story of the elephant in a dark room and the "keep silence" passage, then look back over the past five weeks. Choose two habits to keep and plan four weeks.

Measurable goal: rate the course's six habits; choose two to keep; write a four-week plan with at least two "if… then…" sentences; sit still for 25 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

The elephant in a dark room

[Traditional teaching] Book III of the Masnavi tells how an elephant was put on show in a dark room. It was too dark to see, so everyone felt it with their hands. The one who felt the trunk said the beast was like a water-pipe; the one who felt the ear said it was like a large fan; the one who felt a leg said it was like a pillar; the one who felt the back said it was like a throne. Each described it differently, according to the part they touched. (tamhonkhoe's summary of Whinfield's prose.) Rumi draws the lesson:

"Keep silence"

[Traditional teaching] A few passages later, Rumi says there are things that words cannot hold.

Reading in context

  • In Whinfield's version, the prose opening of the story uses an old nineteenth-century word for the people who brought the elephant; the lesson only summarises it. The story of blind people, or people in the dark, feeling an elephant also appears in other traditions, such as the Buddhist scriptures (Udāna 6.4); the course makes no further comparison.
  • [Inference] In the Masnavi, the elephant story is about the limits of the senses: the outward eye sees only the "foam"; seeing the "sea" needs an inward way of seeing. "Him" and "that Sun" in the second passage mean God.
  • [Expert judgement] Today the elephant story is often used to say that "all religions see the same truth". The course does not use it that way: the traditions really do differ about God, the soul and the path, and tamhonkhoe presents those differences without ranking them.
  • "Keep silence" here is silence in order to listen to God, within the life of a Muslim. The course's sitting still is a neutral rhythm and does not replace that.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] "If… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65), according to a meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 people; goals and measures varied widely. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006)
  • [Verified] 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally et al. 2010)
  • [Inference] That is why the four-week plan below keeps only two habits, and allows for a missed day.

3. Practice for adults

About 35–40 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (5 minutes). On odd days read the "palm of a hand" passage; on even days read "keep silence". One day in the week, reread your favourite passage from the past five weeks.
  2. Sitting still (25 minutes). As in Week 5. In the last minute, sit completely still, doing nothing at all, just listening.
  3. Once in the week: rate yourself (15 minutes). Rate each habit from 1 (hardly did it) to 5 (did it regularly and found it helpful):
Habit Week Score 1–5
Slow reading of poetry or scripture in the morning 1–6
Sitting and listening, 20–25 minutes 1–6
A day buying nothing extra 2
A daily act of care 3
An unseen good deed 4
Talking with a companion 5
  1. Choose two habits and write a four-week plan (10 minutes). For each habit, write one "if… then…" sentence, for example: "If I pour my first coffee, then I read one passage of poetry slowly." Add: "If I miss a day, then the next day I do the shortest version."
  2. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). "Which part of the 'elephant' was I able to touch today?"

4. Kids' corner 6+: The elephant in the dark

About 5–10 minutes, with an adult.

  1. The adult puts 3–4 safe, familiar objects into a cloth bag (a comb, an orange, a Lego brick, a spoon). Nothing sharp, breakable, or small enough to swallow.
  2. Each person puts a hand in the bag, feels one object, and may only describe it, not look: "It's round, a bit bumpy…".
  3. Everyone guesses. Then open the bag and look.
  4. Ask: "When you could only feel a little bit, did you guess right? When do we know better?"

The adult can say: "There's an old story about people feeling an elephant in the dark. Each one touched only one part, so they all described it differently. That's why we listen to others too, and look more carefully."

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You feel as if you are "disappearing", unable to feel your body or emotions, and this lasts after the sitting. Open your eyes, stand up, feel both feet on the floor, talk to someone; if it continues, tell a doctor.
  • Rating yourself makes you blame yourself for "not doing enough". A low score only shows which habit doesn't fit right now, not that you have failed.
  • Sitting for 25 minutes keeps bringing up painful memories or thoughts. Go back to 15–20 minutes, open your eyes, or walk slowly instead of sitting.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for longer than 20 minutes.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

When sitting still brings up strong feelings, use Neo lại khi cảm xúc dâng cao (Anchoring when emotions run high; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Write two "if… then…" sentences for the two habits you want to keep.
