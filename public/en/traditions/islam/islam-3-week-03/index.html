Islam 3 – Week 3: Love

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: explore "love" ('ishq, mahabba) in the Sufi tradition: love directed towards God, which, for Rumi, is what cures pride. Sitting still rises to 25 minutes.

Measurable goal: sit still for 25 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days (or 20 minutes if 25 is too hard); do one concrete act of care for another person on at least 5 of 7 days, and note it down.

1. Traditional teaching

"O LOVE, sweet madness"

[Traditional teaching] Straight after the "pitcher" passage, Rumi praises love. In a note, Whinfield explains that love here means the strong attraction that draws all creatures back to reunion with their Creator.

"If ye love Allah"

[Traditional teaching] In the Qur'an, love of Allah is tied to following the Prophet Muhammad. The Sufi tradition teaches love within that frame: the early Sufis kept prayer, fasting and religious law like all Muslims.

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955.
  • "The Beloved" in Sufi poetry usually means God. [Expert judgement] Reading the passage as a romantic love poem misses its context.
  • Plato and Galen are the great names of Greek philosophy and medicine, well known in the Islamic world of Rumi's time. The line says that love cures an illness that philosophy and medicine cannot: pride.
  • The line about Mount Sinai recalls Moses (Musa) asking to see God, told in chapter 7 of the Qur'an. Whinfield gives "Koran vii. 139" in an older numbering; Pickthall numbers it 7:143.
  • Very important: "Sweet madness" is a poetic image. It does not mean giving up reason, neglecting duties to your family, or staying in a relationship that harms you for the sake of "love".

2. What science finds

  • We have not been able to verify any study that measures "love of God" in the Sufi sense.
  • [Verified] The closest is research on relationships: a meta-analysis of 148 studies, 308,849 people, found that those with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation; it does not prove that relationships are the cause. (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010)
  • [Inference] The "daily act of care" builds on this general idea: relationships are fed by small, regular actions. The study above did not evaluate this exercise.

3. Practice for adults

About 35–38 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (5 minutes). On odd days read the poem; on even days read 3:31. Stop at one line and read it three times.
  2. Sitting still (25 minutes). Up from 20 to 25 minutes. If 25 minutes is too hard, sit for 20 minutes all week.
    • Muslims: keep dhikr as your community teaches.
    • Those who are not Muslim: sit and listen as in the past two weeks. In the last minute, think of someone you love and one small thing you will do for them today. This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation, not a Sufi practice.
  3. During the day: one act of care (5 minutes). Something concrete for another person: pour them a drink, ask how they are, fix something for them, listen to your child's whole story. Nobody needs to know.
  4. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). "What did love do through my hands today?"

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "Sending a wish": the family sits quietly, thinks of one person, and together whispers a kind wish for them. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 7.

The adult can say: "When we love someone, we do something small for them. What would you like to do for someone today?"

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Sitting for 25 minutes keeps bringing up painful memories or thoughts. Go back to 15–20 minutes, open your eyes, or walk slowly instead of sitting.
  • You have long spells of elation, little sleep without feeling tired, or a belief that you have been chosen for a special mission. Stop practising and talk to a doctor or mental health professional.
  • "Caring for others" is wearing you out, or you are in a relationship where there is violence. Look after your own safety first; this is not what the poem teaches.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for longer than 20 minutes.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

When sitting still brings up strong feelings, use Neo lại khi cảm xúc dâng cao (Anchoring when emotions run high; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Write down one person's name and one small thing you will do for them before you go to sleep.
