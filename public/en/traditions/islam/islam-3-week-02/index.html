Islam 3 – Week 2: Separation and return

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: explore the idea of "return" in the Qur'an and in Rumi's poetry. In Week 1 the flute longed for its reed bed; this week Rumi says what keeps people far from their source: a greed that has no bottom.

Measurable goal: sit still for 20 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; keep one full day buying nothing beyond essentials; write down three things you feel are "holding you back".

1. Traditional teaching

"Unto Him we are returning"

[Traditional teaching] The verse just before (2:155) says that Allah will test people with fear, hunger, and loss of wealth, lives and crops, and then gives good news to the steadfast. Verse 156 describes those steadfast people. Muslims often say these words on hearing bad news, especially news of a death.

The pitcher that never fills

[Traditional teaching] Straight after the song of the reed, Rumi turns from lament to advice: what keeps people far from their source is dependence on wealth and desire.

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955.
  • In Whinfield's note, "Raw" and "Ripe" are Sufi terms: the "raw" see only the outside of things, the "ripe" are those who have gone deep into the inner life.
  • [Inference] In 2:156, "returning" is first of all the return to Allah after death. Rumi's poetry uses the same image for a return within this life, by letting go of greed. The course only presents this and takes no position on the afterlife.
  • Very important: "Burst thy bonds" of silver and gold is not financial advice. The passage does not tell anyone to leave their job or home or give away everything; caring for yourself and your family is still a duty. What Rumi points to is greed, the pitcher that "never fills".

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples: people who put more weight on money and possessions reported slightly lower well-being (r = −0.19 for measures of materialist values and beliefs). This is a correlation; it does not prove that wanting possessions is the cause. (Dittmar et al. 2014)
  • [Inference] The figure is small and describes a general tendency. It does not show that a day without shopping will make anyone happier.

3. Practice for adults

About 30–32 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (5 minutes). On odd days read 2:156; on even days read the "pitcher" passage. Stop at one line and read it three times.
  2. Sitting still (20 minutes). As in Week 1: listen to the sounds. When a want appears (to check your phone, to eat, to get up), just note "wanting" and come back to the sounds.
  3. One day this week: buy nothing extra. Choose the day in advance. On that day buy only essentials (food, medicine, what the family needs), no extra shopping, no browsing shopping sites. If you slip, note it and try again on another day.
  4. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). "What held me back today?"
  5. Once in the week (5 minutes). Write down three things you feel are "holding you back" (an object, a habit, a wish to be praised…), and for each one a small step to loosen it.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "A small gift": the child chooses a home-made card, or a toy they want to give, and takes it to someone together with the adult. Don't make the child give away something they still love. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 12.

The adult can say: "If we hold our hands too tight, we can't receive anything new. Opening them a little feels good too."

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You are grieving or have just had a great loss, and verse 2:156 brings up more pain than you can carry. You can skip the verse, read only the poem, or rest this week.
  • The "buy nothing extra" day makes you anxious, self-blaming, or leads you to skip what your family needs. That is not the point of the exercise; stop this part.
  • Sitting for 20 minutes keeps bringing up painful memories or thoughts. Go back to 5–10 minutes, open your eyes, or walk slowly instead of sitting.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for longer than 20 minutes.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

When sitting still brings up strong feelings, use Neo lại khi cảm xúc dâng cao (Anchoring when emotions run high; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Choose this week's "buy nothing extra" day and put it in your calendar.
