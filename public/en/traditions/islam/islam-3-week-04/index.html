Islam 3 – Week 4: Letting go of self

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: explore "letting go of self" in the Sufi tradition through two Masnavi passages: the man at his friend's door, and the rusted mirror. Each day, do one good deed that nobody knows about.

Measurable goal: sit still for 25 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; do one unseen good deed on at least 5 of 7 days; each evening, note one moment when you wanted to be "right" or "praised".

1. Traditional teaching

The self in the Sufi tradition (described)

[Traditional teaching] Sufi teachers speak a great deal about the nafs, the self that desires and puts itself forward, and about purifying it. At the end of the path, many Sufi texts speak of fana, the "passing away" of the self in God. This is deep doctrine, understood in different ways within the Sufi tradition itself; the course only names it, and does not explain it or teach a practice for it.

Just before the passage below, the Masnavi tells of a lion hunting with a wolf and a fox. The wolf divides the prey, talking of "my share" and "thy share", and the lion kills him. Seeing this, the fox says it all belongs to the lion; the lion, pleased, gives it all to the fox, saying: "Thou art no longer a fox, but myself." (tamhonkhoe's summary of Whinfield's version.)

Reading in context

  • The heading of "The man at the door" is Whinfield's. In the story the "friend" stands for God; "raw" and "ripe" (here "well-cooked") are the terms met in Week 2.
  • [Traditional teaching] The image of the heart as a mirror to be polished is common in Sufi writing. In a note, Whinfield says that Bernard of Clairvaux, a twelfth-century Christian monk, has a similar idea; the course makes no further comparison.
  • [Inference] The answer "'Tis Thou who art at the door" is not a password. The story says he travelled for a year and his heart "burned till it was cooked": the inner change comes before the words.
  • Very important: "Letting go of self" does not mean hating yourself, putting yourself down, or letting others belittle, exploit or abuse you. The "fire" and "burning" in the story are images of longing and growing up, not advice to make your body suffer.

2. What science finds

  • We have not been able to verify any study that measures "letting go of self" in the Sufi sense.
  • [Verified] The closest is research on acts of kindness: 27 experiments, 4,045 people, found that acts of kindness raise the doer's own well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] The research does not single out good deeds that "nobody knows about". This week uses them as a way of practising attention to others rather than to being seen.

3. Practice for adults

About 35–40 minutes a day.

  1. Morning: slow reading (5 minutes). On odd days read the story of the man at the door; on even days read the mirror passage.
  2. Sitting still (25 minutes). As in Week 3. When a thought about yourself appears (I'm good, I'm bad, what do people think of me), just note "I" and come back to the sounds.
  3. During the day: one unseen good deed (5 minutes). For example: wash the dishes without saying so, pick up litter in a shared corridor, write an unsigned thank-you note. Don't tell anyone, including online.
  4. Evening: "polishing the mirror" (3 minutes). Note one moment today when you wanted to be "right" or "praised", and what you did then. No scoring; just note it.

4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity "A good deed for someone at home": the child chooses a small way to help someone at home, does it, and then talks about how it felt. This week, try one more thing: do it without telling the person. See the full activity at Kids' corner – Activity 6.

The adult can say: "Some good deeds don't need anyone to praise them. It's enough that we know."

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You feel as if you are "disappearing", unable to feel your body or emotions, and this lasts after the sitting. Open your eyes, stand up, feel both feet on the floor, talk to someone; if it continues, tell a doctor.
  • The idea of "letting go of self" makes you blame yourself or feel worthless, or someone uses it to make you put up with wrong. Stop this part and talk to someone you trust.
  • Sitting for 25 minutes keeps bringing up painful memories or thoughts. Go back to 15–20 minutes, open your eyes, or walk slowly instead of sitting.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for longer than 20 minutes.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

When sitting still brings up strong feelings, use Neo lại khi cảm xúc dâng cao (Anchoring when emotions run high; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Think of three small good deeds you could do tomorrow without anyone knowing.
