Islam 2 – Week 7: Perseverance

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read two passages of the Qur'an about steadfastness and about ease going together with hardship. After six weeks, the daily rhythm starts to meet busy, sick or tired days. This week you practise keeping it through those days without forcing yourself.

Measurable goal: write a "minimum version" for hard days; never drop the whole rhythm for two days in a row all week; sit still for 20 minutes on at least 3 days (other days 15 minutes or the minimum version).

1. Traditional teaching

Steadfastness and prayer

[Traditional teaching] Verse 153 of chapter 2 (Al-Baqarah) joins two things this course has read about: patience (sabr, Islam 1 Week 4) and prayer (Week 3 of this course).

Hardship and ease

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 94 (Ash-Sharh) is a short chapter. The tradition teaches that it was revealed to comfort the Prophet Muhammad during a hard time. Verses 5 and 6 repeat the same words twice.

Reading in context

  • Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955. The second line ends with a semicolon in the file because the chapter continues; the page quotes it as it stands.
  • Pickthall has "with hardship goeth ease": ease goes with hardship. [Inference] The verse does not say hardship will end at once, but that it does not come alone.
  • [Inference] In 2:153, believers are both steadfast and asking for help. As in Islam 1, steadfastness here does not mean carrying everything alone.
  • Very important: steadfastness does not mean putting up with violence, abuse, exploitation, or carrying on with an exercise that is wearing you out. If you are not safe, the first step is to get help.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally et al. 2010)
  • [Verified] 33 studies of self-control training: the effect was small, and smaller still after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017)
  • [Inference] Together they suggest that a rhythm is kept by doing it regularly and small, rather than by great effort. Missing a day does not undo a habit already formed.

3. Practice for adults

About 25–30 minutes on ordinary days; 5 minutes on hard days.

  1. Day 1: write your minimum version (5 minutes). A version you can do even when ill, busy or exhausted, for example: one pause of three breaths and 3 minutes of sitting still. Write it down and keep it beside your three "if… then…" sentences from Week 5.
  2. Morning: slow reading (3 minutes). On odd days read 2:153; on even days read 94:5–6.
  3. Sitting still (20 minutes, on at least 3 days). Try 20 minutes on days when you have time; on other days sit for 15 minutes. In the last minute, open your eyes and feel both feet on the floor.
    • Muslims: keep dhikr as your community teaches.
    • Those who are not Muslim: sit still with the out-breath. This is tamhonkhoe's adaptation, not dhikr.
  4. Three pauses. As last week.
  5. Hard days: do only the minimum version. Do not make up for it the next day.
  6. This week's rule: never miss two days in a row. If you miss a whole day, do at least the minimum version the next day.
  7. Evening: write one line (3 minutes). Mark today as "full", "minimum" or "missed", with one word about why.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "If the tower falls, build it again" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The child builds a tall tower from wooden blocks, Lego or paper boxes.
  2. When the tower falls (on its own, or the child blows it down), the adult says: "It fell. What shall we do next?"
  3. The child builds again, perhaps choosing a lower but sturdier tower this time.
  4. The adult says: "If it falls, we build it again, that's fine. Some days we build tall, some days just one level."

Use only light blocks, built on the floor; no standing on chairs to build higher. No scores, no contest over whose tower is taller.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself.
  • You are forcing yourself to sit for 20 minutes even though it gets worse the longer you sit. Go back to 10–15 minutes; 20 minutes is not a required target.
  • For many days in a row you can manage only the minimum version, together with losing interest in everything, lasting tiredness or feeling low most of the time for two weeks or more. This may be a sign of depression, not "lack of patience"; see a doctor or a mental health professional.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting still for 20 minutes or more.
  • Then stop and see Get help now.
