Taoism 4 – Week 1: Skill as flow

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This course: Level 4, "Integration". There is no long sitting; each day you read for a few minutes, then carry the exercise into work, housework and family life. It is best taken after Taoism 1 "Water, stillness and enough", but that is not required. The course reads Taoist texts; it does not teach Taoist ritual or cultivation methods.

This week: read two short stories about skill in the Zhuangzi: the hunchback catching cicadas (chapter XIX) and the wheelwright (chapter XIII). Choose one skill, break it into its smallest part, and practise one part each day.

Measurable goal: practise one small part of a skill for 5–10 minutes a day, on at least 5 of 7 days, and write one line after each practice.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The Zhuangzi has many stories about skilled workers: a cook, a boatman, a woodcarver, a wheelwright. They do not preach; they only tell how they work. The first story is about a hunchback who catches cicadas with a sticky pole so well that it is as if he picked them up by hand. The second is about an old wheelwright working below the hall, who tells his ruler about the part of a craft that cannot be put into words.

Giles’s footnotes are left out.

Reading it in context

  • According to Giles’s note, the hunchback uses a bamboo pole tipped with birdlime; the "two balls" are balanced at the top of the pole. The course repeats this to make the story clear and leaves out the rest of the note.
  • The word "way" in Confucius’s question is used in its root sense: a road, a way of doing (Giles notes this too). It is the same word as Tao.
  • [Expert judgement] Confucius in the Zhuangzi is a character in parables, often speaking Taoist ideas. That is how the book is written; it is not a historical record of what Confucius said, and the course does not use it to praise or criticise Confucianism.
  • The wheelwright’s story begins while Duke Huan is reading the books of sages long dead. The wheelwright says those words are only "the dregs" of the ancients, then takes his own craft as an example. His closing sentence, the omitted part ("[…]"), repeats that point. [Inference] The reminder applies to this course too: reading scripture does not replace practice.
  • The translation keeps the word "hunchback" as in the story. The Zhuangzi often places great skill in people society overlooks; the word is not meant to mock anyone’s body.
  • The story of Cook Ding (chapter III) was read in Taoism 1 – Week 6, so this week takes two other stories on the same theme.
  • [Inference] Both stories point to one thing: skill comes from steady practice in small steps (two balls, three, five) at a moderate pace, without forcing. The story does not promise that everyone reaches the point of catching "as though with his hand".

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 88 studies with more than 11,000 people: the amount of deliberate practice explained about 26% of the differences in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and less than 1% in professions. Practice matters, but it is not everything. This is mostly correlational. (Macnamara et al. 2014)
  • [Inference] This fits the hunchback’s story in that steady practice helps, and it is also a reminder that results depend on many other things. If you improve more slowly than someone else, that is not your fault.
  • We have not found any study that directly evaluates this week’s exercise.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read both stories. The second time, pause at "neither too slowly nor too fast".
  2. Choose one skill (first day, 5 minutes). Something at work or at home you would like to do better: writing a clear email, reading aloud to a child, cooking a dish, typing, pronouncing an English sound, a gentle exercise movement. Break it into its smallest "ball", a part you can practise in 5–10 minutes.
  3. Practise one ball (5–10 minutes). Practise only that part, at a moderate pace. Put the phone away. While practising, attend only to that part, as the hunchback sees only the cicada’s wings.
  4. Write one line (1 minute). Which part you practised today, and a rating from 1 to 5. Do not compare yourself with others.
  5. End of the week. Read your seven lines. Which part can you now "hold", and which needs another week?

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "One more level" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Use wooden blocks, paper cups or clean bottle caps. The child stacks them into a tower.
  2. Before each new level, both of you take one slow breath, hands very gentle.
  3. If it falls, laugh together and build again. Write today’s highest level on a piece of paper.
  4. Each day, try one more level. The adult says: "Every day our hands get a little more skilful."

No heavy or breakable things; no standing on chairs to build higher. No competing with brothers or sisters.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Practising makes you blame yourself again and again, compare yourself with others, or feel "useless". Then cut down to 5 minutes, choose an easier part, and write down only what you managed.
  • A physical movement you are practising causes pain. Then stop that movement.
  • If the signs are serious, stop and see Get help now.
