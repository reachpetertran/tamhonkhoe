Taoism 2 – Week 2: Useful emptiness

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read chapter 11 of the Tao Te Ching slowly, sit still for 20 minutes each day noticing the quiet gaps, and leave one 10-minute gap in the day with no plans and no phone.

Measurable goal: sit still for 20 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; keep one 10-minute gap on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 11 offers three familiar images: a wheel, a clay vessel, a room. What makes each of them useful is not the solid part but the empty part. From this the chapter concludes: what exists brings benefit, what does not exist makes the use.

Reading it in context

  • [Inference] The chapter does not look down on the solid part: without spokes there is no wheel. The point is that the two parts need each other, like the pairs in Week 1.
  • [Expert judgement] The last sentence is usually read from the Chinese 有之以為利，無之以為用. Legge adds the words in brackets to make it clear; readers should remember that this is the translator's interpretation.
  • The course uses this image for one small thing in daily life: a stretch of time with nothing put into it. That is the course's application, not something the text teaches directly.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any study that evaluates leaving a stretch of time empty, as in this week's exercise.
  • [Verified] The closest evidence is research on rest. A 2017 meta-analysis of 86 publications with 38,124 workers: people who detach from work during time off (not working and not thinking about work) report less exhaustion and better sleep. This is a correlation: rest may not be the cause; people who are less exhausted may find it easier to detach. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017)
  • [Verified] Meditation is not always pleasant: a 2020 review of 83 studies found that about 8% of practitioners had adverse experiences (about 4% in experimental studies). (Farias et al. 2020)

Limits: a 10-minute gap is a habit of self-observation, not a treatment for burnout.

3. Practice for adults

About 25 minutes a day, plus the 10-minute gap.

  1. Slow reading (2 minutes). Read the chapter once. On the second reading, look at something hollow near you (a cup, a bowl, a teapot) and read the last sentence again.
  2. Sit still (20 minutes). Let the breath come and go by itself. Instead of attending to sounds or thoughts, notice the quiet between them: between two passing cars, between the out-breath and the in-breath, between two thoughts. Do not try to stretch the gap; just notice that it is there.
  3. One 10-minute gap. Choose a 10-minute slot in advance. Plan nothing for it, leave your phone in another room, do not read or listen to anything. You might sit by a window, stand on the porch, take a few steps. If you feel restless, let that be.
  4. Write one line (2 minutes). What did today's gap "hold": a new idea, some tiredness, a little calm? No score.
  5. Tick the day. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days for both.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "What works because it is empty?" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Walk around the house with the child looking for things that work because they have an empty space: a cup, a bowl, a box, a bag, a door, a buttonhole.
  2. For each one, ask: "What if this space were filled in?" (A solid cup can't hold water; a bricked-up doorway can't be walked through.)
  3. Pour a little water into a plastic cup so the child can see: the water sits in the empty space.
  4. To finish: "Empty spaces have a job too."

Use a plastic cup or something unbreakable. An adult keeps an eye on the child while walking around the house.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The empty time makes worries or painful memories crowd in without letting up. Then get up and walk slowly, or call someone close instead of sitting alone.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
