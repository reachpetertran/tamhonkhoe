Taoism 3 – Week 1: The butterfly dream

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Before you start this course: this is a Level 3 course about life, death and loss, with 30–45 minutes of practice a day for 6 weeks. It is best taken after Taoism 1 and Taoism 2. If you are depressed, newly bereaved, seriously ill, or having thoughts of dying or harming yourself, this course is not for now: please see Get help now and talk to someone you trust. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe PTSD, or long-lasting feelings of being detached from themselves should not study this course alone; if you want to take it, ask a doctor and study with an experienced guide.

This course only describes how the Zhuangzi speaks about life and death. It does not compare this with other traditions' views of the soul or the afterlife, and it does not ask you to believe anything.

This week: read the butterfly dream at the end of chapter II of the Zhuangzi slowly. Sit for 30 minutes each day, noticing the moments when one thing turns into another (an in-breath becoming an out-breath, a sound arriving and fading). Each evening, note one moment of "change" from the day.

Measurable goal: sit for 30 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; each evening note one change (waking up, switching tasks, moving place, a shift of mood), on at least 5 of 7 evenings.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Chapter II of the Zhuangzi discusses how every distinction, right and wrong, this and that, depends on where one stands. The chapter ends with a short dream. The Taoist tradition calls what the story points to "the transformation of things" (物化): all things changing into one another.

Reading it in context

  • [Expert judgement] The last sentence in the Chinese is 此之謂物化, usually understood as "this is called the transformation of things". Giles translates it as "Metempsychosis" (the transmigration of souls). The course leaves out that sentence and does not follow Giles's reincarnation reading.
  • [Inference] The story does not ask readers to doubt whether they are real. It points out that the boundaries between states (dreaming and waking, man and butterfly) are real, yet crossing them is an ordinary thing for all beings. In the weeks ahead, the Zhuangzi applies this way of seeing to life and death.
  • If reading this story leaves you unsteady, as if you or the world were "not real", stop reading, open your eyes, put your feet on the ground, and see "Stop practising if" below.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any study that directly evaluates reading the Zhuangzi or this week's "noticing change" exercise.
  • [Verified] A critical review describes "decentering" as three processes: knowing what one is experiencing, not identifying with inner experience, and being less carried away by the content of thoughts. It is a conceptual analysis, not a test of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015)
  • [Verified] An interview study of Western meditators recorded a group of difficult experiences to do with the "sense of self"; their impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting. (Lindahl et al. 2017)
  • [Verified] About 8% of meditators had an adverse experience, according to a review of 83 studies. (Farias et al. 2020)

Limits: this is practice in noticing, not treatment.

3. Practice for adults

About 30 minutes a day, plus 2 minutes of notes in the evening.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the story once. The second time, pause at "Suddenly, I awaked, and there I lay, myself again."
  2. Sit for 25 minutes.
    • First 5 minutes: notice where the in-breath turns into the out-breath. Do not control the breath.
    • Middle 15 minutes: notice sounds. Each time a sound starts or stops, note softly "change".
    • Last 5 minutes: notice a thought arising and then fading. Do not follow it.
  3. Back to the ground (2 minutes). Open your eyes, put both feet on the floor, look around and name three things in the room before you stand up.
  4. Evening note (2 minutes). One "change" from the day: waking up, moving from one task to another, a shift of mood. Write one sentence: what it was before, what it was after.
  5. Tick it off. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days for both.

4. Kids' corner 6+

"Caterpillar to butterfly" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. With the child, draw four boxes: egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly.
  2. Adult and child "act out" each box together: curl up like an egg, crawl like a caterpillar, sit still in the "chrysalis" (hug your knees, count slowly to five), then spread your arms and fly like a butterfly.
  3. Ask: "Where did the caterpillar go? Where did the butterfly come from?" Let the child answer.
  4. Finish with: "The caterpillar did not disappear. It changed into a butterfly."

Talk only about the butterfly's life cycle. Do not catch real caterpillars or butterflies to play with; if you look at them in the garden, just look, do not touch.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have traumatic flashbacks, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself or of dying.
  • A feeling that you or the world are "not real", or of being detached from your body, stays after you stand up. If it lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • The thought "life is just a dream, nothing matters" keeps coming back for several days. Then stop the course and talk to someone you trust.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for more than 20 minutes.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

If you feel unsteady while sitting, daynentang has a lesson that brings your attention back to your body and surroundings: Neo lại (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

This morning, just after waking, lie still for ten seconds and notice: going from sleep to waking, what changed?
