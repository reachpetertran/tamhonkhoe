Buddhism 3 – Week 6: Review

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

A reminder: this is a Level 3 course. If you are depressed, recently bereaved or having thoughts of self-harm, please leave this course for another time and see Get help now.

This week: read again the three key passages of the course. Each day, sit for 30 minutes with the exercise from the past five weeks that suits you best. At the end of the week, rate the five exercises and write a plan for the next four weeks.

Measurable goal: sit for 30 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; complete the five-exercise self-rating and a four-week plan.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The three passages below sum up the course: the five aggregates are not "me" (Week 1), the five aggregates are like foam when looked at closely (Week 2), and the teaching itself is a raft for crossing over, not for carrying (Week 3).

Reading it in context

  • Apply the raft to the course itself: the exercises are rafts. Keep those that still help you live more kindly and clearly; set down those that no longer help.
  • In the tradition, understanding impermanence and not-self is the work of a whole life of practice, not of six weeks. This course is only an introduction. Anyone who wants to go deeper should study with a teacher in the tradition.
  • After the course, it is best to return to 20–30 minutes of practice a day. The course does not advise practising alone at home for longer than 45 minutes.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2014 systematic review pooled 47 randomised trials with 3,515 participants. Mindfulness meditation programmes (usually around 8 weeks) reduced anxiety, depression and pain by small to moderate amounts, but were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] A 2018 critical review argued that much mindfulness research is methodologically weak, relies heavily on self-report, and does not monitor harms enough. (Van Dam et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] No study has evaluated this programme directly. The self-rating below only helps you look back on your own experience; it is not a scientific measure.

3. Practice for adults

About 35 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. Sitting (25 minutes). 10 minutes with the breath, then 15 minutes with one exercise you choose from the five you have learned.
  3. Back to the ground (5 minutes). Open your eyes, feel your feet, name three things you see, then stand up and stretch.
  4. At the end of the week: self-rating (15 minutes, once). Rate each exercise from 0 (did not help, or made me uncomfortable) to 3 (clearly helped):
Week Exercise Score 0–3
1 Trying to "give orders"; writing one thing that did not obey
2 Counting bubbles: seeing sounds and sensations begin and dissolve
3 Noticing the "grip"; writing about a raft
4 Seeing, naming, adding
5 Sitting and letting go; letting go of one small thing a day
  1. Write a four-week plan. Keep the two exercises with the highest scores. Write down how many minutes a day you will sit (20–30 minutes is suggested), when, where, and one sign that tells you to stop or cut back.

Any exercise you scored 0 because it left you unsteady or sad for a long time, leave behind. That too is using the raft well.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The book of changes" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Fold a sheet of paper in half. Write "Before" on the left and "After" on the right.
  2. With the child, remember the games you played: the cloud, the bubbles, the raft, the feely bag, the melting ice. The child chooses one and draws two pictures: before and after.
  3. Ask: "What changed? Which picture do you like?"
  4. Stick the paper on the wall or keep it in the child’s notebook.

Talk only about change in objects and nature. Do not talk about death, and do not discuss "there is no self".

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • While contemplating, you feel detached from your body, as if watching yourself from outside, or everything becomes empty and strange, and the feeling stays after you stand up. If so, stop the contemplation, do the "back to the ground" step, and talk to someone you trust. If the feeling lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • Thoughts like "nothing means anything" or "I don’t matter" keep coming back for several days.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before practices longer than 20 minutes.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.
