Buddhism 4 – Week 4: Partners together

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Before you start: This course does not use the discourse to advise anyone to stay in a relationship with violence, threats or control. If that is your situation, please leave this week for another time and see Get help now.

This week: read a short discourse about an elderly couple and what the Buddha told them. Each day, say one kind and specific word to your partner. During the week, talk together about what each of you values. If you do not have a partner, practise with the person closest to you.

Measurable goal: say one specific thank-you or word of appreciation to your partner (or someone close) on at least 5 of 7 days; have one conversation of about 20 minutes about what you both value.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Nakula's parents were a lay couple in the land of the Bhaggas. When the Buddha visits their home, they tell him that since they married young, neither has betrayed the other, even in thought. They want to see each other in this life and the next. The Buddha answers with four qualities the two should share equally.

Reading it in context

  • In ancient Indian society, parents usually arranged their children's marriages. The discourse reports this as a fact of its time; this course does not discuss how people marry.
  • "Seeing each other in the next life" belongs to the Buddhist belief in rebirth. Learners do not need to accept that belief to practise the rest.
  • "Equal in faith" in the discourse means sharing faith in the Buddha. This course does not discuss whether partners share a religion or not. What it keeps is two people both valuing living kindly, generously and wisely, and saying nice things to each other.
  • The Advice to Sigālaka (DN 31, section 30) also lists five things a husband does for his wife (honouring her, not looking down on her, being faithful, relinquishing authority to her, giving her adornments) and five things a wife does for her husband (organising the household well, managing the domestic help, being faithful, preserving his earnings, being deft and tireless). That division of roles reflects its society. This course keeps the idea that duties go both ways, not the division of roles by gender.
  • The discourse is about faithfulness. This course makes no judgement about anyone who is divorced, widowed or living alone.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any peer-reviewed review that evaluates practice based on this discourse.
  • [Inference] This week's exercise rests on an ancient text and on practitioners' experience. We give no scientific figures for this week so as not to borrow results from other studies. Long-standing problems between partners are best discussed with a family or couples counsellor.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day, plus one conversation during the week.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. One kind word a day. Say one specific thank-you or word of appreciation to your partner: not "you're great", but "thank you for picking up our child today so I could finish my meeting". Note it in the evening.
  3. One conversation (about 20 minutes, once this week). Choose a time when neither of you is tired. Take turns answering one or more questions; the other only listens, without interrupting or arguing: - What do I think matters most in how we live? - Where do I want to be kinder? - Who do I want to help, or what do I want to share with others? - What am I trying to understand at the moment?

At the end, each person says one thing they heard from the other. 4. If you argue easily: choose just one question and keep it to 10 minutes. If the conversation turns into an argument, stop and try another day.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Kind words at dinner" (5–10 minutes, the whole family)

  1. At dinner, each person thanks someone else in the family for one specific thing today.
  2. The person thanked just says "thank you for noticing".
  3. Anyone who can't think of something can say it later; no pressure.

The game is about how the whole family speaks; parents' own issues are not discussed in front of the child.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The conversation stirs up a big argument or fear. Then stop and, if needed, find a family counsellor.
  • Your partner threatens, controls or harms you. Do not practise "saying nice things" to calm someone who is harming you; find someone who can help.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

To practise saying what you mean honestly and gently in the family, daynentang has a lesson: Nói thật, lòng nhẹ (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Think of something small your partner (or someone close) did for you this week that you haven't thanked them for. Thank them today.
