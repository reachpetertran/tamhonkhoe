Buddhism 3 – Week 1: The characteristic of not-self

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Before you start this course: this is a Level 3 course, 30–45 minutes a day for 6 weeks, best taken after Buddhism 1 and Buddhism 2. Contemplating impermanence and not-self can leave some people feeling unsteady, as if they or the world were "not real". If you are depressed, recently bereaved, going through a major crisis, or having thoughts of death or self-harm, this course is not for now: please see Get help now and talk to someone you trust. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe PTSD, or lasting feelings of being detached from themselves should not take this course alone; if you want to, ask a doctor and learn with an experienced teacher.

This course only describes Buddhist teaching. It does not compare it with other traditions’ views of the soul or the self, and it does not ask you to believe anything.

This week: get to know the five aggregates, then practise noticing things in body and mind that do not go the way you want. Sit for 30 minutes a day; each evening, write down one thing that day that did not go the way you wanted.

Measurable goal: sit for 30 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; each evening, write down one thing that "did not obey", on at least 5 of 7 evenings.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Buddhist tradition holds the discourse on the characteristic of not-self (Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta) to be the Buddha’s second teaching, given to his first five monks in the deer park. It goes through the five aggregates, the five groups that make up a person in Buddhist analysis:

  • Form: the body and matter.
  • Feeling: pleasant, unpleasant or neutral feeling tone (practised in Buddhism 2, Week 3).
  • Perception: recognising and naming ("that is a bird").
  • Choices: intentions and what the mind puts together (Sujato’s translation; often rendered "formations").
  • Consciousness: knowing.

The discourse repeats the same reasoning for feeling, perception, choices and consciousness. Then the Buddha asks:

Reading it in context

  • The reasoning is practical: what is truly "me" should obey me; the five aggregates do not obey us, so they are not fit to be regarded as "me, mine". This week only practises seeing that in small things.
  • The discourse is about the five aggregates. It does not say "you do not exist", "you do not matter" or "no one is responsible for what they do". If you notice yourself reading it that way, stop and read this section again.
  • The end of the discourse says that a disciple who sees this grows disillusioned, desire fades, they are freed, and the five monks were freed while hearing it (sections 11.1–12.3). This course presents the practice and does not ask you to believe in rebirth or stages of attainment.
  • Other traditions have their own understanding of the soul and the self. This course does not compare them or rank them.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2015 critical review grouped related ideas in psychology (self-distanced perspective, cognitive distancing, cognitive defusion) under the name "decentering". The authors propose it involves three processes: awareness of what one is experiencing, disidentification from internal experience, and reduced reactivity to the content of thoughts. This is a conceptual analysis, not a trial of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015)
  • [Inference] "Disidentification from internal experience" is close to "this is not mine", but the aims differ: psychology aims at mental health, while the discourse aims at liberation as Buddhism understands it. No study has measured this week’s exercise on its own.
  • [Verified] An interview study of Western Buddhist meditators and teachers recorded 59 kinds of meditation-related experience in 7 domains, including "sense of self". The same kind of experience was very positive for some people and very negative for others; its impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting. (Lindahl et al. 2017)

Limits: this is why every lesson in the course has a "Stop practising if" section and a "back to the ground" step at the end of each sitting.

3. Practice for adults

About 30 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. Sit with the breath (10 minutes). As in Buddhism 2.
  3. Try to "give orders" (12 minutes). Notice each aggregate in turn, about two minutes each: a sensation in the body (form), whether it is pleasant or unpleasant (feeling), the moment the mind names a sound (perception), an urge that just arose, such as wanting to scratch or stand up (choices), and the knowing (consciousness). With each, ask silently: "Can I command this?" Just look and answer honestly; do not force a conclusion.
  4. Back to the ground (5 minutes). Open your eyes. Feel your feet on the floor, look around and name three things you see, then stand up and stretch.
  5. Each evening, write one line. One thing that day that did not go the way you wanted: sleepiness coming early, a hoarse voice, a thought that kept coming back. Example: "I wanted to focus on a report, but my mind kept going back to this morning."

Do not use this exercise to blame yourself ("I can’t control anything"). The point is only to see clearly, with kindness.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The shape-changing cloud" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Go somewhere you can see the sky, or look out of a window. Choose one cloud.
  2. Each person says what shape it looks like ("a dinosaur", "a cake").
  3. Wait a little, then look again: "What does it look like now?"
  4. Ask: "Can we tell the cloud to stay still?" Then laugh together: clouds keep changing with the wind.

Talk only about change in nature. Do not talk about death, and do not discuss "there is no self" with children. On a cloudless day, watch the steam rising from a cup of warm water instead.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • While contemplating, you feel detached from your body, as if watching yourself from outside, or everything becomes empty and strange, and the feeling stays after you stand up. If so, stop the contemplation, do the "back to the ground" step, and talk to someone you trust. If the feeling lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • Thoughts like "nothing means anything" or "I don’t matter" keep coming back for several days.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before practices longer than 20 minutes.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

If you feel unsteady while sitting, daynentang has a lesson that brings attention back to the body and surroundings: Neo lại (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Next time something does not go the way you want, say silently: "I can’t command this." Then take one breath.
