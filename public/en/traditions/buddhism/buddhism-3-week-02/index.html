Buddhism 3 – Week 2: The lump of foam

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

A reminder: this is a Level 3 course. If you are depressed, recently bereaved or having thoughts of self-harm, please leave this course for another time and see Get help now.

This week: read the five images of the discourse on a lump of foam, then, while sitting, practise seeing a sound or a sensation begin and end, like a bubble on water.

Measurable goal: sit for 30 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; after each sitting, write down one thing you clearly saw begin and end, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The discourse on a lump of foam (Pheṇapiṇḍūpama Sutta) belongs to the Linked Discourses, in the collection on the aggregates. The Buddha is staying near Ayodhya on the bank of the Ganges, and uses five images for the five aggregates met in Week 1.

The discourse goes on: feeling is like a water bubble, perception like a midsummer mirage, choices like a banana trunk peeled layer by layer with no heartwood, and consciousness like a magic trick at the crossroads. It ends with verses:

Reading it in context

  • "Kinsman of the Sun" is a name for the Buddha.
  • "Vacuous, hollow, without essence" refers to the five aggregates: none of them has a lasting core to hold on to as "me". The discourse does not say that life is meaningless or that people are not precious.
  • The verses have three more stanzas about the body left behind when warmth, life and consciousness leave it (sections 11.1–13.4). This course does not quote them or practise contemplating them.
  • The bubble image fits feeling because feelings come and go quickly. You practised noticing feelings in Buddhism 2, Week 3; this week only adds noticing when a feeling begins and when it dissolves.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2014 systematic review pooled 47 randomised trials with 3,515 participants. Mindfulness meditation programmes (usually around 8 weeks) reduced anxiety, depression and pain by small to moderate amounts, but were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] A 2018 critical review argued that much mindfulness research is methodologically weak, relies heavily on self-report, and does not monitor harms enough. (Van Dam et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] No study has measured "foam" contemplation on its own. The figures above concern mindfulness programmes in general.

3. Practice for adults

About 30 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages, then read the verses again.
  2. Sit with the breath (10 minutes).
  3. Counting bubbles (12 minutes). Widen your attention to sounds and body sensations. When a sound comes (a car, a fan), notice when it begins, while it lasts, and when it stops. Do the same with a sensation (an itch, warmth, a slight twinge). Each time you clearly see something end, say silently: "gone". If nothing is clear, come back to the breath: each breath also has a beginning and an end.
  4. Back to the ground (5 minutes). Open your eyes, feel your feet, name three things you see, then stand up and stretch.
  5. Write one line. One thing you clearly saw both begin and dissolve. Example: "A bicycle bell: clear, then fainter, then gone."

If watching things "dissolve" leaves you sad or empty, switch to noticing what newly arises: a new sound, a new breath.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Soap bubbles" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Mix soapy water, or use a ready-made bubble bottle. Play outside or on a floor that is easy to wipe.
  2. The adult blows one bubble. Watch it float with the child, then pop.
  3. Ask: "What colours does the bubble have? How long did it fly?" Count the seconds together.
  4. Let the child blow. Ask: "Can we keep a bubble forever?" Then blow new ones.

Do not let the child drink the soapy water; wash hands afterwards. Talk only about bubbles and change, not about death.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • While contemplating, you feel detached from your body, as if watching yourself from outside, or everything becomes empty and strange, and the feeling stays after you stand up. If so, stop the contemplation, do the "back to the ground" step, and talk to someone you trust. If the feeling lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • Thoughts like "nothing means anything" or "everything dissolves anyway, why bother" keep coming back for several days.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before practices longer than 20 minutes.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

Listen to one sound until it has completely stopped. Then listen to the next one.
