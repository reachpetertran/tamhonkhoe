Buddhism 2 – Week 4: The second arrow

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: with small discomforts in the day, practise separating two parts: the unpleasant sensation (the first arrow) and what the mind adds afterwards (the second arrow). Sit for 25 minutes a day and write down one pair of "arrows".

Measurable goal: sit for 25 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; each day, write down one small situation with its first and second arrow, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The Arrow discourse (Salla Sutta) belongs to the Linked Discourses (Saṃyutta Nikāya), in the collection on feelings. Buddhism teaches that a trained person still feels pain like everyone else; the difference is that they do not add mental suffering to it. This discourse follows on from last week’s mindfulness of feelings.

Reading it in context

  • The verse is clear: a trained person still feels pleasure and pain. The discourse does not teach "feeling no pain", and it does not look down on someone who is in pain.
  • The image of "beating their breast" describes added suffering; it is not a ban on crying. With a great loss, a recent bereavement or trauma, this course does not use the "second arrow" exercise; such times need time, people close to you and sometimes a professional.
  • The discourse makes one more point: when ordinary people are in pain they often look for sensual pleasure to escape, and so feed greed for pleasant feeling (sections 1.16–1.19). This week practises only the two arrows.
  • Physical pain is a signal worth listening to. The exercise does not replace seeing a doctor for new, severe or lasting pain.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis included 38 randomised trials of mindfulness meditation for people with chronic pain. With low-quality evidence, mindfulness meditation was associated with a small decrease in pain compared with control groups (30 trials); depression and quality of life also improved. The authors call for larger, more rigorous trials. (Hilton et al. 2017)
  • [Verified] A review of 20 meta-analyses of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), a therapy that teaches making room for unpleasant sensations rather than fighting them, found ACT effective for anxiety, depression and chronic pain, and broadly as effective as cognitive behavioural therapy. (Gloster et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] ACT is guided and uses many other techniques. The idea of "not adding a second arrow" is close to the "acceptance" part of ACT, but no study has measured this exercise on its own.

Limits: these figures are averages; severe or lasting pain needs medical attention.

3. Practice for adults

About 25–30 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages, then read the verse again.
  2. Sit with the breath (10 minutes).
  3. Meet a small discomfort (10 minutes). When a small discomfort comes up while sitting (an itch, a tired back, a noise), stay with it. First arrow: where is the sensation, how strong is it? Second arrow: what is the mind adding ("this is boring", "I'm so bad at sitting", "I can't stand this")? Name it silently: "second arrow", then come back to the actual sensation.
  4. Close (2 minutes). Know the whole body sitting.
  5. Write one pair of arrows each day. Choose a small event from the day (a traffic jam, knocking your knee on the table, an irritating message). Write two lines: "Arrow 1: …" and "Arrow 2: …".

Example: Arrow 1: waiting 15 minutes for the bus in the sun. Arrow 2: "Every day goes wrong, the whole day is ruined."

Do not try to sit still through sharp pain, lasting numbness or chest pain: change position or stop.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The stumble and the words" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The adult tells a story about a rabbit who trips on a stone and hurts its knee a little. Ask the child: "The rabbit’s knee hurts. That is the first hurt."
  2. Go on: the rabbit says, "I'm so clumsy, I can't do anything." Ask: "Do these words make the rabbit feel better or sadder?"
  3. Think together of a kind thing the rabbit could say: "Ouch, I'll rub it a bit and keep going."
  4. Ask if the child has ever felt like the rabbit, but do not push them to tell.

Use only small hurts that are over. Do not tell stories about accidents, serious illness or bullying. Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: Kind to myself (in Vietnamese).

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The exercise makes you blame yourself more ("I added a second arrow again, I'm hopeless"). If so, drop the writing and just sit with the breath.
  • Pain in the body gets stronger, is new, or lasts: see a doctor.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

7. Practice for today

Next time there is a small discomfort, say silently: "This is the first arrow." Then notice the next thing the mind says.
