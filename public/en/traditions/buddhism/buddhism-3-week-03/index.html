Buddhism 3 – Week 3: The cobra and the raft

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

A reminder: this is a Level 3 course. If you are depressed, recently bereaved or having thoughts of self-harm, please leave this course for another time and see Get help now.

This week: read the two well-known similes of this discourse: grabbing a cobra in the wrong place, and the raft for crossing a river. While sitting, practise noticing when the mind "grips" an opinion. During the week, write about one of your own "rafts": a way of doing things that helped you, and whether you are still carrying it when you no longer need it.

Measurable goal: sit for 25 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; write about one "raft" at least 3 times during the week.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The simile of the cobra (Alagaddūpama Sutta) belongs to the Middle Discourses. It opens with the monk Ariṭṭha, who claims that the acts the Buddha calls obstructions do not really obstruct the one who performs them. The Buddha rejects this, then speaks of people who memorise the teaching only to find fault and win debates, without examining its meaning with wisdom.

The discourse goes on: a skilled person first holds the cobra down with a cleft stick, and only then grasps it correctly by the neck; in the same way, someone who learns the teaching and examines its meaning with wisdom finds lasting benefit in it. The Buddha adds that whatever is not understood should be asked of him or of competent monks (section 12.2). Then comes the raft:

Reading it in context

  • The raft does not mean the teaching is unimportant. The person in the simile needs the raft, builds it by hand and paddles hard to cross. Only after crossing do they stop carrying it.
  • "Giving up" does not mean giving up ethics or a decent way of life. This same discourse opens with the Buddha rejecting the idea that harmful acts are no obstruction.
  • "Let alone what is not the teachings" is said in the context of the Buddha’s teaching to his monks. This course does not use the line to discuss other religions or traditions.
  • The cobra simile is about how we hold a teaching: if we learn it to find fault with others or win debates, the teaching turns and harms us. This week’s practice applies that idea to small everyday opinions.
  • A later part of the discourse criticises some views of the self current at the time. This course does not quote that part, since it does not compare traditions.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] The critical review by Bernstein and colleagues (2015) places "cognitive defusion" in the same family of constructs as "decentering", which includes "reduced reactivity to the content of thoughts". This is a conceptual analysis, not a trial of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015)
  • [Verified] A review of 20 meta-analyses of Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) found it effective for anxiety, depression, chronic pain and substance use, and broadly as effective as cognitive behavioural therapy. (Gloster et al. 2020)
  • [Inference] Cognitive defusion is one part of ACT, and is close to this week’s idea of "not gripping". But ACT is a guided therapy; no study has measured the "raft" exercise on its own.

3. Practice for adults

About 35 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (4 minutes). Read the two raft passages.
  2. Sit with the breath (10 minutes).
  3. Notice the "grip" (15 minutes). Watch thoughts pass. When an opinion or a judgement arises ("that person is wrong", "my way is right"), say silently "gripping", and notice whether the body tightens anywhere: jaw, shoulders, belly. Breathe out and soften that place. You do not need to change your opinion; just loosen your hold.
  4. Back to the ground (3 minutes). Open your eyes, feel your feet, name three things you see.
  5. Write about one raft (3–5 minutes, at least 3 times this week). Choose a method, rule or habit that once helped you through a stage of life: a way of studying, a work routine, a spending rule. Write three lines: "It helped me through…", "Does it still help now?", "If not, how do I set it down?"

Example: "When I started work, I checked every email three times; it helped me make fewer mistakes. Now I have much more work, and checking three times makes me slow and anxious. Try checking ordinary emails once, important ones three times."

Use the writing only for everyday ways of doing things. The exercise is not meant to make you re-examine your faith or your moral values.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The raft across the river" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Lay a blue towel on the floor as the "river". With the child, build a small raft from wooden blocks, Lego or ice-lolly sticks.
  2. Put a toy on the raft and push it across the river.
  3. On the other side, ask: "Your friend wants to go and play. Do they need to carry the raft?" Let the child answer.
  4. Leave the raft neatly on the bank and let the toy go off to play. You can say: "The raft helped us cross. Once we’re across, we leave it tidy."

Do not play near real water. Talk only about the raft, not about giving up things the child loves.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • While contemplating, you feel detached from your body, as if watching yourself from outside, or everything becomes empty and strange, and the feeling stays after you stand up. If so, stop the contemplation, do the "back to the ground" step, and talk to someone you trust. If the feeling lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
  • The writing leaves you feeling unsteady, as if you had lost something to lean on. If so, stop the writing; any raft that is still helping you, keep.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before practices longer than 20 minutes.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

If you feel unsteady while sitting, daynentang has a lesson that brings attention back to the body and surroundings: Neo lại (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Next time you want to win a small argument, say silently: "grabbing the cobra by the tail". Then let the other person finish their sentence.
