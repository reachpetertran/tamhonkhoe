Buddhism 1 – Week 4: Non-harm

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read verses 129–130 of the Dhammapada slowly, sit with the breath for 5 minutes, and each morning choose one deliberate act of "non-harm" to carry out during the day.

Measurable goal: one deliberate act of non-harm a day, on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Non-harm (ahiṃsā) is the foundation of Buddhist ethics. The first of the five precepts for lay people is to refrain from killing living beings, animals included. The two verses below open the chapter on the Rod in the Dhammapada. The reason for not harming is put very simply: take yourself as the example.

Reading it in context

  • "The rod" (daṇḍa) stands for punishment and violence in general. [Inference] The verses speak of killing, but the tradition often widens non-harm to cover words and deeds that cause others pain.
  • "Treating others like oneself" is close to the Golden Rule practised in Convergence 0 – Week 3. Here the reason rests on something all beings share: fearing pain and wanting to live.
  • Buddhist traditions differ on vegetarianism. This course does not discuss diet; this week’s exercise involves no change to what you eat.
  • This exercise does not ask you to give up defending yourself or protecting others from violence. If you are being harmed, what needs doing is to find help.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any study that evaluates practising Buddhist "non-harm" as such.
  • [Verified] The closest evidence is on acts of kindness. A 2018 meta-analysis of 27 experiments with 4,045 people found that doing kind acts for others raised the well-being of the person doing them by a small-to-moderate amount (δ = 0.28). The studies were often small: average statistical power was only about 0.42, meaning many studies would struggle to detect an effect even if one existed. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] "Non-harm" is often restraint (not saying the cutting word, not stepping on the ant) rather than doing an extra good deed, so this result is close to, but not the same as, this week’s exercise.

Limits: this exercise is self-observation, not therapy.

3. Practice for adults

About 10–15 minutes a day, plus one small act during the day.

  1. Slow reading (2 minutes). Read the two verses. Pause on the line "Treating others like oneself."
  2. Sit with the breath (5 minutes) as in Week 1.
  3. Choose one act (2 minutes). Write down one act of "non-harm" you will do today. For example: - not criticising when someone at home is slow; - driving more slowly where there are children; - putting an insect outside instead of swatting it, if it is safe; - not posting or sharing a cutting comment; - not making yourself work late tonight.
  4. Do it, then look back (3 minutes in the evening). Write one line: did you do it, how did it feel, was anyone, person or animal, spared some pain?
  5. Tick the day. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "If I were…" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Take turns choosing a small animal: an ant, a cat, a bird.
  2. Ask: "If I were an ant, what would I be afraid of?" (being stepped on, being washed away). "What would I like?" (a path back home).
  3. Think together of one gentle thing to do today: walk round a line of ants, put out water for a cat or dog, never throw stones at birds.
  4. To finish: "Every animal is like us: it is afraid of pain and wants to live."

Use only familiar animals and safe actions; children do not catch insects themselves or go near unknown animals. Do not tell stories of animals being hurt or killed. Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: Secret good deeds (in Vietnamese).

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • The exercise makes you worry too much about harming by accident (fear of stepping on insects, checking again and again). Then drop the choosing step and keep only sitting with the breath.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Next time you are about to say something critical, ask yourself silently: "What if someone said this to me?"
