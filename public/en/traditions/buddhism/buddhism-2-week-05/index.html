Buddhism 2 – Week 5: Mindfulness of mind

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: practise looking at the mind the way you look at the weather: knowing how it is, with no need to change it straight away. Sit for 25 minutes a day; at the end of the day write one line of "mind weather".

Measurable goal: sit for 25 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; write one line of "mind weather" each evening on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The third place in the Discourse on Mindfulness Meditation is the mind (citta). At this step the discourse does not teach fixing the mind, but knowing its state clearly: with or without greed, with or without hate, with or without delusion. Buddhism regards greed, hate and delusion as the three roots of actions that cause suffering.

Reading it in context

  • The discourse lists eight pairs of mind states. This week we read the first four. The other four (expansive or not, supreme or not, immersed in samādhi or not, freed or not) concern deep meditative states; this course does not go into them.
  • "Delusion" (moha) is usually understood as confusion, not seeing clearly. In daily practice it may feel like being foggy or vague, not knowing what you are thinking.
  • "Constricted" (saṅkhitta) and "scattered" (vikkhitta) are read in different ways by commentators; one common reading is a sluggish mind that pulls inward, and a mind that runs outward in all directions.
  • The discourse says "they understand": there is no word of praise or blame. Knowing "the mind has hate" is already mindfulness, not failure.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2025 meta-analysis of 29 randomised trials with 2,535 people looked at mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT). MBCT reduced rumination (repetitive brooding) by a moderate amount (SMD = −0.51), and the effect held at follow-up. (Wei et al. 2025)
  • [Verified] The 2018 critical review points out that most measures of mindfulness are self-report questionnaires, so results are easily influenced by participants’ expectations. (Van Dam et al. 2018)
  • [Inference] MBCT is a guided group programme, usually for people with a history of depression. This week’s self-study exercise resembles only one part of MBCT; we cannot say it has the same effect.

Limits: these figures are averages across many studies; if brooding affects your sleep, eating or work, seek a professional.

3. Practice for adults

About 25–30 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (2 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. Sit with the breath (10 minutes).
  3. Looking at the mind (12 minutes). Now and then, when you notice you have drifted into thought, ask silently: "Is there greed in the mind now? Hate? Fog? Is it constricted or scattered?" Choose the word that fits best, for example "wanting", "irritated", "foggy", "restless" or "calm". Then return to the breath.
  4. See it change (last 2 minutes). Notice whether the state of mind at the end is the same as at the start. This is the "originate and vanish" part of the refrain.
  5. Mind weather (1 minute, in the evening). Write one line: "Today the mind was mostly…, at times…". For example: "Restless in the morning, irritated by traffic in the afternoon, fairly calm tonight."

There is no need to change the state of mind. If a thought keeps coming back, Week 7 teaches ways to work with it.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Weather inside" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Draw four pictures: sun, clouds, rain, wind.
  2. Ask: "What weather is it inside you right now?" The child points to a picture. The adult picks one for themselves too.
  3. Say: "Weather changes. Rain stops." Ask again in the afternoon or evening and see whether it has changed.
  4. Do not ask "why" if the child does not want to say.

If the child picks "rain" for many days in a row, take note and talk with them more, outside the game. Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: Telling the truth, a lighter heart (in Vietnamese).

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Looking at the mind makes you brood or blame yourself more, or your mood stays low for several days.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

7. Practice for today

Right now, ask: "What weather is my mind in?" Name it in one word.
