Buddhism 2 – Week 1: Mindfulness of the body (1) – The breath

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Before you start this course: this is a Level 2 course, with 20–30 minutes of sitting a day for 8 weeks. If you have never meditated, it is better to take Buddhism 1 first. If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

This week: read the opening of the Discourse on Mindfulness Meditation (MN 10), then sit with the breath each day. For the first three days sit for 15 minutes; from day four, sit for 20 minutes.

Measurable goal: sit with the breath for at least 15 minutes on 5 of 7 days, with at least 3 of those days at 20 minutes.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta belongs to the Middle Discourses (Majjhima Nikāya) of the Pali canon. Buddhism regards it as one of the main discourses on meditation. It names four places to set up mindfulness: the body, feelings, the mind and principles. This course goes through them in turn, one part each week.

Reading it in context

  • The Pali word ekāyana in the first sentence is translated in several ways. Bhikkhu Sujato has "path to convergence"; many translations have "the only way" or "the direct path". This course does not choose between them; reviewers from the tradition will comment.
  • The last words of the opening speak of "extinguishment" (nibbāna). [Traditional teaching] In this tradition, the four kinds of mindfulness meditation lead to liberation. This course presents the practice; learners do not need to accept that belief.
  • Sections 4.4–4.7 (the first four steps of breathing) are identical to the passage read in Buddhism 1 – Week 1, so they are not repeated here. The practice below still uses those four steps.
  • Sections 5.1–5.3 are a "refrain": the discourse repeats it after every part. "Internally, externally" is often understood as one’s own body and the bodies of others; commentators read this point in different ways.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2014 systematic review pooled 47 randomised trials with 3,515 participants. Mindfulness meditation programmes (usually around 8 weeks) reduced anxiety, depression and pain by small to moderate amounts, but were not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
  • [Verified] A 2015 meta-analysis of the MBSR programme in healthy adults included 29 studies with 2,668 people. The programme reduced stress, anxiety and depression by a moderate amount (between-group g = 0.53), but the studies varied a lot in design. (Khoury et al. 2015)
  • [Inference] MBSR has a teacher, group sessions and many exercises beyond the breath. This course is self-study and follows the discourse closely, so it cannot be treated as the same as MBSR, and no study has evaluated this course directly.

Limits: these figures are averages across many studies and do not predict what will happen for any one person. Meditation does not replace treatment if you have a mental health problem.

3. Practice for adults

About 20–25 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages. The second time, read only the second passage, slowly and softly aloud.
  2. Sit (1 minute). Sit on a chair or a cushion, back upright but not stiff. Set a timer so you do not need to check the clock.
  3. Long breath, short breath (5 minutes). Like the lathe-turner who knows a long pull from a short one: breathing in, know whether this breath is long or short; the same breathing out. Do not adjust the breath.
  4. Feel the whole body (5 minutes). Breathing in, feel the whole body sitting; breathing out, let the body settle a little.
  5. Rise and fall (the rest of the time). Notice each breath beginning, then ending; the next one begins again. This is a simple way to practise "originate and vanish" from the refrain.
  6. When the mind wanders. Say "thinking" silently, then come back to the breath without blaming yourself.
  7. Tick the day. Write down how many minutes you sat each day.

If attending to the breath makes you anxious, move your attention to your feet on the floor or open your eyes and look down at the floor.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Five fingers, five breaths" (5 minutes, with an adult)

  1. The child spreads one hand. The index finger of the other hand rests at the base of the thumb.
  2. Breathing in, the finger climbs up the thumb; breathing out, it slides down the other side. Go through all five fingers.
  3. The adult does it too, without talking while "climbing the fingers".
  4. Ask: "Which breath was the longest? Which was the shortest?" There is no right or wrong answer.

If the child does not want to, stop; do not push.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Attending to the breath makes you short of breath, gives you a racing heart or dizziness, and this does not ease when you move to your feet or open your eyes.
  • If so, stop and see Get help now.
  • If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

Short 1–3 minute breathing for stressful moments has its home on daynentang: Naming feelings, breathing slowly (in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Once today, while you are waiting (a red light, a kettle, a computer starting up), know three breaths: long or short.
