Buddhism 4 – Week 2: A good lay life

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read the Buddha's answer to a lay person who asked how to live "for welfare and happiness in this life and lives to come". Each morning, choose one task in your work and see it through. During the week, meet one good friend.

Measurable goal: finish the task chosen in the morning on at least 5 of 7 days; have at least one conversation with a good friend and note one thing you learned.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Dīghajāṇu, of the Koliyan clan, tells the Buddha that he is a lay person living with his family, using perfumes and accepting gold, and asks for a teaching that fits that life. The Buddha gives four things for this life (initiative, protection, good friendship, balanced finances) and four for lives to come (faith, ethics, generosity, wisdom). This week reads the first and third; the second and fourth are kept for Week 5.

Reading it in context

  • "Byagghapajja" is the name the Buddha uses for Dīghajāṇu in this discourse. "Gentleman" (kulaputta) is the discourse's word for a decent lay person; the English follows the original's male wording, but the teaching applies to everyone.
  • The list of trades belongs to ancient Indian society ("archery" means soldiering). The main idea is: earn an honest living, and know how to organise the work so it gets done.
  • "Accomplished in faith" in the discourse means faith in the Buddha. This course does not read the passage as telling you to avoid people of other religions; what it keeps is staying close to people who live ethically, generously and wisely, and learning from them.
  • "The path to heaven" belongs to the Buddhist belief in lives to come. Learners do not need to accept that belief to practise the rest.
  • The DN 31 passage comes after a section on "fake friends" (the taker, the talker, the flatterer, the spender). The course does not ask you to cut anyone off; the exercise is only to notice who you are close to and what you learn.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any peer-reviewed review that evaluates practice based on the discourse with Dīghajāṇu.
  • [Inference] This week's exercise rests on an ancient text and on practitioners' experience. We give no scientific figures for this week so as not to borrow results from other studies.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. One task seen through (2 minutes in the morning). Choose one task at work or at home that you tend to leave half done. Write one line: "Today I will finish… by…". Keep it small enough to finish in a day.
  3. Evening check (2 minutes). Done, partly done, or not yet. If not yet, note one reason. DN 31 has a familiar list of reasons: "too cold, too hot, too late, too early, too hungry, too full". Is your reason on it?
  4. One good friend (during the week). Think of someone who fits one of the four grounds in the DN 31 passage. Meet or call them and ask them one honest question about something you are unsure of. Note one thing you learned.
  5. Ask yourself (end of the week, 3 minutes). For one of your own friends, which of those four things have you done?

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "What does a good friend do?" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Ask: "Has a friend ever helped you?" The child tells the story.
  2. Together, think of three things good friends do: invite you to play when you're alone, remind you when you do something wrong, show you how to do your homework.
  3. The child picks one to do for a classmate this week.
  4. At the end of the week, the child tells how it went: how the friend reacted, how the child felt.

If the child talks about being bullied, stop the game and listen to them first.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • "One task seen through" turns into pressure to do more and more, to the point of exhaustion. The discourse speaks of knowing how to get the work done, not of working without food or sleep.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Write down the small task you have left unfinished the longest. Spend 10 minutes on it.
