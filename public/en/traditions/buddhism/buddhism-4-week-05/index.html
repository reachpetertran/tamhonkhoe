Buddhism 4 – Week 5: Money and giving

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read three passages about money: knowing income and spending, dividing wealth, and generosity. Track your own income and spending for seven days, only to see it clearly, not to criticise yourself. During the week, give once, with money, things or time.

Measurable goal: track income and spending on at least 6 of 7 days; give at least once this week and note how it felt.

Note: this lesson describes an ancient teaching; it is not financial advice. For debt or big money decisions, ask a qualified person.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In Week 2, Dīghajāṇu was taught four things for this life. This week reads the fourth, balanced finances. The second, "protection", means looking after honestly earned wealth so it is not taken by rulers or bandits, burned by fire, swept away by flood, or taken by unloved heirs (AN 8.54, section 3.4). The discourse also names four drains on wealth: womanizing, drinking, gambling and bad friends (section 6.2).

Reading it in context

  • "Eating wealth like a fig-eater" is an ancient image. We have not checked how the traditional commentary explains it; the general sense of the passage is still clear: spending beyond a small income.
  • Dividing wealth in four is an image of its time, not a formula. The idea kept here: a part to live on, a part for work, and a part saved for hard times.
  • The discourse faults both extremes: extravagance, and frugality to the point of hurting yourself. Tracking this week is for seeing clearly, not for cutting back until you go without.
  • "Loving to give" in the discourse is a quality of heart, not a fee to pay. This course asks for no money and recommends no organisation. If money is tight for you, give time or effort instead.
  • If alcohol, gambling or debt is harming you or your family, that is not something a week of practice can fix; find someone who can help.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2008 paper in three parts: a survey (people who spent more on others were happier), a follow-up of people receiving a bonus, and a randomised experiment: people assigned to spend money on others felt happier than those assigned to spend it on themselves. The survey part is a correlation; this is one research group's result. (Dunn, Aknin & Norton 2008)
  • [Verified] A 2018 meta-analysis (27 experiments, 4,045 people): doing acts of kindness for others raises the doer's well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
  • We have not found any study that evaluates tracking spending or dividing wealth in four as the discourse describes.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. Track income and spending (2–3 minutes each evening). Write down the day's income and spending in a notebook or on your phone. Just record it; no "right" or "wrong".
  3. End-of-week review (10 minutes). Sort your spending into four groups: living; work and learning; saving; giving. Ask: which way do I lean, extravagant or too frugal? Is there one small item I want to change next week?
  4. Give once (during the week). Give something within your means: a small sum, a good item you no longer need, or an hour of help. Afterwards write one line: how did it feel to give?

If tracking makes you too anxious, record only spending and skip the review.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "Three jars" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Set out three jars or cups labelled "Use", "Save", "Share". Use beans, buttons or counting sticks instead of money.
  2. Give the child ten beans. The child divides them among the jars. There is no right or wrong way.
  3. Ask: "Who would you like the 'Share' jar to be for?" The child can choose something real: giving away an old toy, helping grandparents with a task.
  4. Play again next week and see if the child wants to divide them differently.

Don't use the game to talk about adults' money worries.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Tracking money leaves you anxious for long periods or losing sleep over money. Then stop tracking and talk to someone you trust or a qualified person.
  • You feel you must give until you or your family go without. The discourse also faults starving yourself.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

7. Practice for today

Write down everything you have spent since this morning.
