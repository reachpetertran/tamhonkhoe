Buddhism 4 – Week 3: Reflecting on our actions

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read the Buddha's teaching to his son, Rāhula, on checking our actions as we check ourselves in a mirror. Each day, choose one action (a message, a decision, something you say to someone at home) and check it three times: before, during and after.

Measurable goal: check one action three times each day and note it, on at least 5 of 7 days; note at least 2 good things you did and felt glad about.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In this discourse the Buddha visits Rāhula, his son, who has become a monk. He leaves a little water in a pot, throws it out, turns the pot upside down, then right side up, each time comparing it to someone who is not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie. Then he asks Rāhula what a mirror is for, and teaches him how to check deeds of body, speech and mind.

Reading it in context

  • The discourse checks at three moments: before acting, while acting (section 10, not quoted) and after acting. Then it repeats this for speech and for thought.
  • There is only one question: does this lead to hurting myself, hurting others, or both? The discourse does not ask you to check what others think of you.
  • When you see you have done wrong, the discourse says to tell a trustworthy person and restrain yourself in future. Today that could be a friend, a family member, or the person affected, if it is safe to speak to them.
  • For wrong thoughts, the discourse uses strong words: one should be horrified, repelled and disgusted by that deed (section 17.6). This course does not practise self-disgust or self-punishment; what it keeps is noticing and restraining yourself in future.
  • The second half of each check matters too: when an action was good, the discourse says to be glad about it. The exercise is not only for finding faults.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any study that directly evaluates a reflection exercise based on MN 61.
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 146 randomised studies of writing about experiences and feelings: a positive but very small overall effect (r = 0.075). (Frattaroli 2006)
  • [Inference] The evening note is close to the "writing" studied above, but with a different aim: reviewing actions, not releasing feelings. The result above does not prove this exercise works; if there is a benefit, it is likely to be small too.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). Read the three passages.
  2. Choose the day's action (1 minute in the morning). Something specific: a difficult email, a talk with your child, a decision at work.
  3. Check before. Just before acting, ask: "Does this hurt me, others, or both?" If it does, change how you do it or put it off.
  4. Check during. Halfway through, pause for one breath and ask again. If you see it is causing harm, stop.
  5. Check after (in the evening, 5 minutes). Write one line. If you see it caused harm: write one thing you will do to put it right, or tell someone you trust. If it was good: write "I'm glad that…".
  6. Also this week: notice your jokes. Was any of them untrue?

If you are not sure whether something was harmful, just write "not sure". You do not need a firm answer every time.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The little mirror" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Give the child a small mirror. Ask: "What is a mirror for?" (To see if there's something on your face.)
  2. The adult describes a situation: "A wants to take her little brother's toy without asking." The child holds up the mirror and asks: "Would this make anyone sad? Me, my friend, or both?"
  3. Together, find another way to do it.
  4. Swap roles: the child describes a situation and the adult holds the mirror.

Use only small situations, not ones that have made the child feel ashamed.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
  • Checking sets off a stream of self-blame. Then just write "next time I will…", with no need to score your mistakes.
  • Checking turns into checking again and again without end, or the fear that "I have hurt someone" keeps coming back without evidence. Then stop the exercise and talk to a doctor or psychologist.
  • If there are serious warning signs, stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

For a family way to practise owning up to and fixing mistakes, daynentang has a lesson: Nhận lỗi và sửa lỗi (Owning up and putting things right; in Vietnamese).

7. Practice for today

Before sending your next message, read it once more and ask: could this hurt anyone?
