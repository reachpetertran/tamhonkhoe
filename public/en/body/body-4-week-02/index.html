Body 4 – Week 2: Yom Kippur

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

A reminder: this course only describes; it does not teach fasting. Each week's practice has nothing to do with food.

This week: read the Torah passage that sets the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) and the passage from Isaiah about "the fast that I have chosen". Practice: notice one small wrong you did to someone and put it right.

Measurable goal: read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; write three things the Torah passage says about this day; complete one act of putting things right (an apology, returning something, making up for it) during the week.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement, falls on the tenth day of the seventh month of the Jewish calendar. The Torah passage below comes at the end of Leviticus 16, after the description of the high priest's rites.

Judaism: a day of affliction and complete rest

A note on context: the passage does not use the word "fast". [Expert judgement] Rabbinic tradition understands "afflict your souls" as refraining from food and drink and from several other things for about 25 hours, from just before sunset to nightfall the next day. The Jewish reviewer will say more.

Judaism: the fast that is chosen

A note on context: in the verses before (58:3–5), the prophet rebukes people who fast while exploiting their workers, quarrelling and fighting. [Expert judgement] Isaiah 58 is usually read in the Yom Kippur morning service. The Jewish reviewer will confirm.

What the text says, read closely:

  • Time: one fixed day each year, the tenth day of the seventh month.
  • What is required: to "afflict your souls" and do no work, as on a sabbath.
  • Purpose: atonement, to be "clean before the LORD".
  • Isaiah adds: fasting means nothing if it goes with injustice; the chosen fast is freeing the oppressed and sharing bread with the hungry.

[Expert judgement] In Jewish tradition: children do not fast the full day; a sick person for whom fasting could be dangerous must eat and drink, because saving life overrides almost all commandments. Tradition also teaches that Yom Kippur does not atone for wrongs between people until one has made peace with the person wronged. The Jewish reviewer will confirm and give the sources.

Where the traditions differ

  • Ramadan is a month of fasting from dawn to sunset; Yom Kippur is one day a year, a continuous fast of about 25 hours.
  • Ramadan is tied to the revelation of the Qur'an and to thankfulness; Yom Kippur is tied to atonement and repentance (teshuvah).
  • Both Qur'an 2:184 and Isaiah 58:7 place feeding the hungry next to fasting, but in different frames: in one it is a way to make up for not being able to fast, in the other a rebuke to fasting without justice.

2. What science finds

  • We have not found any review on Yom Kippur and mental health.
  • [Verified] The closest to this week's practice: a meta-analysis of 54 reports with 2,323 people on therapeutic programmes that promote forgiveness found that they helped people forgive more than no intervention. This research is about forgiving someone who hurt you, not about apologising to someone you hurt. (Wade et al. 2014)
  • [Inference] The benefit of taking the first step to put right a wrong you did has not been measured in the studies we have read.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day. No fasting.

  1. Slow reading (5 minutes). Read Leviticus on odd days and Isaiah on even days.
  2. Look back (days 1–2, 5 minutes). Write down one small, recent wrong you did to someone: harsh words, a forgotten promise, something borrowed and not returned. Choose something small; not something big, and not something that still hurts.
  3. Put it right (during the week). One concrete act: a short apology without long explanations; returning something; making up for it. If the other person does not accept the apology, you have still done your part.
  4. Write one line (day 7). How did putting it right feel?

Do not use this exercise to contact someone who harmed you, or to make yourself apologise within a violent or controlling relationship. The lesson on forgiveness is in Convergence 1, Week 8.

4. Children's corner (6+)

Children do not fast in this course.

Game: "Owning and fixing mistakes" (5–10 minutes, with an adult): do the activity Owning and fixing mistakes on daynentang (in Vietnamese). The adult goes first: tell one small mistake you made this week and how you put it right.

Do not make your child apologise in front of others, and do not punish your child for owning up.

5. Stop practising if

  • Looking back at your mistakes makes you blame yourself a lot, lose sleep, or feel worthless. Stop this exercise; talk to someone you trust or a professional.
  • Memories of being harmed by someone flood back. Stop and find support.
  • Reading about fasting makes you want to eat less, skip meals, or feel guilty about eating. Stop the course and talk to a doctor.
  • You feel panic, have traumatic flashbacks, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

Families with young children: Owning and fixing mistakes (in Vietnamese). If you need support right now: Get help now.

7. Today's practice

Read Isaiah 58:6–7 and write down one small thing you need to put right with someone.
