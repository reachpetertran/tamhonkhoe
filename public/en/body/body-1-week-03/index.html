Body 1 – Week 3: A screen-free meal

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read three passages about shared meals. Have at least three meals this week with no phone, television or computer on the table. If you eat with others, use the time to talk.

Measurable goal: at least 3 screen-free meals this week; note for each who you ate with and what you talked about.

1. Traditional teaching

Judaism: a table with good words

[Traditional teaching] Pirkei Avot (Ethics of the Fathers) records a saying of Rabbi Simeon about people who eat together.

A note on context: Gorfinkle numbers this saying III.4; many other editions number it 3:3. The first half of the saying describes the opposite: a meal with no words of Torah is compared to something very harsh; this lesson quotes only the second half. "Words of Torah" means learning about God's teaching. [Inference] For readers who are not Jewish, the idea one might borrow is that a shared meal is a time for words that matter. The Jewish reviewer will say more.

Christianity: taking food together with gladness

A note on context: this passage describes the first Christian community in Jerusalem. "Breaking bread" may refer to ordinary meals or to the Lord's Supper; churches understand it differently and this lesson does not discuss it.

Judaism and Christianity: eat your bread with joy

A note on context: Ecclesiastes (Kohelet) belongs to the Scriptures of both Judaism and Christianity. This verse sits in a passage reflecting on death and the shortness of life: because life is short, enjoy simple things. Wine was an everyday drink at the time; this lesson does not encourage drinking alcohol.

Where the traditions differ

  • Pirkei Avot places the value of the meal in the words of Torah spoken there; Acts in the unity of the community and its praise; Ecclesiastes in simple joy within a short life.
  • This lesson borrows only the shared idea that a shared meal is a time to be present to one another.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2013 meta-analysis (24 experimental studies): eating while distracted led people to eat moderately more in that meal and more at a later meal. (Robinson et al. 2013)
  • [Verified] A 2011 meta-analysis (17 studies, 182,836 children and adolescents): children who ate with their family 3 or more times a week had healthier eating patterns and less disordered eating. This is a correlation: families who often eat together may differ in many other ways. (Hammons & Fiese 2011)
  • We have not found any review on screen-free meals in adults and well-being.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes for each screen-free meal.

  1. Slow reading (2 minutes). Read one passage a day, taking the three in turn.
  2. Choose three meals this week and write them in your calendar in advance.
  3. Before the meal: leave your phone in another room or face down on silent; turn off the television.
  4. Pause for one minute as in Week 1.
  5. During the meal: if you eat with others, ask one question and listen to the whole answer. For example: "What was good about today?", "Who does this dish remind you of?" If you eat alone, eat slowly as in Week 2.
  6. After the meal: write one line: who you ate with, what you talked about.

You do not need to make the whole family join in. You can start with your own meal and invite others when they want to.

4. Kids' corner 6+

Game: "The dinner question jar" (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. With your child, write or draw 6–8 questions on slips of paper, fold them and put them in a jar. For example: "What made you laugh today?", "Who would you like to thank?", "Which animal do you like most?"
  2. At a screen-free meal, everyone draws a question and answers it.
  3. The adult answers first as an example. Anyone who does not want to answer can swap their question.

Do not use mealtimes to scold the child or talk about school marks, and do not mention weight or how much anyone eats.

5. Stop practising if

  • Shared meals often involve arguments, tension or violence. Your safety then matters more than the exercise; look for help, see Get help now.
  • The exercise makes you preoccupied with how much you eat, makes you want to eat less, or to skip meals. Stop this course and talk to a doctor.
  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself. Stop practising and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

Families with young children can add Walking slowly, eating slowly (in Vietnamese) at one screen-free meal.

7. Practice for today

Choose three screen-free meals for this week and write them in your calendar.
