Body 1 – Week 4: Thanking growers and cooks

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

This week: read two passages about the path of food from the earth to the table. Draw the "path" of one dish in your meal, then say one specific thank-you to someone who helped make the meal. At the end of the week, look back over all four weeks.

Measurable goal: at least one specific thank-you, in person or by message, to someone who made your food (the cook, the seller, the grower…); rate the four habits of the course and choose at least one to keep.

1. Traditional teaching

Islam: let man consider his food

[Traditional teaching] Chapter 80 of the Qur'an invites people to reflect on their daily food: water, earth, seed, plants and fruit.

A note on context: this is a translation of the meaning; Muslims generally hold that only the Arabic text is the Qur'an. In this passage the rain and the harvest are presented as gifts of Allah; the gratitude is directed to Him.

Judaism: so much effort for one loaf

[Traditional teaching] The Talmud tells that Ben Zoma, on seeing a crowd, blessed God "Who created all these to serve me", and then explained as follows.

A note on context: the words "Explaining his custom", "man", "in the wind", "the grain from the chaff", "the grain into flour", "only" and "on the other hand" are explanations added in the Steinsaltz edition, not the words of the Talmud. Ben Zoma goes on to say the same about a garment. [Inference] "To serve me" is not a boast: Ben Zoma recognises that he lives from the work of a great many people. Because this page quotes a CC BY-NC source, the whole page is licensed CC BY-NC 4.0.

Where the traditions differ

  • The Qur'an passage directs gratitude to Allah, who sends the rain and the harvest. The Talmud passage opens with a blessing of God but dwells on human labour.
  • [Expert judgement] Other traditions have similar ideas; in Buddhism, for example, monastics receive their food as alms from others. This lesson does not quote a passage because we have no source with a confirmed licence for it yet.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2021 meta-analysis (27 studies, 3,675 people): gratitude exercises reduced symptoms of depression and anxiety by a small amount, and by less when compared with control groups that also did an activity. (Cregg & Cheavens 2021)
  • We have not found any study that evaluates thinking about, or thanking, the people who produce our food as such.

3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes a day, plus 15 minutes of review at the end of the week.

  1. Slow reading (3 minutes). On odd days read the Qur'an, on even days the Talmud.
  2. Draw the path of one dish (days 1–3, 10 minutes). Choose one dish in your meal, for example a bowl of rice. Draw or write the steps that brought it to the table: who grew it, harvested it, transported it, sold it, cooked it. You do not need names; it is enough to see that they were there.
  3. One specific thank-you (days 4–6). Say it to a real person: the cook at home, the vegetable seller, the canteen staff. Say exactly what you are thankful for: "Thank you, the vegetables are lovely and fresh today."
  4. Review (day 7, 15 minutes). Rate the four habits of the course from 0 to 3 (0 = not at all, 3 = regularly): a one-minute pause before a meal; one slow meal; screen-free meals; thanking the people who make the food. Choose one or two habits to keep for the next four weeks and write them in your calendar.

4. Kids' corner 6+

daynentang already has the activity Thanking working people (in Vietnamese). This week, do that activity and add a small game:

Game: "Where did the rice come from?" (10 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Take a handful of rice or a bowl of cooked rice. Ask: "Where did this rice come from?"
  2. Draw a path together: field → farmer → truck → market → kitchen → bowl.
  3. The child chooses one person on the path to thank, says one sentence before the meal, or draws a picture for the cook at home.

Do not make the child finish the plate "so the farmer's work is not wasted"; gratitude is not pressure to eat.

5. Stop practising if

  • The exercise makes you feel guilty about eating, or preoccupied with how much you eat, makes you want to eat less or skip meals. Stop this course and talk to a doctor.
  • You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself. Stop practising and see Get help now.

6. Foundation skills on daynentang

The activity Thanking working people (in Vietnamese) is for families with children aged 2–8.

7. Practice for today

Look at your next meal and silently name three people who helped make it.
