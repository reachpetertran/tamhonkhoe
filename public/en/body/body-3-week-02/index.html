Body 3 – Week 2: Plant-based eating as a discipline

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet reviewed by a dietitian

A reminder: this course is only for healthy adults who have completed the self-check in Week 1. Pregnant or breastfeeding women, children and teenagers should change to plant-based eating only under the care of a doctor or dietitian.

This week: read three passages about plant-based eating. Choose a manageable level, for example one plant-based meal a day, and keep it for 7 days. No calorie counting, and weight loss is not the aim.

Measurable goal: keep your chosen level on at least 5 of 7 days.

1. Traditional teaching

Judaism and Christianity: the first food was plants

[Traditional teaching] In the creation account in chapter 1 of Genesis, God gives human beings plants for food.

A note on context: after the Flood, in Genesis 9:3, God allows human beings to eat animals as well. So reading 1:29 as a command to be vegetarian does not match the text. [Inference] Many readers take the passage as a picture of an original world without killing. Reviewers from both traditions will say how it is usually read.

Judaism and Christianity: Daniel asks for pulse

[Traditional teaching] The Book of Daniel tells how Daniel and three friends were taken to the Babylonian court. They did not want to eat the king's food, so they asked the steward to put them to a test.

A note on context: verse 8 says Daniel did not want to "defile himself" with the king's food and wine. [Inference] The reason is usually explained as the dietary laws and avoiding food offered to idols, not health or vegetarianism. The story is a narrative of faith, not a scientific experiment.

Stoicism: a year without meat

[Traditional teaching] In Letter 108, Seneca recalls studying in his youth with Sotion, who explained why Pythagoras and Sextius abstained from animal food.

A note on context: Pythagoras had a different reason from Sextius: he believed that souls pass from one body to another. Seneca later went back to eating meat at his father's request, because at the time abstaining from certain meats was seen as a sign of following a foreign cult. This lesson leaves out the passage on Pythagoras's teaching about souls.

Where the traditions differ

  • Judaism has dietary laws (kashrut) that set out what may be eaten; eating meat is not forbidden. Most Christian churches have no binding food laws; some keep days without meat. Islam allows halal meat.
  • [Expert judgement] In Buddhism, the Mahayana traditions of Vietnam, China and Korea value vegetarian eating. The Pāli discourses tell that the Buddha allowed monastics to accept meat when they had not seen, heard or suspected that the animal was killed for them (MN 55, Sujato's translation). Many Hindu traditions and Jainism are vegetarian.
  • Seneca tried vegetarian eating for ethical reasons and for restraint, then gave it up for social reasons. He says plainly that he is not sure his mind really was more active.
  • This course does not claim that plant-based eating is morally or religiously better. It is only an optional discipline.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2023 meta-analysis of 30 randomised trials: compared with an omnivorous diet, vegetarian or vegan diets lowered total cholesterol (−0.34 mmol/L), LDL cholesterol (−0.30 mmol/L) and apolipoprotein B; there was no difference in triglycerides. (Koch et al. 2023)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 13 observational studies with 17,809 people: mixed results on vegetarian diets and mood (slightly lower anxiety, higher depression). This is a CORRELATION; it may reflect reverse causation or other factors. Science has not shown that a vegetarian diet improves mood. (Iguacel et al. 2021)
  • [Verified] The US National Institutes of Health Office of Dietary Supplements (NIH ODS): plant foods do not naturally contain vitamin B12; vegans and some vegetarians are at higher risk of B12 deficiency; supplements and fortified foods can substantially reduce the risk. (NIH ODS, updated 26 March 2024)
  • Limits: the trials measured blood fats, not mental health, and the reductions are group averages. One plant-based meal a day is very different from a fully vegan diet.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day for reading and planning, plus the meals.

  1. Slow reading (2 minutes). Each day, read one of the three passages.
  2. Choose a level (day 1). Choose the lightest level you are sure you can keep: (a) one plant-based meal a day; (b) plant-based all day on 1–2 fixed days a week; (c) plant-based on weekdays, eating as usual at weekends. You may keep eggs and dairy if you wish.
  3. Make sure of protein. Each plant-based meal has a protein dish: tofu, beans and lentils, soya, peanuts, sesame, or eggs and dairy if you eat them. A plant-based meal is still a full meal; do not cut portions.
  4. If you plan to be fully vegan for a long time: ask a doctor or dietitian about vitamin B12 and other nutrients. This lesson gives no doses.
  5. Write one line each evening: did I keep my chosen level today; how did my body feel (hungry, full, tired, well)?
  6. Eat with your family. Do not make others eat as you do. If the family shares one table, just add one plant-based dish for yourself.

4. Children's corner (6+)

Children do not follow this discipline. Your child eats the family's usual meals. If the family wants a child to be fully vegetarian, ask a doctor or a paediatric dietitian.

Game: "A rainbow on the plate" (5 minutes, with an adult)

  1. During a meal, the family looks for the colours of vegetables and fruit on the table: green, red, yellow, purple, white.
  2. Each person names one dish and its colour.
  3. Anyone who wants to can taste one bite of something they rarely eat.

Do not make your child finish the plate, do not sort foods into "good" and "bad", and do not use food as reward or punishment.

5. Stop practising if

  • You feel tired for a long time, dizzy, pale, or have numbness in your hands or feet. See a doctor and say that you are changing how you eat.
  • Choosing food makes you preoccupied, guilty about eating something outside your plan, or wanting to eat less. Stop the course and talk to a doctor.
  • The discipline causes lasting tension at home or at work. Go back to a lighter level or stop.
  • You feel panic, have trauma flashbacks, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see Get help now.
