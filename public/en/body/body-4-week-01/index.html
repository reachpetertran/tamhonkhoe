Body 4 – Week 1: Ramadan

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet expert-reviewed

Read before you start this course: this course only describes how four traditions fast (Ramadan, Yom Kippur, Lent and the Buddhist eight precepts). It does not teach fasting and does not invite anyone to fast. Each week has a small practice that has nothing to do with food. If you fast as part of your own faith, ask your teacher or religious leader about the rules, and your doctor about your health. Anyone with a current or past eating disorder should talk to a doctor before reading this course.

This week: read three verses of the Qur'an about fasting in the month of Ramadan, see what the text says about who is exempt, and do one act of giving.

Measurable goal: read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; write in your own words three things the passage says (the purpose, the time, who may fast other days or feed someone instead); do one act of giving during the week.

1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In Islam, fasting in Ramadan (sawm) is one of the five pillars. The three verses below are in chapter 2 (al-Baqarah).

Islam: fasting so that you may ward off evil

A note on context: this is Pickthall's translation of the meaning; Muslims generally hold that only the Arabic text is the Qur'an.

What the text says, read closely:

  • Purpose: "that ye may ward off (evil)", to magnify Allah and to be thankful (verses 183, 185). The month of Ramadan is tied to the revelation of the Qur'an.
  • Those who fast other days: the sick and those on a journey make up the days later (verses 184, 185).
  • Those who feed someone instead: verse 184 speaks of a ransom, "the feeding of a man in need". Translations and commentators understand who this applies to in different ways; the Vietnamese translation on this site reads it as those for whom fasting is very hard because of old age or frailty. The Muslim reviewer will say how it is usually understood.
  • The spirit: "Allah desireth for you ease; He desireth not hardship for you" (verse 185).

[Expert judgement] In common practice, adult Muslims in good health refrain from food and drink from dawn to sunset throughout Ramadan; the month follows the lunar calendar, so it comes about ten days earlier each solar year. Many Muslim scholars also exempt pregnant and breastfeeding women when there is concern for health, and children who have not reached maturity. These details are not in the three verses above; the Muslim reviewer will confirm them.

Where the traditions differ

  • Verse 183 says fasting "was prescribed for those before you". The following weeks read how Judaism, Christianity and Buddhism fast. Each tradition has its own calendar, rules and purpose; the course does not treat them as one.
  • In Islam, fasting in Ramadan is a duty for adults who are able. In the coming weeks you will see a tradition where fasting is a commandment for one day a year, one where each church sets its own rules, and one where lay people choose their own days to keep extra precepts.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A systematic review of 22 papers on Ramadan and mental health: effects on mental well-being were mixed and usually minor, both positive and negative. Exceptions: people with schizophrenia and metabolic syndrome, and people with bipolar disorder, who had a substantial increase in relapses. The author concludes that Ramadan fasting is safe in most conditions, but caution is needed in schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. (Heun 2018)
  • [Verified] A meta-analysis of 11 studies with 1,436 people on fasting interventions in general (not only Ramadan): in randomised trials at low risk of bias, fasting groups had lower anxiety and depression scores than controls. The authors call the results preliminary; no study was carried out in people with psychiatric conditions. (Berthelot et al. 2021)
  • Limits: studies of Ramadan can hardly have a true control group, because the month also involves prayer, community life and changes in sleep. Science cannot measure the religious meaning of fasting.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day. No fasting.

  1. Slow reading (5 minutes). Days 1–2 read verse 183; days 3–4 verse 184; days 5–6 verse 185; day 7 all three again.
  2. Write three lines (day 7). In your own words: what the passage says fasting is for; for how long; who may fast other days or feed someone instead.
  3. One act of giving (once this week). Verse 184 speaks of feeding a person in need. Choose something within reach: pay for a meal at a charity kitchen, buy a colleague lunch, take food to an elderly neighbour. This is your own act, not a Muslim ritual.
  4. If you have friends or colleagues keeping Ramadan (optional): ask what they would like you to respect, for example meeting times or shared meals. Listen; do not debate their faith.

4. Children's corner (6+)

Children do not fast in this course. Your child eats all meals as usual.

Game: "Moon diary" (5 minutes each evening, with an adult)

  1. Explain: "Some of our Muslim friends begin a special month when they see the new moon. In that month they pray more and share food with poor people."
  2. Every evening for 7 days, look at the sky with your child (or at a photo if it is cloudy) and draw the shape of the moon in a calendar box.
  3. At the end of the week, ask: "How did the moon change?"

Do not present fasting as something a child should try. Do not praise or criticise anyone's faith.

5. Stop practising if

  • Reading about fasting makes you want to eat less, skip meals, or worry a lot about weight or body shape. Stop the course and talk to a doctor.
  • You fast as part of your faith and have a chronic illness, diabetes treated with medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have a history of schizophrenia or bipolar disorder. Talk to your doctor before Ramadan; this course does not replace medical advice or the guidance of your religious leaders.
  • You feel panic, have traumatic flashbacks, cannot sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see Get help now.
