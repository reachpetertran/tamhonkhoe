Body 3 – Week 1: Who should not do this

Published after review by the project owner. No reviewer from within a tradition has checked this page yet.

Not yet trial-taught Not yet reviewed by a dietitian

Read this first: this course is for healthy adults only. It is an optional discipline, not medical advice and not a weight-loss diet. No dietitian or doctor has reviewed it yet. If you belong to any group in the list below, do not do this course.

This week: change nothing in how you eat. Read two passages on moderation, complete the self-check, and note your meal times for 3 days.

Measurable goal: complete the self-check; note meal times for 3 full days; if you are in the "ask a doctor first" group, have asked a doctor before moving on to Week 2.

Who should not do this course

Do not do the 16:8 eating window (Week 3), and change to plant-based eating only under the care of a doctor or dietitian, if you:

  • are pregnant or breastfeeding;
  • are a child or teenager (under 18);
  • have diabetes and take medication, especially insulin or a sulfonylurea, because skipping meals can cause low blood sugar;
  • have or have had an eating disorder, or often worry about your weight, body shape or eating.

[Expert judgement] Ask a doctor first if you: are underweight; take medication that must be taken with food or at fixed times; are older, frail or prone to falls; are being treated for a chronic illness (heart, kidney, liver, blood pressure, stomach); have fainted or had low blood sugar before; do heavy physical work, drive long distances or work night shifts.

1. Traditional teaching

Hinduism: not fasting too much, not feasting too much

[Traditional teaching] In chapter 6 of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna teaches Arjuna about meditation, then says that this path asks for moderation in eating, sleeping and work.

A note on context: Edwin Arnold translates the Gita as verse and does not number the verses; the passage above corresponds to verses 16–17 of chapter 6. [Inference] The teaching is about the conditions for meditation, not a diet. A reviewer from this tradition will say how it is usually read.

Buddhism: health is the ultimate blessing

[Traditional teaching] The Dhammapada, in the chapter on happiness, lists health and contentment among the most precious things.

A note on context: "extinguishment" translates nibbāna. This lesson uses only the first two lines: an eating discipline only makes sense if it protects health rather than harms it.

Where the traditions differ

  • The Gita makes moderation in eating and sleeping a condition of the path of yoga and devotion. The Dhammapada places health beside contentment and nibbāna in a verse about happiness.
  • Neither passage speaks of time-restricted eating or plant-based eating. This lesson borrows only the shared idea: avoid extremes and put health first.
  • Religious fasting (Ramadan, Yom Kippur, Lent, the Buddhist eight precepts) is a different matter, with each tradition's own rules and exemptions. This course does not teach those practices.

2. What science finds

  • [Verified] A 2025 network meta-analysis of 99 randomised trials with 6,582 adults (most with conditions such as overweight or obesity): all types of intermittent fasting and of daily calorie restriction led to more weight loss than eating freely. Compared with daily calorie restriction, only alternate-day fasting gave about 1.3 kg more; time-restricted eating (such as 16:8) did not. There was no difference in HbA1c. In trials of 24 weeks or longer, a benefit was seen only against eating freely. (Semnani-Azad et al. 2025)
  • Limits: the trials mostly measured weight and heart and metabolic markers in people with overweight or illness. They did not measure inner life, and say very little about healthy adults of normal weight.
  • What this means: on current evidence, 16:8 is not magic. This course presents 16:8 as an optional discipline and promises no health benefit.

3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day, with no change in how you eat.

  1. Slow reading (2 minutes). Read the Gita on odd days and the Dhammapada on even days.
  2. Self-check (once, day 1). Reread the two lists at the top. Does anything apply to you? If something in the first list does, stop the course here. If something in the second list does, book a doctor's appointment and do not move on to Week 2 yet.
  3. Ask yourself why (once, day 1). Write one sentence: "I want to do this because…". If the answer is mainly fear of gaining weight, dislike of your body, or guilt after eating, this course is not right for you now. Talk to a doctor or someone you trust.
  4. Note meal times (3 days, days 2–4). Write only when each meal or snack starts and ends, and one line on how you feel: hungry, just right, full. No calories, no weighing, no scoring of foods.
  5. Look back (days 5–7). How many hours lie between your first and last food of the day? Do you eat late at night from hunger, boredom or habit? Just notice; do not fix anything yet.

4. Children's corner (6+)

This course is not for children. Your child keeps eating full meals and snacks as usual, even while you practise.

Game: "What is your tummy saying?" (5 minutes, with an adult)

  1. Before a meal, ask your child: "What is your tummy saying? Very hungry, a bit hungry, or not hungry yet?"
  2. After the meal, ask again: "What is it saying now?"
  3. Parents answer too. There are no right or wrong answers.

Do not praise your child for eating little, do not criticise them for eating a lot, and do not mention weight. If you are practising an eating window, do not tell your child that skipping meals is admirable.

5. Stop practising if

  • You realise you belong to one of the "should not" groups above.
  • Noting your meal times makes you preoccupied with food, makes you want to eat less, or leaves you feeling guilty after meals. Stop the course and talk to a doctor.
  • You feel panic, have trauma flashbacks, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see Get help now.
