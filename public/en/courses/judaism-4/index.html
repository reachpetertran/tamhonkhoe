Shabbat in the family

A Level 4 course bringing three things from the Jewish tradition into family life: the day of rest, giving to the poor (tzedakah) and the shared table. Readings: the sabbath as a sign and a delight (Exodus 31:16–17, Isaiah 58:13–14), rest for workers too (Exodus 23:12), opening your hand to the needy (Deuteronomy 15:7–11) with Maimonides' eight levels of giving (summarised), the corner of the field and the forgotten sheaf (Leviticus 19:9–10, Deuteronomy 24:19), the open house (Pirkei Avot I, 5), and the song for the sabbath day (Psalm 92). The exercises are for any family: a shared rest period prepared in advance, sharing housework again, a sharing box, setting something aside for others, meals without screens and inviting guests.

Lessons with a link are published. Lessons without a link are still a planned outline; their titles and passages may change during writing and review.

What science finds

  • 38,124 workers: people who detach from work when off duty report less exhaustion and better sleep. This is a correlation. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 7 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 148 studies, 308,849 people: those with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation. (Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton 2010, PLoS Medicine 7(7) · source)
  • Joint WHO/ILO estimates: in 2016 about 488 million people worked 55 or more hours a week; about 745,000 deaths from ischemic heart disease and stroke were attributed to such long hours. These are model estimates built on systematic reviews. (Pega et al. 2021, Environment International 154 (ước tính chung WHO/ILO) · source)
  • A survey, a follow-up and a randomised experiment: spending money on others went with, and in the experiment raised, happiness. The survey part is a correlation; one research group. (Dunn, Aknin & Norton 2008, Science 319(5870) · source)
  • 27 experiments, 4,045 people: acts of kindness raise well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 76 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 people: "if… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65); goals and measures varied widely. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 17 studies, 182,836 children and adolescents: 3 or more family meals a week went with healthier eating and less disordered eating (correlational). (Hammons & Fiese 2011, Pediatrics 127(6) · source)
  • 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 4 course, about 15 minutes a day plus a few family activities each week; best taken after Judaism 1. It describes what the Jewish tradition teaches about Shabbat, tzedakah and the shared table; learners do not need to be Jewish. It does not teach Jewish law (halakha) or Shabbat ritual (candles, Kiddush, Havdalah), does not teach Hebrew, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs. The shared rest period, sharing box, setting aside and screen-free meals are tamhonkhoe's own exercises; they are not Shabbat and not rituals of the tradition. The giving exercise is not financial advice. The description of a Friday evening and of home customs is the writer's judgement. Bible passages on the Vietnamese pages are tamhonkhoe's own translations from JPS 1917, not from Hebrew; the English pages quote JPS 1917 word for word. Pirkei Avot is translated from Gorfinkle's edition (Gutenberg). Mishnah Pe'ah 1:1, Pirkei Avot III, 4 and Maimonides' eight levels of giving are only summarised. No reviewer from within the Jewish tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme directly; the evidence in the lessons is only indirect.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
