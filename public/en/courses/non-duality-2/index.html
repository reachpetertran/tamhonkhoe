Karma yoga and meditation in the Gita

A Level 2 course reading chapters 2, 3 and 6 of the Bhagavad Gita on two paths: action without seeking gain (karma yoga) and meditation. Even-mindedness in success and failure (2:48, 2:56), no one lives without acting (3:5, 3:7–8), the seat, posture and gaze in meditation (6:10–14), moderation in food and sleep (6:16–17), a mind as hard to hold as the wind and the lamp sheltered from the wind (6:33–34, 6:19), steady practice and letting go (6:35–36, 6:26), acting for others (3:20–21), one who strives is not lost (6:40). Each day you sit for 15 minutes, rising to 25, following the Gita's description of seat and posture, along with one small everyday task.

What science finds

  • 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people: mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount; not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine 174(3) · source)
  • Review of 20 meta-analyses: Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) works for anxiety, depression and chronic pain, about as well as CBT. This is guided therapy. (Gloster et al. 2020, Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science 18 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 people: "if… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65); goals and measures varied widely. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38 · source)
  • Review of 83 studies: about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression; it can happen to people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica 142(5) · source)
  • 38,124 workers: people who detach from work when off duty report less exhaustion and better sleep. This is a correlation. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 7 · source)
  • Joint WHO/ILO estimates: in 2016 about 488 million people worked 55 or more hours a week; about 745,000 deaths from ischemic heart disease and stroke were attributed to such long hours. These are model estimates built on systematic reviews. (Pega et al. 2021, Environment International 154 (ước tính chung WHO/ILO) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 29 randomised trials, 2,535 people: mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) reduced rumination moderately (SMD = −0.51), with effects held at follow-up. (Wei et al. 2025, BMC Psychology 13 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 29 studies, 2,668 healthy adults: the MBSR programme reduced stress, anxiety and depression moderately (between-group g = 0.53); studies varied widely. (Khoury et al. 2015, Journal of Psychosomatic Research 78(6) · source)
  • 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6) · source)
  • 33 studies of self-control training: a small effect, smaller still after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017, Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(6) · source)
  • 27 experiments, 4,045 people: acts of kindness raise well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 76 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies, 19,951 people: interventions that help people monitor their progress made them more likely to reach their goals, by a small-to-medium amount (d = 0.40); the effect was larger when progress was physically recorded or reported to others. It measured behavioural goals, not character. (Harkin et al. 2016, Psychological Bulletin 142(2) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 2 course, about 20–30 minutes a day for 8 weeks, with sitting growing from 15 to 25 minutes; best taken after Non-duality 1. It describes what the Bhagavad Gita teaches; learners do not need to be Hindu, and the course does not ask anyone to change their beliefs or to be devoted to anyone. The section name "Non-duality" comes from one reading (Advaita Vedanta); other schools read the same text differently, and the course takes no side. It does not discuss war in the Gita's setting, does not teach ritual, mantras or Sanskrit, does not ask anyone to take the brahmacharya vow, and does not discuss the belief in rebirth. The meditation follows closely the Gita's description of seat, posture and gaze, but it is tamhonkhoe's reading of the text (a chair or cushion instead of kusa grass and deerskin; a neutral point of attention for those outside the tradition), not a substitute for a teacher within the tradition; reading "gazing at the nose-tip" as a soft, lowered gaze is the writer's judgement. The other exercises (noting success and failure, "if… then…" plans, a steady waking time, waiting 10 minutes before a small craving, small acts as an example) are tamhonkhoe's own. The part on moderation is not a diet or medical advice: no skipping meals, no counting calories. On the Vietnamese pages the Gita is tamhonkhoe's own translation from Edwin Arnold's verse version (Gutenberg #2388; Arnold does not number verses, so verse numbers are matched by content); nothing is translated from Sanskrit. The English pages quote Arnold word for word. No reviewer from within the Hindu tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme directly; the evidence in the lessons is only indirect.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
