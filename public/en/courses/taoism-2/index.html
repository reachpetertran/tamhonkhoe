Non-action in practice

Tao Te Ching chapters 2, 11, 22, 48, 37, 63 and 43, and two stories from Zhuangzi chapter IV (‘fasting of the mind’, the li tree); each day 20–30 minutes of sitting and one small action: doing less, listening, not forcing. ‘Fasting of the mind’ is of the mind, not of the body.

Lessons with a link are published. Lessons without a link are still a planned outline; their titles and passages may change during writing and review.

What science finds

  • 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people: mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount; not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine 174(3) · source)
  • Critical review of "decentering" and related constructs such as cognitive defusion: proposes three processes (meta-awareness, disidentification from internal experience, reduced reactivity to thought content). A conceptual analysis, not a test of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(5) · source)
  • 38,124 workers: people who detach from work when off duty report less exhaustion and better sleep. This is a correlation. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 7 · source)
  • Review of 83 studies: about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression; it can happen to people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica 142(5) · source)
  • 65 studies: humility goes with slightly better mental health (small associations). This is a correlation. There is no meta-analysis of humility training yet. (Lee et al. 2025, The Counseling Psychologist 53(6) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 29 studies, 2,668 healthy adults: the MBSR programme reduced stress, anxiety and depression moderately (between-group g = 0.53); studies varied widely. (Khoury et al. 2015, Journal of Psychosomatic Research 78(6) · source)
  • Critical review: "mindfulness" has no agreed definition, many studies are methodologically weak and rely heavily on self-report, and adverse effects are not monitored enough. (Van Dam et al. 2018, Perspectives on Psychological Science 13(1) · source)
  • Interview study of Western Buddhist meditators and teachers: 59 kinds of meditation-related experience in 7 domains, including "sense of self"; impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting; 26 categories of influencing factors. (Lindahl et al. 2017, PLoS ONE 12(5) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of controlled studies: trying to suppress a thought makes it come back more often, by a small-to-moderate amount; the effect varied with the kind of thought and how it was measured. (Abramowitz, Tolin & Street 2001, Clinical Psychology Review 21(5) · source)
  • 33 studies of self-control training: a small effect, smaller still after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017, Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(6) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 2 course: 20–30 minutes of sitting a day, best taken after Taoism 1. Longer meditation can bring difficult experiences; every lesson has a ‘Stop practising if’ section, and people with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor first. ‘Non-action’ in this course means not doing what is not needed and not forcing; it does not mean giving up responsibilities, accepting harm or keeping quiet in the face of injustice. ‘Fasting of the mind’ is of the mind: the course and its kids’ corner involve no fasting from food. The course reads Taoist texts and does not teach Taoist ritual or cultivation methods; the ways of sitting are tamhonkhoe’s own application. The Vietnamese is tamhonkhoe’s own translation from James Legge (1891) and Herbert Giles (1889), not yet checked against the Chinese original; the course leaves out one sentence where Giles renders Heaven as ‘God’ and does not follow Giles’s ‘soul’ interpretation. No reviewer from within the Taoist tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme directly.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
