God beyond concepts

The Cloud of Unknowing (14th century) and The Mystical Theology by the author writing as Dionysius: the Christian contemplative tradition teaches that understanding cannot grasp God, only love turned towards Him. Each day, 25–40 minutes of silent sitting: not trying to picture anything, letting thoughts pass, returning to one short word, setting down an image, and practising gently without straining.

What science finds

  • Online randomised trial, 702 self-identified Christians, 28 days: Centering Prayer with a religious framing, the same practice without it, and simply reflecting on the day. Well-being on the main measure one week later did not differ between the three groups; all three improved from the start. The religious framing changed only measures of spiritual experience. (Rohde, Goldy, Graziosi, Ferguson, Thrul & Yaden 2024, BMC Psychology 12(1) · source)
  • Interview study of Western Buddhist meditators and teachers: 59 kinds of meditation-related experience in 7 domains, including "sense of self"; impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting; 26 categories of influencing factors. (Lindahl et al. 2017, PLoS ONE 12(5) · source)
  • Critical review of "decentering" and related constructs such as cognitive defusion: proposes three processes (meta-awareness, disidentification from internal experience, reduced reactivity to thought content). A conceptual analysis, not a test of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(5) · source)
  • Review of 83 studies: about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression; it can happen to people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica 142(5) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of controlled studies: trying to suppress a thought makes it come back more often, by a small-to-moderate amount; the effect varied with the kind of thought and how it was measured. (Abramowitz, Tolin & Street 2001, Clinical Psychology Review 21(5) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of randomised trials: religious/spiritual interventions reduce anxiety; programmes vary widely and study quality is limited. (Gonçalves et al. 2015, Psychological Medicine 45(14) · source)
  • 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people: mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount; not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine 174(3) · source)
  • Critical review: "mindfulness" has no agreed definition, many studies are methodologically weak and rely heavily on self-report, and adverse effects are not monitored enough. (Van Dam et al. 2018, Perspectives on Psychological Science 13(1) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 3 course: 30–45 minutes a day, best taken after Christianity 1 and 2. It is not for people who are depressed, newly bereaved, going through a major upheaval or having thoughts of dying; people with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe PTSD or long-lasting feelings of being detached from themselves should not study it alone. The course reads only a few passages, while the prologue of The Cloud of Unknowing asks readers to read the whole book and keeps it for those who wish to live a contemplative life; anyone who wants to follow this path should read the whole book with a guide within the tradition. The course does not compare this with how other traditions speak of God, and does not quote the lines on fiends and purgatory, the passage on keeping these things from "the uninitiated", or the translator’s notes. The practice uses a gentle return rather than suppressing thoughts, and offers a way of sitting for those who do not pray (tamhonkhoe’s adaptation). The kids’ corner is only about things that are real but cannot be seen or fully named, and does not speak of God. The English quotes Evelyn Underhill (1922) and C. E. Rolt (1920). No reviewer from within the Christian tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme directly.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
