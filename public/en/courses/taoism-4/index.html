The Way in ordinary things

The stories of the hunchback catching cicadas, the wheelwright and the gambler in the Zhuangzi (chapters XIX, XIII and III), and Tao Te Ching chapters 64, 44, 46, 54, 81 and 9; each day a few minutes of reading, then practice in work, housework and family life: practising small parts, dealing with things while small, knowing enough, care at the end, giving without keeping score.

Lessons with a link are published. Lessons without a link are still a planned outline; their titles and passages may change during writing and review.

What science finds

  • Meta-analysis of 88 studies, more than 11,000 people: deliberate practice explained about 26% of the differences in performance in games, 21% in music, 18% in sports, 4% in education and less than 1% in professions. Mostly correlational. (Macnamara, Hambrick & Oswald 2014, Psychological Science 25(8) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 people: "if… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65); goals and measures varied widely. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples: people who put more weight on money and possessions reported slightly lower well-being (r = −0.19 for measures of materialist values and beliefs). This is a correlation. (Dittmar, Bond, Hurst & Kasser 2014, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 107(5) · source)
  • 38,124 workers: people who detach from work when off duty report less exhaustion and better sleep. This is a correlation. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017, Frontiers in Psychology 7 · source)
  • 27 experiments, 4,045 people: acts of kindness raise well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 76 · source)
  • 33 studies of self-control training: a small effect, smaller still after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017, Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(6) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 4 course: no long sitting, just a few minutes of reading a day and practice in work, housework and family life; best taken after Taoism 1. The course reads Taoist texts; it does not teach Taoist ritual or cultivation methods. ‘Knowing enough’ and ‘not accumulating’ are not financial advice, do not mean accepting hardship or giving up fair rights, and are not about eating; ‘not keeping score’ does not mean one person carrying all the housework; ‘putting the stake down’ does not replace a trade’s safety procedures. The course does not quote the boatman who treats ‘an upset as an ordinary cart accident’, or the passages about war and sacrifices. In chapter 54 Legge writes ‘riches’ where the Chinese speaks of virtue; the course promises no wealth. The Vietnamese is tamhonkhoe’s own translation from James Legge (1891) and Herbert Giles (1889), not yet checked against the Chinese original. No reviewer from within the Taoist tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme directly; the science in the lessons is only nearby evidence.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
