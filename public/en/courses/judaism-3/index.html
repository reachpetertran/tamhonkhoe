Job and the question of suffering

Six passages from the Book of Job (JPS 1917): the opening (chapters 1–2), Job’s lament (7, 23), Eliphaz and Job’s reply to his friends (4, 16), the voice from the whirlwind (38), Job’s final reply (42:1–6) and the ending (42:7–11), with a few Talmud discussions. Each day, slow reading and 25–30 minutes of sitting; each week one small practice: writing a question without rushing to answer, writing a lament, listening without explaining, walking to notice something vast, reading with someone, checking in on someone who is suffering. The course does not explain why any particular person suffers.

Lessons with a link are published. Lessons without a link are still a planned outline; their titles and passages may change during writing and review.

What science finds

  • 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people: mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount; not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine 174(3) · source)
  • Review of 83 studies: about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression; it can happen to people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica 142(5) · source)
  • 146 randomised studies of writing about experiences and feelings: a positive but very small overall effect (r = 0.075). (Frattaroli 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(6) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 49 studies (105 effect sizes) on drawing on religious faith in hard times: "positive" forms (seeking support from God or a community) went with better adjustment (r = 0.33); "negative" forms (such as feeling punished by God) went with poorer adjustment (r = 0.22). These are correlations. (Ano & Vasconcelles 2005, Journal of Clinical Psychology 61(4) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 148 studies, 308,849 people: those with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation. (Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton 2010, PLoS Medicine 7(7) · source)
  • Randomised trial, 60 healthy older adults, a 15-minute outdoor walk each week for 8 weeks: the group taught to notice awe reported more prosocial emotions and less daily distress than the group taking ordinary walks. A small study. (Sturm et al. 2022, Emotion 22(5) · source)
  • 97 studies, 7,181 people: therapy that integrates the client’s faith reduces distress as well as standard therapy and adds spiritual well-being. There is no review of lectio divina itself. (Captari et al. 2018, Journal of Clinical Psychology 74(11) · source)
  • Critical review: "mindfulness" has no agreed definition, many studies are methodologically weak and rely heavily on self-report, and adverse effects are not monitored enough. (Van Dam et al. 2018, Perspectives on Psychological Science 13(1) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 3 course on suffering and loss: 30–40 minutes a day, best taken after Judaism 1 and 2. It is not for people who are depressed, recently bereaved (especially of a child), seriously ill or having thoughts of dying; people with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should not study it alone. It describes what the Book of Job tells and some ways the Jewish tradition reads it; it does not explain why any particular person suffers, does not say that suffering is a punishment or a reward, does not read the ending as a promise of repayment, and does not compare with other traditions. It does not quote chapter 3 (Job cursing the day of his birth) or verse 7:16. The lament writing, walking and reading with someone are tamhonkhoe’s own exercises, not rituals of the tradition. The Talmud (Bava Batra 15a–16b, Moed Katan 28b) is only summarised. The Vietnamese is tamhonkhoe’s own translation from JPS 1917, not from Hebrew; the English pages quote JPS word for word. The kids’ corner is only about small hard days, never death, illness or loss. No reviewer from within the Jewish tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme directly.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
