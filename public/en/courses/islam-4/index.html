Zakat, Ramadan and daily life

Who zakat is for (Qur'an 9:60) and giving without reproach (2:262–263); the month of Ramadan and the Night of Power (97:1–5); guarding speech (49:12, Bukhari 1903) and who is exempt from fasting; parents (17:23–24); neighbours and the wayfarer (4:36, Bukhari 6014); giving without wanting thanks (76:8–9). Zakat and fasting are only described; the exercises are everyday actions: one act of giving, slow evening reading, guarding one speech habit, asking after parents, learning a neighbour's name, and an optional light restraint exercise for healthy adults.

What science finds

  • A survey, a follow-up and a randomised experiment: spending money on others went with, and in the experiment raised, happiness. The survey part is a correlation; one research group. (Dunn, Aknin & Norton 2008, Science 319(5870) · source)
  • 27 experiments, 4,045 people: acts of kindness raise well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018, Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 76 · source)
  • Review of 22 papers: Ramadan’s effect on mental health is generally small, but people with schizophrenia or bipolar disorder have a higher risk of relapse. (Heun 2018, Global Psychiatry 1(1) · source)
  • 11 studies, 1,436 people, on fasting interventions in general: in randomised trials at low risk of bias, fasting groups had lower anxiety and depression scores than controls. The authors call the results preliminary; no study included people with psychiatric conditions. (Berthelot et al. 2021, Nutrients 13(11) · source)
  • 33 studies of self-control training: a small effect, smaller still after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017, Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(6) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 94 independent tests, more than 8,000 people: "if… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65); goals and measures varied widely. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38 · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 148 studies, 308,849 people: those with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation. (Holt-Lunstad, Smith & Layton 2010, PLoS Medicine 7(7) · source)
  • 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 4 course, about 15 minutes a day plus a few actions in the week. It describes zakat, sadaqah and the month of Ramadan as Muslims understand and practise them; learners do not need to be Muslim. It does not teach ritual: it does not teach how to calculate zakat, does not teach the Ramadan fast, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs. It is not financial advice; the giving exercises are within your means, without borrowing or cutting essential spending. The light restraint exercise in Week 2 is optional and for healthy adults only: leaving out one snack or sweet drink on 3 days, with no skipped meals, no going without drinks and no weight-loss aim; it is not for anyone under 18, pregnant or breastfeeding, with a current or past eating disorder, with diabetes treated with medication or a chronic illness under treatment. The kids' corner has no fasting or giving up foods. The exercises are not zakat or fasting and do not replace them. The Vietnamese pages quote Hasan Abdulkarim's translation on QuranEnc.com word for word; the English pages quote Pickthall. Hadith are only summarised, because the translations on sunnah.com are in copyright. The course notes some differences between Sunni and Shi'a practice (khums, the last nights of Ramadan) without comparing them. The descriptions of practice are the writer's judgement; no reviewer from within the Islamic tradition has checked it yet, no nutrition expert has reviewed the restraint exercise, and no study has evaluated this programme directly; the evidence in the lessons is only indirect.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
