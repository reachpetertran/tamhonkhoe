Self-inquiry: ‘Who am I?’

A Level 3 course reading Upanishad passages that ask about the Self, and practising Ramana Maharshi's "Who am I?" inquiry. Brahman taught by "No, no!" (Brihadaranyaka 2.3.6), Indra learning from Prajapati about waking, dreaming and deep sleep (Chandogya 8.7–11), salt dissolved in water and "thou art it" (Chandogya 6.13), you cannot see the seer of sight (Brihadaranyaka 3.4.2) and the one who knows "let me hear this" (Chandogya 8.12.4–5), two birds on one tree (Mundaka 3.1.1–2), the Self to be heard, reflected on and meditated on (Brihadaranyaka 2.4.5). Each day you sit for 30–45 minutes; inquiry starts at 10 minutes, with an "unmoored" rating and a back-to-the-ground step after every sitting.

What science finds

  • Critical review of "decentering" and related constructs such as cognitive defusion: proposes three processes (meta-awareness, disidentification from internal experience, reduced reactivity to thought content). A conceptual analysis, not a test of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015, Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(5) · source)
  • Review of 83 studies: about 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (about 4% in experimental studies), most often anxiety or depression; it can happen to people with no history of mental illness. (Farias et al. 2020, Acta Psychiatrica Scandinavica 142(5) · source)
  • Interview study of Western Buddhist meditators and teachers: 59 kinds of meditation-related experience in 7 domains, including "sense of self"; impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting; 26 categories of influencing factors. (Lindahl et al. 2017, PLoS ONE 12(5) · source)
  • 47 randomised trials, 3,515 people: mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount; not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine 174(3) · source)
  • Meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies, 19,951 people: interventions that help people monitor their progress made them more likely to reach their goals, by a small-to-medium amount (d = 0.40); the effect was larger when progress was physically recorded or reported to others. It measured behavioural goals, not character. (Harkin et al. 2016, Psychological Bulletin 142(2) · source)
  • 96 people each chose a small daily action for 12 weeks (82 with enough data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing one occasion did not materially affect this. Small study, self-reported. (Lally, van Jaarsveld, Potts & Wardle 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6) · source)

Limits, honestly

A Level 3 course, about 30–45 minutes a day for 6 weeks; best taken after Non-duality 1 and Non-duality 2. People who are depressed, recently bereaved, going through a major upheaval or having thoughts of self-harm should not take it now; people who have had psychosis, bipolar disorder, severe PTSD or a lasting sense of being detached from themselves should not study it alone. It describes what the Upanishads teach and the teaching of Ramana Maharshi; learners do not need to be Hindu, and the course does not ask anyone to change their beliefs or to be devoted to anyone. The section name "Non-duality" comes from one reading (Advaita Vedanta); schools such as those of Ramanuja and Madhva read the same passages differently, and the course gives each a single sentence and takes no side. It does not teach ritual, mantras or Sanskrit, does not discuss death or rebirth, does not ask learners to believe that the world is "not real", and does not suggest anyone leave home or work. Week 2 uses chapter 8 of the Chandogya Upanishad instead of the Mandukya Upanishad in the original plan, because no public-domain translation of the Mandukya with a checked licence was available. The Ramana material is only summarised in tamhonkhoe's own words with a link, because T. M. P. Mahadevan's English translation is in copyright. The exercises ("this is known", noticing the passage between waking and sleep, three pauses a day, the "unmoored" rating, slow walking as "the second bird", self-rating and a 4-week plan) are tamhonkhoe's own and no substitute for a teacher within the tradition. On the Vietnamese pages the Upanishads are tamhonkhoe's own translation from F. Max Müller's English (Sacred Books of the East, volumes 1 and 15); nothing is translated from Sanskrit. The English pages quote Müller word for word. No reviewer from within the Hindu tradition has checked it yet, and no study has evaluated this programme or Ramana-style inquiry directly; the evidence in the lessons is only indirect.

Stop practising if

Stop or shorten practice and talk to a professional if you notice: rising anxiety or panic; low mood that lingers; feeling that you or the world is ‘not real’; flooding memories of trauma; several nights of poor sleep; hearing or seeing things others do not; thoughts of harming yourself. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should ask a doctor before practising more than 20 minutes a day.
