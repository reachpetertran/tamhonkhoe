**This week:** a slow reading of the image of the chariot in the Katha Upanishad, and watching one of your own "horses": a habit that runs after the senses on its own.

**Measurable goal:** choose one habit; each day mark how many times you noticed it and how many times you paused for one breath before following it; record this on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### What the Upanishads are

[Traditional teaching] The Upanishads are texts at the end of the Vedas. They say little about ritual and much about the question "who am I" and about the deepest reality. The Vedanta tradition (literally "the end of the Veda") is built on the Upanishads, the Gita and one further text, the Brahma Sutra.

The Katha Upanishad tells of the boy Nachiketa, who goes to Yama, the god who rules over death, and asks what remains after death. This lesson does not discuss that question; it only reads the image Yama uses to speak about human beings.

The lesson uses Swami Paramananda's English translation (1919). It divides the Katha into six parts; the "third part" is usually numbered 1.3.

### The chariot and the horses

::: kinh upan-paramananda nguyen-van | Katha Upanishad, Part Third, verses III–IV
Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins.

The senses are called the horses; the sense objects are the roads; when the Atman is united with body, senses and mind, then the wise call Him the enjoyer.
:::

::: kinh upan-paramananda nguyen-van | Katha Upanishad, Part Third, verses V–VI
He who is without discrimination and whose mind is always uncontrolled, his senses are unmanageable, like the vicious horses of a driver.

But he who is full of discrimination and whose mind is always controlled, his senses are manageable, like the good horses of a driver.
:::

A note on context: [Inference] The image divides a person into parts: the owner of the chariot (the Self), the driver (the intellect, the power to discern), the reins (the mind), the horses (the senses), the roads (whatever attracts the senses). The horses are not bad; good horses are horses that answer to the reins. "Discrimination" (viveka) in Paramananda's commentary means telling what is good from what is merely pleasant, and the real from the unreal. The verses that follow in the Katha say that the skilled driver reaches the goal; schools understand that goal differently, and the lesson does not go into it.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] 96 people each chose one small thing to do every day for 12 weeks (82 had full data): it became automatic after 18 to 254 days depending on the person; missing a single day did not matter much. A small, self-reported study. (Lally et al. 2010)
- [Verified] Meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies, 19,951 people: ways of helping people monitor their own progress increased goal attainment by a small-to-moderate amount (d = 0.40); the effect was larger when progress was written down or shared with others. (Harkin et al. 2016)
- [Inference] Habits form slowly and differently in each person, so one week is only enough to notice the "horse", not to train it. Writing it down is the part of the exercise with the best evidence.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 10–12 minutes a day.

1. **Read slowly (3 minutes).** Read the two Katha passages once. Draw a simple chariot and label each part.
2. **Day 1: choose one horse (3 minutes).** A small habit that runs after the senses on its own: picking up your phone the moment you are free, snacking when you see food, opening the news while working. Write down its "road": what sets it off (seeing, hearing, boredom, tiredness).
3. **During the day: notice.** Each time you see the horse running, make a tally mark. Do not blame yourself.
4. **Try the reins.** When you notice in time, pause for one breath before deciding whether to do it. Doing it is fine; what you are practising is having one breath to choose. Each time you pause, circle the tally mark.
5. **End of the day (3 minutes).** Count the marks and circles. Write one line: "My horse tends to run when …".

Choose a small habit, not an addiction or a health problem. Those need help from people with the right expertise.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "The driver and the horse"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The adult is the "horse"; the child is the "driver", holding both ends of a scarf as reins.
2. The child pulls gently on the left, the horse turns left; on the right, the horse turns right; both together, the horse stops.
3. Now and then the "horse" sees a toy and wants to run to it. The driver says "wait a moment" and pulls the reins. The horse stops for one breath.
4. Swap roles. Ask: "When do you want to run straight to something? How can you be your own driver?"

Move slowly, indoors, in a clear space. Never put the scarf around anyone's neck or body.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- "Controlling the senses" turns into punishing yourself: skipping meals, skipping sleep, criticising your body. The exercise is only noticing and pausing for one breath.
- The habit you are watching is drinking, drugs, gambling or eating behaviour that worries you. Please find someone with the right expertise.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
