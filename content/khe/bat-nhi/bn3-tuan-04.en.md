**This week:** reading two Upanishad passages about "the one who knows", then practising the "Who am I?" inquiry as taught by Ramana Maharshi.

**Measurable goal:** sit for at least 35 minutes on 5 of 7 days, with 10 minutes of inquiry for the first 3 days, rising to 20 if it feels all right; after each sitting rate how "unmoored" you feel from 0 to 10.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad the sage Yajnavalkya answers many questioners. Ushasta asks him about the Self within all:

::: kinh upanishads-muller-15 nguyen-van | Brihadaranyaka Upanishad III, 4, 2 (end), p. 129
'Which Self, O Yâgñavalkya, is within all?'

Yâgñavalkya replied: 'Thou couldst not see the (true) seer of sight, thou couldst not hear the (true) hearer of hearing, nor perceive the perceiver of perception, nor know the knower of knowledge. This is thy Self, who is within all.'
:::

[Traditional teaching] At the end of the Indra story (Week 2), Prajapati teaches about the one who knows:

::: kinh upanishads-muller nguyen-van | Chandogya Upanishad VIII, 12, 4–5 (first sentence of 5), p. 142
'Now where the sight has entered into the void (the open space, the black pupil of the eye), there is the person of the eye, the eye itself is the instrument of seeing. He who knows, let me smell this, he is the Self, the nose is the instrument of smelling. He who knows, let me say this, he is the Self, the tongue is the instrument of saying. He who knows, let me hear this, he is the Self, the ear is the instrument of hearing.

'He who knows, let me think this, he is the Self, the mind is his divine eye.
:::

### Ramana Maharshi's "Who am I?" inquiry

[Traditional teaching] Ramana Maharshi (1879–1950) lived most of his life at the hill of Arunachala, Tiruvannamalai, in south India. Around 1902 a man named Sivaprakasam Pillai asked him about self-inquiry; the answers were later printed as a small booklet, "Who am I?" (*Nan Yar?*, in Tamil). The English translation is in copyright, so here the ideas are summarised in tamhonkhoe's own words. You can read the English text on the [Sri Ramanasramam website](https://www.sriramanamaharshi.org/Teachings/who_am_I.pdf).

- According to Ramana, of all thoughts the "I" thought arises first; other thoughts arise after it.
- When a thought arises, do not follow it, but ask: "To whom does this thought arise?" The answer will be: "To me." Then ask: "Who am I?", and let attention turn back to where the sense of "I" arises, instead of following the thought.
- However many thoughts arise does not matter; each time, do the same. Ramana compares the question "Who am I?" to the stick used to stir a fire: it puts out the other thoughts, and in the end is itself used up.
- Ramana says that other means, such as breath control, repeating a name or mantra, only quieten the mind for a time, and he treats inquiry as the main path. That is his view; other traditions and teachers may not agree.

### Reading it in context

- [Inference] The two Upanishad passages and Ramana point the same way: the knower cannot become a thing that is known. So the question "Who am I?" is not meant to find an answer in words. If you find yourself making up a definition, that too is just another thought.
- Ramana also says things that are hard for people outside the tradition, such as that the world appears only when the mind turns outward. The course does not ask you to believe that; it only borrows the way of asking.
- Inquiry is not meant to erase who you are in daily life. After the sitting you are still a parent, a child, a colleague, with things to do.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] The critical review by Bernstein and colleagues (2015) describes "decentering" as three processes: meta-awareness of what you are experiencing, disidentification from internal experience, and reduced reactivity to the content of thoughts. It is a conceptual analysis, not a trial of effectiveness. (Bernstein et al. 2015)
- [Verified] An interview study of Western Buddhist meditators and teachers: one domain of experience was "sense of self"; impact ranged from minimal and passing to severe and lasting. (Lindahl et al. 2017)
- [Inference] tamhonkhoe has not found any trial of Ramana-style self-inquiry. The step "to whom does this thought arise?" is close to the reduced reactivity to thought content that Bernstein describes, but the question "Who am I?" aims straight at the sense of "me", the very domain Lindahl found can sometimes be severe. That is why this week starts short and has an "unmoored" rating.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 40 minutes a day.

1. **Read slowly (3 minutes).** Read the two passages and the summary of Ramana.
2. **Sit with the breath (10 minutes).**
3. **Inquiry (10 minutes; from day four 20 minutes if it feels all right).** Sit still. When a thought arises, do not follow it; ask silently: "To whom does this thought arise?" – "To me." – "Who am I?" Then let attention turn back to the sense of "I" and rest there for a few breaths, without looking for an answer. When a new thought arises, do it again. Do not try to force the mind blank.
4. **Sit with the breath (5 minutes).** Drop the question; just breathe.
5. **Back to the ground (5 minutes).** Open your eyes, feel your feet, name three things, take a sip of water, stretch.
6. **Rate and note.** Rate how "unmoored" you feel (no ground to stand on, a stranger to yourself) from 0 to 10. If two sittings in a row score 7 or more, go back to the Week 3 exercise and do not lengthen the time.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Who is hearing the bell?"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The adult rings a bell (or taps a spoon on a glass). The child closes their eyes and raises a hand when they can no longer hear it.
2. Ask: "What do you hear with?" (ears). "What do you see with?" (eyes). "How do you know whether you feel happy or sad?" Let the child think; any answer is fine.
3. Swap: the child rings the bell, the parent raises a hand.

Ask only about the senses; do not ask a child "who are you?".

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The question "Who am I?" leaves you feeling unmoored, as if you had nowhere to stand, or a sense of being outside your body, watching yourself from outside, stays after you stand up. Then stop the inquiry, do the "back to the ground" step, talk to someone you trust, and for the next few days only sit for 20 minutes with the breath. If the feeling lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
- Your "unmoored" rating is 7 or more two sittings in a row.
- You start thinking that this body, this life or the people close to you "do not matter", or you skip meals, sleep, work or time with loved ones to sit longer.
- If you have ever had psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask your doctor before doing exercises longer than 20 minutes.
- If the signs are serious, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
