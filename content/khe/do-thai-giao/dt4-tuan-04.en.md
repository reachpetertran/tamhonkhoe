**This week:** slow reading of two harvest laws: leaving the corner of the field and leaving a forgotten sheaf. During the week, deliberately set something aside for others, in a way that means the receiver does not have to ask and does not feel in debt.

**Measurable goal:** read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; set something aside for others at least 3 times this week; at least once, give without saying that you are the giver.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### The corner and the gleanings

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Leviticus 19:9–10
And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corner of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleaning of thy harvest.

And thou shalt not glean thy vineyard, neither shalt thou gather the fallen fruit of thy vineyard; thou shalt leave them for the poor and for the stranger: I am the LORD your God.
:::

A note on context: these two verses open a series of laws on how to treat others in chapter 19, just before "Ye shall not steal" and "the wages of a hired servant shall not abide with thee all night". Verse 18 of this chapter is "love thy neighbour as thyself", read in [Judaism 1 – Week 2](/en/traditions/judaism/judaism-1-week-02/).

### The forgotten sheaf

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Deuteronomy 24:19
When thou reapest thy harvest in thy field, and hast forgot a sheaf in the field, thou shalt not go back to fetch it; it shall be for the stranger, for the fatherless, and for the widow; that the LORD thy God may bless thee in all the work of thy hands.
:::

A note on context: the next verses (24:20–21) say the same about olive trees and vineyards. [Traditional teaching] The Book of Ruth tells how Ruth, a Moabite widow, gleaned in the field of Boaz (Ruth chapter 2); it is the gleaning law told as a story.

### No minimum measure

[Traditional teaching] The Mishnah has a whole tractate called Pe'ah ("corner"). Its opening teaching (Mishnah Pe'ah 1:1) lists things that "have no definite quantity", among them the corner of the field, doing deeds of kindness for others, and the study of Torah.

[Inference] These three laws share one point: the poor walk into the field and take for themselves; they do not stand waiting for the owner to hand things out. Their share is left in advance, so they do not have to ask, and the owner is not standing there to be thanked.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experiments (4,045 people) found that doing kind acts for others raises the doer's own well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 94 independent tests (more than 8,000 people): "if… then…" plans helped people reach goals more than intentions alone, by a medium-to-large amount (d = 0.65); goals and measures varied widely. (Gollwitzer & Sheeran 2006)
- [Inference] No study has measured "setting aside a share" or quiet giving as such. The "if… then…" plan below helps the setting aside not to be forgotten.

## 3. Practice for adults

Five minutes of reading each morning, two minutes of looking back each evening; the setting aside happens during the day.

1. **Read slowly (5 minutes, morning).** Days 1–4 read Leviticus 19:9–10; days 5–7 read Deuteronomy 24:19. Ask: "What is the corner of my field today?" It might be food, time, belongings, a seat, an opportunity.
2. **Make one "if… then…" plan.** For example: "If I go to the market, then I buy an extra portion for the elderly lady next door." "If I cook rice, then I set aside a portion for the security guard." "If work is handed out, then I give a visible easy task to the new colleague."
3. **Set something aside at least 3 times this week.** Set it aside in advance, not just the leftovers.
4. **Give in a way that protects dignity.** At least once, give without saying that you are the giver, or give so that the receiver does not feel in debt (for example "we cooked too much, could you help us eat it?").
5. **"The forgotten sheaf".** Once this week, when someone takes by mistake or forgets to return a small thing of yours (a pen, a piece of cake), let it go and do not ask for it back.
6. **Look back (2 minutes, evening).** One line: what did I set aside today, and for whom?

**Very important.** Food given to others must be fresh, clean and covered; ask first whether the receiver has allergies or avoids certain foods. Step 5 is only for small things; it does not apply to large sums of money, documents, or someone deliberately taking your things.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "The saved piece"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. When the family eats fruit or cake, before eating, the child picks one good piece and sets it aside on a plate.
2. The adult asks: "Who shall we save this piece for?" (grandparents, a brother or sister, a neighbour's child, someone coming home late from work).
3. The child puts the saved piece on a plate, covers it, and may add a small drawing.
4. When the person receives it, the child does not need to say "I gave you this"; the adult can simply say: "Our family saved this for you."

Ask first whether the person is allergic to anything. This is not a game of making the child go without: the child still eats their own share as usual.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The exercise makes you feel guilty or blame yourself for "not giving enough". If so, reduce it to once a week.
- Setting things aside leaves you or your family short of what you need. If so, stop.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
