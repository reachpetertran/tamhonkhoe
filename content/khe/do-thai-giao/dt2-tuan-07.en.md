**This week:** reading a short saying from Pirkei Avot and two passages from the chapter on humility in Mesillat Yesharim. The trait of the week: humility, in Hebrew *anavah*.

**Measurable goal:** each day, one of three humble acts (learning from someone, admitting a mistake, using a strength to help someone), on at least 5 of 7 days; keep the evening notebook on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### A trait with no "middle way"

::: kinh pirkei-avot-gorfinkle nguyen-van | chapter IV, 4
R. Levitas of Jabneh said, "Be exceedingly lowly of spirit, since the hope of man is but the worm."
:::

A note on context: Gorfinkle's footnote says Rabbi Levitas lived around the year 120, and that Maimonides (Rambam) taught that the path of virtue is usually the middle way between the extremes of "too little" and "too much", but made an exception of humility: following this saying, he held that humility taken to the extreme is the virtue. [Inference] The second half recalls that the human body will return to the earth: no one has lasting grounds for pride. This course does not use the saying as a practice of contemplating death. Gorfinkle's footnote number (10) is left out of the quotation.

### Humility in thought

[Traditional teaching] Luzzatto writes that humility means not attributing importance to oneself for any reason, and that it must begin in one's thoughts before it shows in one's deeds; someone humble only on the outside is a hypocrite (chapter 22, segments 2–4). He says what most easily makes a person proud is wisdom.

::: kinh mesillat-sebag nguyen-van | chapter 22, segment 8
But, there is no sage that will not make mistakes and that will not need to learn from the words of his peers, and very often, even from his students. How then can he pride himself on his wisdom?
:::

### What we have is for helping others

::: kinh mesillat-sebag nguyen-van | chapter 22, segments 10–11
Rather, if he possess great wisdom, behold, he is under duty to teach it to those in need of it, similar to the statement of Rabbi Yochanan ben Zakai: "if you learned much Torah, do not take credit for yourself since for this you were created!" (Avot 2:8).

If he is wealthy, he may rejoice in his lot, but it is incumbent upon him to help those who do not have. If he is strong, he must help those who are weak and rescue the oppressed.
:::

A note on context: straight after (segment 12), Luzzatto compares people to servants in a household, each given a task. [Inference] Humility here is not thinking oneself inferior or useless. A humble person still knows what they are good at, but sees it not as grounds for standing above others but as the task they have been given. Ben Zoma's saying in Week 1 ("he who learns from all men") and segment 8 above meet here. The Jewish reviewer will say how the tradition tells humility apart from putting oneself down. Segment numbers follow Sefaria's division of the text.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A 2025 meta-analysis of 65 studies: more humble people reported slightly better health, life satisfaction and sense of meaning, and slightly less depression and anxiety. All the associations were small (correlations of about 0.06 to 0.20). This is a **correlation**, mostly from cross-sectional, self-report studies: we do not know whether humility makes people healthier, or healthier people find humility easier. (Lee et al. 2025)
- We have not verified any meta-analysis of humility training programmes.
- [Inference] The research measures humility with self-report questionnaires; someone who rates themselves as very humble may not be.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

1. **Morning: slow reading (3 minutes).** Read the three passages. The second time, stop at "even from his students".
2. **Reminder phrase (1 minute).** "What I have is for helping others."
3. **Reflection (8 minutes).** Day 1: write down three of your strengths (a skill, some knowledge, a circumstance you enjoy). Next to each, write the name of someone who could be helped by it. On the following days: write about a time you were wrong and someone pointed it out, and what you learned.
4. **During the day: one humble act.** Choose one of three:
    - **Learn:** ask the opinion of, or ask to be shown something by, someone younger, less experienced or in a lower position than you.
    - **Admit a mistake:** say clearly "I was wrong about …", without adding "but".
    - **Help:** use one of the strengths you wrote down to help the person you named, without anyone needing to know.
5. **Evening: trait notebook (5 minutes).** Mark ✓ or ✗, then write two lines: "Today I learned / admitted / helped …"; "The moment I wanted to be praised was …".

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity **"Water flows low"**. See the full activity at [Kids' corner – Activity 9](/en/kids-corner/activity-09/).

Afterwards, you can ask: "What are you good at? Who in your class could you help with it?" Do not tell the child they are "not good at anything" or "don't show off": humility is not running yourself down.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The exercise leaves you feeling useless, hateful or undeserving for several days in a row. That is not humility and may be a sign of depression; stop the exercise and ask a doctor.
- The words "the hope of man is but the worm" weigh on you or set you thinking a lot about death. If so, leave that saying out and read only the two passages from Mesillat Yesharim.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
