**What this course is, and is not.** Judaism 4 belongs to Level 4 (Integration): bringing teaching into the rhythm of family life. It describes how the Jewish tradition speaks of Shabbat, of tzedakah (giving to the poor; the root of the word means "justice") and of the shared table. It does not teach Jewish law (halakha) or Shabbat ritual, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs. The exercises are for any family: a shared rest period, sharing housework, a sharing box, setting something aside for others, a meal without screens. Best taken after [Judaism 1 – Week 3: The day of rest](/en/traditions/judaism/judaism-1-week-03/).

**This week:** slow reading of two passages on the sabbath, learning about Friday evening in a Jewish home, then preparing ahead as a family and sharing one rest period.

**Measurable goal:** read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; one shared family rest period of at least 2 hours this week, with one thing prepared the day before; three lines written afterwards.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### A sign

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Exodus 31:16–17
Wherefore the children of Israel shall keep the sabbath, to observe the sabbath throughout their generations, for a perpetual covenant.

It is a sign between Me and the children of Israel for ever; for in six days the LORD made heaven and earth, and on the seventh day He ceased from work and rested.’
:::

A note on context: this passage closes the instructions to Moses about building the Tabernacle (Exodus 25–31). The closing ’ in JPS ends a speech that begins at verse 13. [Expert judgement] The Hebrew word JPS renders "rested" at the end of verse 17 is often explained as "caught His breath, was refreshed"; a word from the same root appears in Exodus 23:12, next week's passage. The other two reasons for the sabbath (Genesis 2:1–3 and Deuteronomy 5:12–15) are quoted in [Judaism 1 – Week 3](/en/traditions/judaism/judaism-1-week-03/).

### Calling the sabbath a delight

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Isaiah 58:13–14
If thou turn away thy foot because of the sabbath, From pursuing thy business on My holy day; And call the sabbath a delight, And the holy of the LORD honourable; And shalt honour it, not doing thy wonted ways, Nor pursuing thy business, nor speaking thereof;

Then shalt thou delight thyself in the LORD, And I will make thee to ride upon the high places of the earth, And I will feed thee with the heritage of Jacob thy father; For the mouth of the LORD hath spoken it.
:::

A note on context: Isaiah 58 opens by rebuking fasting that goes along with injustice (58:6–7 is quoted in [Body 4 – Week 2](/en/body/body-4-week-02/)) and ends with these two verses on the sabbath. [Expert judgement] The Hebrew word for "delight" is oneg; from this verse the tradition speaks of "oneg Shabbat" (the delight of Shabbat) and "kavod Shabbat" (honouring Shabbat), for example by wearing good clothes and serving a better meal than on other days. [Inference] The passage says not only what to stop, but that the day of rest should be a day of joy.

### Friday evening in a Jewish home (described)

[Expert judgement] In many Jewish homes Friday afternoon is a time of preparation: cooking ahead, cleaning, bathing, laying a tablecloth. Before sunset someone in the household lights the Shabbat candles. Dinner often includes a song welcoming Shabbat, parents blessing their children, the blessing over a cup of wine (Kiddush), two loaves of challah, a long meal with singing, and grace after the meal. The two loaves are often explained as a reminder of the double portion of manna in Exodus 16. Families and communities keep these customs in very different ways.

The above is description only. The course does not teach Shabbat observance, and the exercise below is not Shabbat.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 86 publications (38,124 workers) found that people who detach from work when off duty, neither working nor thinking about work, report less exhaustion and better sleep. This is a correlation; it does not prove that rest is the cause. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 people): people with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation. (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010)
- [Inference] None of these studies measures keeping Shabbat. For observant Jews, Shabbat is a commandment and a holy day, not a health measure.

## 3. Practice for adults

Five minutes of reading each morning, two minutes of looking back each evening; one shared family rest period this week.

1. **Read slowly (5 minutes, morning).** Days 1–3 read Exodus 31:16–17; days 4–7 read Isaiah 58:13–14. Read twice, pausing at any word that catches you.
2. **A 10-minute family meeting (start of the week).** Choose one shared period of at least 2 hours (for example Saturday afternoon or Sunday morning). Each person names one thing they would enjoy doing then (after Isaiah 58:13, "a delight") and one thing the family will set aside (for example work, shopping, big cleaning jobs, screens).
3. **Prepare the day before.** Do at least one thing in advance so that chores do not crowd in: cook a dish ahead, lay the table, charge your phone and put it away, tell colleagues beforehand that you will not read messages during that time.
4. **Keep the rest period.** If someone slips into work partway through, do not blame them; invite them back.
5. **Look back (2 minutes that evening).** Write three lines: what the family set aside; the happiest moment; what we want to keep next week.

This is a general exercise for any family and does not imitate Shabbat ritual (no candle lighting or Kiddush as a ceremony). Jewish families keep Shabbat in the way of their community; families of other traditions can use their own tradition's day of rest.

**Very important.** Shift workers, people caring for someone ill, and parents of young children may not have 2 hours in one block. Choose a shorter period or split it in two. Do not drop care that someone else needs in order to keep the exercise.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Getting ready for rest"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The day before the shared rest period, the adult asks: "Tomorrow the whole family rests. What can we do now so we don't have to rush tomorrow?"
2. The child picks one safe task they can manage: putting toys away, setting spoons and chopsticks on the table, choosing a book to read together, drawing a card saying "Everyone is invited to rest".
3. Do it together, and the adult gives one specific thank-you ("You tidied up, so tomorrow we have room to play").
4. The next day, when the rest period begins, the child hands out the invitation card or names what they prepared.

The child does not use knives, the stove or hot water. The game does not teach how to keep Shabbat; Jewish families can tell their child about it in their own way.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The rest period makes you strongly anxious or restless about work. If so, shorten it, and if the anxiety lasts, talk with a trusted friend or a professional.
- The shared rest period causes tension or arguments at home. Stop, talk again when everyone is calm, or choose a shorter period.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
