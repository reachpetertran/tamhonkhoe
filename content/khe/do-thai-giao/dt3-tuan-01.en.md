**Before you start this course:** this is a Level 3 course reading the Book of Job, a book about loss, illness and the question "why do good people suffer". It asks for 30–40 minutes a day for 6 weeks. It is best taken after [Judaism 1](/en/courses/judaism-1/) and [Judaism 2](/en/courses/judaism-2/). If you are depressed, recently bereaved (especially if you have lost a child), seriously ill, or having thoughts of dying or harming yourself, this course is not for now: please see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/) and talk to someone you trust. People with a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD should not study this course alone; if you want to take it, ask a doctor and study with an experienced guide.

**How to use this course:** the course reads six passages from the Book of Job and describes how the Jewish tradition reads the book. You do not need to be Jewish. The course does not explain why any particular person suffers, does not say that suffering is a punishment or a reward, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs. It does not compare this with how other traditions speak about suffering. Jews taking the course should treat it as extra reading, not a substitute for learning with a rabbi or their own community.

**This week:** slow reading of the opening of the Book of Job (chapters 1–2). Sit for 25 minutes each day. Each evening, write down one question the reading raises for you, and do not write an answer.

**Measurable goal:** a full session of reading and sitting (about 35 minutes) on at least 5 of 7 days; one question written down each evening, on at least 5 of 7 evenings.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### The Book of Job and the translation used here

[Traditional teaching] The Book of Job belongs to the third part of the Hebrew Bible, the "Writings" (Ketuvim). It opens and closes in prose (chapters 1–2 and the end of chapter 42); the middle is poetry: the speeches of Job and his friends, then God's answer out of the whirlwind.

The English pages of this course quote the JPS 1917 translation by the Jewish Publication Society (public domain). As in Judaism 1, JPS prints "the LORD" in capitals where the Hebrew has the personal name of God, which the Jewish tradition does not pronounce aloud.

### The opening story

[Traditional teaching] The first verse introduces Job:

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Job 1:1
THERE was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job; and that man was whole-hearted and upright, and one that feared God, and shunned evil.
:::

In brief, the verses that follow (1:2–19): Job is wealthy, with seven sons and three daughters. In a scene in heaven, a figure called "the satan" (in Hebrew with the article, *ha-satan*, "the accuser" or "the adversary") asks whether Job fears God for nothing. He is allowed to test Job. In a single day Job hears four messages of disaster: his oxen, sheep and camels are lost with their servants, and finally his children die when a house collapses. Job responds like this:

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Job 1:20–22
Then Job arose, and rent his mantle, and shaved his head, and fell down upon the ground, and worshipped;

And he said; Naked came I out of my mother’s womb, And naked shall I return thither; The LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; Blessed be the name of the LORD.

For all this Job sinned not, nor ascribed aught unseemly to God.
:::

Chapter 2 tells of a second test: Job is struck with sores all over his body and sits among the ashes. His wife speaks bitterly; he answers that if we receive good from God, we also receive evil. Then the three friends arrive:

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Job 2:11–13
Now when Job’s three friends heard of all this evil that was come upon him, they came every one from his own place, Eliphaz the Temanite, and Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite; and they made an appointment together to come to bemoan him and to comfort him.

And when they lifted up their eyes afar off, and knew him not, they lifted up their voice, and wept; and they rent every one his mantle, and threw dust upon their heads toward heaven.

So they sat down with him upon the ground seven days and seven nights, and none spoke a word unto him; for they saw that his grief was very great.
:::

### Reading in context

- [Expert judgement] The reader knows something Job does not: the scene in heaven. Job and his friends are never told about it. Many readers find that this gap is what makes the book so hard to read.
- [Expert judgement] In a common Jewish reading, "the satan" here is an angel with the role of accuser in the heavenly council, unlike the image of the devil in some other traditions. A reviewer from the Jewish tradition will confirm this description.
- [Traditional teaching] The Talmud (Bava Batra 15a–16b) records many opinions about when Job lived. One view there holds that Job never existed and the story is a parable; other sages reject this view in the same passage. Either way, the book is studied as teaching.
- Tearing clothes, shaving the head and sitting on the ground were ancient signs of mourning. [Traditional teaching] The Talmud (Moed Katan 28b) teaches that those who come to comfort mourners should not speak words of comfort until the mourner speaks first, and cites this very verse, 2:13, with the opening of chapter 3 ("After this Job opened his mouth").
- "Rent", "mantle", "aught" and "unseemly" are old English for "tore", "cloak", "anything" and "improper".
- This passage speaks of the loss of children. If reading it is too painful, stop and see "Stop practising if".

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any study that evaluates reading the Book of Job, or this week's exercise of "writing a question without answering it".
- [Verified] A review of 47 randomised trials (3,515 people) found that mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount, but are no better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
- [Verified] About 8% of people who meditate report an adverse experience, most often anxiety or depression, according to a review of 83 studies. (Farias et al. 2020) That is why every session ends with "coming back to the ground", and why there is a "Stop practising if" section.

Limits: the evidence above is indirect. Reading Job is learning about a tradition, not treatment.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 35 minutes a day.

1. **Settle (1 minute).** Sit still and take a few slow breaths.
2. **Read slowly (5 minutes).** Days 1–2: read all three passages and "Reading in context". Days 3–7: reread just one passage each day, slowly, pausing for a moment after each verse.
3. **Sit for 25 minutes.**
    - First 5 minutes: notice the breath in the belly.
    - Middle 15 minutes: notice how the body responds to the passage you read (tightness in the chest, heaviness in the shoulders, an urge to get up). Just notice; there is nothing to do with it.
    - Last 5 minutes: recall verse 2:13. Sit quietly as the friends sat with Job: present, without speaking.
4. **Come back to the ground (2 minutes).** Open your eyes, put both feet on the floor, look around and name three things in the room.
5. **Evening note (2 minutes).** One question the reading raises for you, for example "Does Job know why?" or "What would I say if I were his friend?". Do not write an answer.
6. **Mark** your calendar. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**The game "Sitting next to Teddy"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Put a teddy bear (or another toy) on a chair. The adult says: "Teddy is sad today because he broke his favourite cup."
2. Ask the child: "When you are sad, what do you want other people to do?" Let the child answer in their own words.
3. Sit next to Teddy with the child for one minute without speaking, just resting a hand beside him.
4. Ask: "How do you think Teddy feels when we sit quietly with him?" To finish: "Sometimes we don't need to say anything. Sitting next to a friend is enough."

Use only small, everyday sadness (a broken cup, a lost toy, a low mark). Do not tell stories about losing a family member or about illness.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself or of dying.
- The reading brings back a loss of your own so strongly that you cry for days or cannot manage ordinary tasks. If so, stop the course and talk with a trusted friend or a mental health professional.
- You begin to think that your suffering (or a loved one's) is a punishment you deserve. Stop and talk with someone you trust; this course does not teach that.
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for more than 20 minutes.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
