**This week:** slow reading of three short teachings by Shammai. The trait of the week: receiving people warmly.

**Measurable goal:** at least three full greetings a day, on at least 5 of 7 days; once this week, "promise little, do much"; keep the evening notebook on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### Shammai, remembered as strict

[Traditional teaching] Shammai lived at the same time as Hillel, around the turn of the Common Era. The Talmud often sets the schools of the two sages side by side, and the tradition remembers Shammai as stricter than Hillel (see the story of the would-be convert in [Convergence 0, Week 3](/en/convergence/week-03/)). That makes the teaching below all the more striking: the strict teacher is the one who says to receive everyone with a cheerful face.

::: kinh pirkei-avot-gorfinkle nguyen-van | chapter I, 15
Shammai said, "Set a fixed time for thy (study of) Torah; say little and do much; and receive all men with a cheerful countenance."
:::

A note on context: Gorfinkle's footnote says "say little" can also mean "promise little", and points to Abraham in Genesis: he offered his guests only "a morsel of bread", then served a calf "tender and good". [Inference] The three teachings belong together: a steady rhythm of study, fewer words than deeds, and a face that welcomes people. The last one says "all men", not only the people we like or the people who matter. Gorfinkle's footnote number (34) and italics are left out of the quotation.

### Warmth is not pretending

[Inference] The cheerful countenance in this saying is how we *receive others*, not a demand that we always feel cheerful. Someone who is sad can still stop, look at the other person and greet them fully. The Jewish reviewer will say how the sages explain this saying.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not verified any review that measures the effect of a warm greeting as such.
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experimental studies (4,045 people): doing kind things for others raised the doer's own happiness by a small-to-medium amount; the studies were often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 148 studies with 308,849 people: those with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a **correlation**; it does not show that social relationships are the cause. (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010)
- [Inference] A greeting is a very small kindness and a small building block of a relationship, but the research does not measure greetings, and it does not measure Shammai's teaching.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

1. **Morning: slow reading (3 minutes).** Read the three teachings twice. The second time, stop at "all men".
2. **Reminder phrase (1 minute).** "Whoever I meet, I receive fully."
3. **Reflection (8 minutes).** Write a few lines: "Who do I usually greet half-heartedly, or not at all? The last time someone received me warmly, how did I feel?"
4. **During the day: three full greetings.** A full greeting has three parts: stop what you are doing (eyes off the phone), look at the person, say a short sentence, using their name if you know it. Give at least one to someone you rarely notice: the security guard, the delivery driver, a quiet colleague, someone at home just coming in.
5. **Once this week: promise little, do much.** Take on a small task for someone and do a little more than you promised, without saying so in advance.
6. **Evening: trait notebook (5 minutes).** Mark ✓ or ✗, then write two lines: "The greeting I remember most today was …"; "Tomorrow I will fully greet …".

If three greetings feel like a lot, start with one.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Greeting at the door"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The adult and child take turns playing the person coming home and the person at the door.
2. First round: the person at the door keeps working and says hello without looking up. Second round: they stop, look up, smile and say the other's name.
3. Ask the child: "Which time felt better? Why?"
4. Together, choose someone to greet fully tomorrow: a grandparent, a teacher, the guard at school.

Do not make the child greet strangers or anyone they are afraid of. A child has the right to keep their distance from someone who makes them uneasy.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The exercise makes you feel you must hide sadness behind a smile, or wears you out from forcing it. If so, keep only the stopping and looking, and drop the smile.
- The other person has harmed, threatened or harassed you. The exercise does not ask you to be warm towards them; safety comes first.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
