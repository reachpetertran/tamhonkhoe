**This week:** slow reading of the Torah passage on opening your hand to the needy, learning about tzedakah in the Jewish tradition, then setting up a sharing box as a family.

**Measurable goal:** read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; a sharing box at home, with every family member contributing at least once this week (money, a useful item in good condition, or a note naming a way they will help); the family has chosen where to give.

## 1. Traditional teaching

::: kinh jps1917 nguyen-van | Deuteronomy 15:7–11
If there be among you a needy man, one of thy brethren, within any of thy gates, in thy land which the LORD thy God giveth thee, thou shalt not harden thy heart, nor shut thy hand from thy needy brother;

but thou shalt surely open thy hand unto him, and shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need in that which he wanteth.

Beware that there be not a base thought in thy heart, saying: ‘The seventh year, the year of release, is at hand’; and thine eye be evil against thy needy brother, and thou give him nought; and he cry unto the LORD against thee, and it be sin in thee.

Thou shalt surely give him, and thy heart shall not be grieved when thou givest unto him; because that for this thing the LORD thy God will bless thee in all thy work, and in all that thou puttest thy hand unto.

For the poor shall never cease out of the land; therefore I command thee, saying: ‘Thou shalt surely open thy hand unto thy poor and needy brother, in thy land.’
:::

A note on context: chapter 15 is about the "year of release": every seven years a creditor must release what he has lent to his neighbour and brother (15:1–2). Verse 9 warns lenders not to refuse because the year of release is near and they fear losing the money. [Inference] The passage speaks both of the hand ("nor shut thy hand") and of the heart ("harden thy heart", "thy heart shall not be grieved"): giving is not only the hand's work.

### Tzedakah (described)

[Expert judgement] Giving to the poor is called tzedakah in Hebrew, from the same root as tzedek, "justice". So in the Jewish tradition giving to the poor is first of all seen as restoring justice, not only as occasional kindness. Many families keep a tzedakah box at home, and there is a custom of putting something in it before Shabbat begins.

[Traditional teaching] Maimonides (12th century), in his law code Mishneh Torah (the section "Gifts to the Poor", chapter 10), ranks giving in eight levels. In summary, from highest to lowest:

1. Helping someone who is falling into poverty (the original says: a fellow Jew) to stand on their own feet: a gift, a loan, a business partnership or finding them work, so that they do not need to ask.
2. Giving where the giver does not know who receives and the receiver does not know who gave.
3. The giver knows who receives; the receiver does not know who gave.
4. The receiver knows who gave; the giver does not know who receives.
5. Giving before being asked.
6. Giving after being asked.
7. Giving less than is fitting, but with a pleasant face.
8. Giving with a sour heart.

[Inference] The ranking places two things high: helping the other person stand on their own, and protecting the receiver's dignity.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A survey, a follow-up and a randomised experiment: spending money on others went with, and in the experiment raised, the giver's happiness. The survey part is a correlation; the sums in the experiment were small; one research group. (Dunn et al. 2008)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experiments (4,045 people) found that doing kind acts for others raises the doer's own well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
- [Inference] These studies measure how the giver feels, not the benefit to the receiver. In the Jewish tradition tzedakah is a duty of justice, not a way for the giver to feel good.

## 3. Practice for adults

Five minutes of reading each morning, two minutes of looking back each evening; 15 minutes at the start of the week to make the sharing box.

1. **Read slowly (5 minutes, morning).** Read Deuteronomy 15:7–11 twice. Notice two images: the closed hand and the open hand.
2. **Make a sharing box (start of the week, whole family).** A box or jar with a lid, decorated by the children. Put it where everyone can see it.
3. **Contribute.** Everyone contributes at least once this week. It can be small change, a useful item in good condition, or a note naming something you will do for someone (for example: "an hour reading to the elderly lady next door"). Suggestion: contribute at a fixed time, for example before the shared rest period from Week 1.
4. **Choose where to give (end of the week, whole family).** Together choose a place the family knows well (someone you know who is struggling, a charity with clear reports, a food kitchen nearby). Ask: which way helps people stand on their own, and protects their dignity?
5. **Look back (2 minutes, evening).** One line: where did my hand close today, and where did it open?

**Very important.** This exercise is not financial advice. A small amount, or only contributions of time, is fine; do not borrow or cut the family's essential spending to contribute. Be careful with online appeals for donations that you cannot verify.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "The sharing box"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The child decorates the family box: drawing two open hands.
2. The child chooses one thing to give: a book they have finished, a toy in good condition that they really want to give away, or a drawing for someone. The child chooses; the adult does not push.
3. The adult asks: "If you were the child receiving this, how would you like to be given it?" (for example: not teased, not reminded of it later).
4. At the end of the week, if possible, the child comes along when the family gives.

Do not take children to unsafe places, and do not photograph the people receiving help to post online.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The exercise makes you feel guilty or blame yourself for "not giving enough". If so, drop the money part and keep only the reading.
- Your family is going through money difficulties. If so, give time instead of money, or skip this part.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
