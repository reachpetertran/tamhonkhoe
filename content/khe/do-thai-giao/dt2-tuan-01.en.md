**This week:** getting to know Mussar, the Jewish tradition of training character, through Ben Zoma's four questions in Pirkei Avot. The trait of the week: learning from everyone.

**Measurable goal:** on day 1, rate the course's seven traits; write down one thing learned from a specific person on at least 5 of 7 days; keep the evening notebook on at least 5 of 7 days.

**How to use this course:** Judaism 2 goes through seven character traits (middot), one a week, through short passages from Pirkei Avot and the book Mesillat Yesharim ("The Path of the Just"). Week 8 is a review. It is best taken after [Judaism 1](/en/courses/judaism-1/), but that is not required.

## What this course is and is not

- The course **describes** what the Mussar tradition teaches about character, through the tradition's own texts. You do not need to be Jewish.
- The course **does not teach** Jewish law (halakha) or ritual, does not teach Hebrew, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs.
- The weekly exercises (slow morning reading, one small act during the day, an evening notebook) are tamhonkhoe's own, adapted from the "one trait a week" approach of Mussar books. They are not ritual, and they do not replace study with a teacher (rabbi), a study partner or a Mussar group in a community.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### What Mussar is

[Traditional teaching] The Hebrew word *mussar* means instruction or correction; Rabbi Yosef Sebag's translation of Mesillat Yesharim keeps the word with the gloss *instruction*. In Judaism, Mussar is the tradition of studying and practising to train character traits, in Hebrew *middot* (singular *middah*), such as patience, humility, contentment and diligence.

[Verified] According to the book descriptions in the Sefaria library:

- [Mesillat Yesharim](https://www.sefaria.org/Mesillat_Yesharim) by Rabbi Moshe Chaim Luzzatto (1707–1746) was written and published in Amsterdam (earliest manuscript 1738, printed 1740). It goes step by step through the stages of the moral life, and has been studied in almost every yeshiva since the Mussar movement of Rabbi Yisrael Salanter brought the study of Mussar books into the curriculum.
- [Cheshbon HaNefesh](https://www.sefaria.org/Cheshbon_HaNefesh) ("Accounting of the soul") by Menachem Mendel Lefin, printed in 1808, works on 13 traits, one a week, repeating the cycle four times a year. Lefin modelled it on Benjamin Franklin's method, without naming him. Rabbi Yisrael Salanter, known as the father of the Mussar movement, encouraged an 1845 edition.

[Inference] In the tradition, training character is not an end in itself: chapter 1 of Mesillat Yesharim opens by asking what a person's duty in the world is, and places the training of traits within the service of God. This course does not cut the traits off from that setting; only the exercises are meant for everyone.

### Ben Zoma's four questions

[Traditional teaching] Ben Zoma was a second-century sage, remembered for his great learning. His saying in Pirkei Avot asks four questions, each turning an ordinary idea upside down.

::: kinh pirkei-avot-gorfinkle nguyen-van | chapter IV, 1
Ben Zoma said, "Who is wise? He who learns from all men, as it is said, 'from all my teachers have I gotten understanding'. Who is mighty? He who controls his passions, as it is said, 'He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty, and he that ruleth over his spirit than he that taketh a city'. Who is rich? He who rejoices in his portion, as it is said, 'When thou eatest the labor of thine hands, happy art thou, and it shall be well with thee'; happy art thou in this world, and it shall be well with thee in the world to come. Who is honored? He who honors others, as it is said, 'For them that honor me I will honor, and they that despise me shall be held in contempt'".
:::

A note on context: each answer comes with a verse of Scripture as a proof text, a familiar way of teaching among the sages. [Inference] For Ben Zoma, wisdom is measured not by how much you know but by whom you can still learn from; strength is measured not by defeating others but by mastering yourself. This week practises only the first question. The fourth (honouring others) returns in Week 4; the third (rejoicing in one's portion) is in [Convergence 0, Week 2](/en/convergence/week-02/). Gorfinkle's footnote numbers (1)–(5) are left out of the quotation.

### How the course works

[Expert judgement] The course borrows three common features of Mussar practice: choosing one trait for a period of time, reminding yourself of it every day, and each evening "keeping accounts" of how you lived with it. Each week has:

| When | What | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Morning | Slow reading of the week's passage and the reminder phrase | 3–5 minutes |
| Morning or midday | Writing a reflection on the week's question | 8–10 minutes |
| During the day | One small act of the week's trait | as needed |
| Evening | Trait notebook: a tick or cross, two lines | 5 minutes |

The course's seven traits: learning from everyone (Week 1), greeting people warmly (Week 2), not judging quickly (Week 3), honouring others (Week 4), watchfulness (Week 5), zeal (Week 6), humility (Week 7).

## 2. What science finds

- We have not verified any study that evaluates a Mussar programme as such.
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies with 19,951 people found that interventions helping people monitor their progress made them more likely to reach their goals, by a small-to-medium amount (d = 0.40). The effect was larger when progress was physically recorded or reported to others. (Harkin et al. 2016)
- [Inference] The course's evening notebook is one such way of monitoring. The research measured reaching behavioural goals (exercise, eating, study…), not character or moral life.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

1. **Day 1: rate yourself (5 minutes).** Copy the seven traits above onto a page of your notebook. Rate each from 0 to 10 as honestly as you can right now. Don't polish it; you will rate them again in Week 8.
2. **Morning: slow reading (3 minutes).** Read Ben Zoma's saying twice. The second time, stop at "He who learns from all men".
3. **Reminder phrase (1 minute).** Write on a slip of paper or your lock screen: "Today I learn from everyone."
4. **Reflection (8 minutes).** Write a few lines: "Who is someone I rarely think I could learn from? What does that person do better than me?" Think of a different person each day: someone younger, someone whose work you seldom notice, someone you dislike.
5. **During the day.** Notice and learn one specific thing from one person: a way of speaking, a skill, a way of treating people.
6. **Evening: trait notebook (5 minutes).** Mark ✓ or ✗ for the day's act, then write two lines: "Today I learned from … that …"; "Tomorrow I will notice …".

If you miss a day, carry on the next day; do not make it up.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "My child, my teacher"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The child chooses something they can do and want to teach: folding a paper animal, the names of some dinosaurs, how to build a model, a game from school.
2. The adult becomes a real pupil: listens, asks questions, copies, and when they get it wrong asks the child to show them.
3. At the end the adult says it out loud: "Today I learned from you …"
4. Ask the child: "What did you learn from someone today?" They might talk about a friend, a teacher, a younger child.

No scores, and no correcting how the child teaches. What the child needs to see is that adults can learn from children too.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Rating yourself or the evening notebook leaves you self-critical or feeling worthless for several days in a row. If so, drop the numbers and just write one good thing you did that day.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
