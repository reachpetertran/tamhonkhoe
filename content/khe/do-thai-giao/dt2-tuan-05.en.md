**This week:** starting the book Mesillat Yesharim, "The Path of the Just", with its first step. The trait of the week: watchfulness, in Hebrew *zehirut*.

**Measurable goal:** pause to consider before something done out of habit at least three times a day, on at least 5 of 7 days; keep the evening notebook on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### Rabbi Pinchas ben Yair's ladder

[Traditional teaching] In his introduction, Rabbi Moshe Chaim Luzzatto explains that he built the whole book on an ancient teaching quoted in several places in the Talmud, including tractate Avodah Zarah (20b). This teaching of Rabbi Pinchas ben Yair arranges the traits as a ladder, each step leading to the next: Torah leads to watchfulness, watchfulness to zeal, zeal to cleanliness, and on to higher steps that include humility, fear of sin and holiness. Each chapter of the book explains one step: what it is, what it consists of, how to acquire it and what gets in its way.

This course reads only three steps: watchfulness (this week), zeal (Week 6) and humility (Week 7). The passages below are quoted from Rabbi Yosef Sebag's English translation.

### What watchfulness is

::: kinh mesillat-sebag nguyen-van | chapter 2, segment 1
The idea of watchfulness is for one to be cautious of his deeds and matters, namely, contemplating and watching over his deeds and ways whether they are good or evil; not abandoning his soul to the danger of destruction, G-d forbid, and not walking through the course of habit like a blind man in darkness.
:::

::: kinh mesillat-sebag nguyen-van | chapter 2, segment 7
The explanation is that they would pursue and go by the momentum of habit and conduct, without leaving themselves time to consider their deeds and ways. Thus they fell into evil without even seeing it.
:::

A note on context: segment 7 explains the verse from Jeremiah that Luzzatto has just quoted, about people "each one running to his own course, as the horse rushes into the battle". Straight after (segments 8–10), he writes that the "evil inclination" (yetzer hara) tries to keep people's hearts constantly busy so that they have no room to reflect, and compares this with Pharaoh's order to make the people's labour heavier. [Inference] For Luzzatto, the enemy of watchfulness is not only bad intentions but busyness and habit: acting without time to ask what we are doing.

[Inference] Watchfulness here is not anxiety or doubting everything. It is the habit of pausing long enough to see what we are about to do. The Jewish reviewer will say how the tradition distinguishes watchfulness from excessive fear. Segment numbers follow Sefaria's division of the text.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not verified any study that evaluates practice based on Mesillat Yesharim as such.
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 138 randomised studies with 19,951 people: monitoring one's own progress made people more likely to reach their goals, by a small-to-medium amount (d = 0.40), more so when it was written down. (Harkin et al. 2016)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 33 studies on self-control training: a small effect (g = 0.30), and about 0.13–0.24 after correcting for publication bias. (Friese et al. 2017)
- [Inference] Together these suggest modest expectations: writing things down and paying attention help somewhat, but a week of practice does not bring large change. The research does not measure moral life.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 20 minutes a day.

1. **Morning: slow reading (3 minutes).** Read both passages. The second time, stop at "like a blind man in darkness".
2. **Reminder phrase (1 minute).** "Before I act, I look at what I am doing."
3. **Reflection (8 minutes).** Days 1 and 2: list five things you do every day almost without thinking (picking up the phone as soon as you wake, replying to messages when annoyed, eating while working, saying "later"…). Circle three of them. On the following days, write about one of the three: "Does this help me, or harm me and others?"
4. **During the day: three pause points.** Before each of the circled actions, pause for one breath and ask: "Is this good or not good, right now?" Then choose: do it, put it off, or do it differently. You do not always have to choose "don't"; what you are practising is seeing that you are choosing.
5. **Evening: trait notebook (5 minutes).** Write how many pauses you made (0–3), then two lines: "Once I paused and saw …"; "Once I drifted along with habit was …".

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Traffic lights"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Draw three circles on paper: red, yellow and green.
2. The adult describes a small situation: "Your little brother knocked over your Lego tower", "A friend grabbed your pen". The child points to red and says "Stop", to yellow and says "Think: what would be good to do?", then to green and says what they will do.
3. Swap roles: the child describes a situation and the adult plays.
4. Ask the child: "Was there a time today when you managed to stop at the red light?"

Do not use the game to scold the child for the times they could not stop.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Considering your actions turns into checking and rechecking, fear of having done wrong or being at fault, that you cannot stop. That is not watchfulness; stop the exercise and talk to a doctor or psychologist.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
