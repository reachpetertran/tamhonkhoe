**This week:** slow reading of a teaching about the open house, learning about the table in the Jewish tradition, then two shared family meals without screens. Within the month, invite someone to eat with you.

**Measurable goal:** read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; at least 2 shared meals this week, without screens, with each person saying one sentence; within the next 4 weeks, invite someone from outside the household to eat with you once.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### The open house

::: kinh pirkei-avot-gorfinkle nguyen-van | chapter I, 5
Jose, the son of Jochanan, of Jerusalem said, "Let thy house be open wide; let the poor be members of thy household […]"
:::

A note on context: Jose, the son of Jochanan, is one of the early "pairs" of teachers named in chapter I of Pirkei Avot. His saying has a second clause on another subject, and an explanation added by later sages; they are left out here (marked […]). [Inference] The quoted words do not only say "feed the poor"; they say "let them be members of thy household": the poor guest sits at the table like family, rather than standing at the door to receive a portion.

[Traditional teaching] Welcoming guests is called hachnasat orchim in Hebrew ("bringing in guests"). The tradition often points to Abraham, sitting at the door of his tent and running to meet three strangers (Genesis 18:1–8), as its model.

### The table in the Jewish tradition (described)

- [Traditional teaching] Grace after meals (Birkat Hamazon) is usually grounded in Deuteronomy 8:10, "thou shalt eat and be satisfied, and bless the LORD thy God"; that verse is quoted in [Body 1 – Week 1](/en/body/body-1-week-01/).
- [Traditional teaching] Pirkei Avot (chapter III, 4 in Gorfinkle's edition; 3:3 in many editions) records Rabbi Simeon's teaching: when three have eaten at one table and spoken words of Torah there, it is as if they had eaten at the table of God. Only this half of the teaching is summarised here.
- [Expert judgement] For this reason the Jewish tradition often sees the family table as a small altar: a place for good words, singing, stories and guests.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 17 studies (182,836 children and adolescents) found that 3 or more family meals a week went with healthier eating and less disordered eating. This is a correlation. (Hammons & Fiese 2011)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 people): people with stronger social relationships were more likely to survive over the follow-up period (OR = 1.50). This is a correlation. (Holt-Lunstad et al. 2010)
- [Inference] We cannot say that shared meals are the cause: families who eat together often may differ in many other ways. This week's exercise rests on the tradition's teaching, not on a health promise.

## 3. Practice for adults

Five minutes of reading each morning, two minutes of looking back each evening; two shared meals this week.

1. **Read slowly (5 minutes, morning).** Read Pirkei Avot I, 5 twice. Ask: "Who is my door open to, and who is it closed to?"
2. **Choose two meals (start of the week).** The family chooses two shared meals this week. Before the meal, put phones in one place and switch off the TV.
3. **One sentence each.** During the meal, each person in turn says one sentence: something good that happened, something learned, or someone to thank. Nobody is forced to speak; anyone can say "next time".
4. **A thank-you when the meal ends.** Before getting up, each person says or thinks a thank-you in their own words (to the cook, the growers, whoever clears up).
5. **Invite a guest.** Within the next 4 weeks, invite someone to eat with you: someone who lives alone, a colleague far from home, a new neighbour. A simple dish is enough.
6. **Look back (2 minutes, evening).** One line: which words at today's meal stayed with me?

**Very important.** The table is not a place to talk about weight or dieting, or to criticise how much someone eats. Do not push anyone to finish their plate or eat more. If someone at home is having difficulties with eating, ask them first before changing the family's mealtime habits.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "One sentence each"** (5–10 minutes, during a meal, with an adult)

1. Put a small object in the middle of the table as the "talking stick" (a wooden spoon, a small ball).
2. Whoever holds the "talking stick" tells one happy thing from the day; the others listen without interrupting.
3. An adult goes first as an example, short and cheerful.
4. The child can tell it, draw it on paper, or pass. End with a thank-you to the cook.

Do not use this game to make the child talk about school things they do not want to share.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Shared meals regularly turn into arguments. If so, keep them shorter, choose light topics, or pause the "one sentence each" part.
- You or someone at home feel a lot of anxiety or tension around eating. If so, stop the mealtime part and talk with a doctor or a professional.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
