**This week:** slow reading of the short chapter that Muslims recite most often: Al-Fatihah, "The Opening".

**Measurable goal:** read the seven verses slowly and write one line, on at least 5 of 7 days.

**How to use this course:** Islam 1 reads six short passages and describes what the Islamic tradition teaches about gratitude, patience and small good deeds done regularly. You do not need to be Muslim. The course does not teach ritual, does not teach Arabic recitation, and does not ask anyone to change their beliefs. Muslims taking the course should treat it as extra reading, not a substitute for learning with a teacher in their own community.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### The Qur'an and translations of its meaning

[Traditional teaching] The Islamic tradition teaches that the Qur'an is the word of Allah revealed to the Prophet Muhammad in Arabic. For this reason Muslims generally regard only the Arabic text as the Qur'an; every Vietnamese or English version is a translation of its meaning, that is, one translator's understanding.

The English pages of this course quote Marmaduke Pickthall's translation (1930, in the public domain). The Vietnamese pages quote Hasan Abdulkarim's translation on QuranEnc.com word for word, because its licence allows republishing but not changes.

### Al-Fatihah

[Traditional teaching] Al-Fatihah is chapter 1 of the Qur'an. This tradition teaches that Muslims recite it in every unit of the Salah prayer, so someone who keeps all five daily prayers recites it many times a day. The chapter opens with praise, moves to the declaration that Allah alone is worshipped, and ends with a request to be guided on the straight path.

::: kinh quran-pickthall nguyen-van | Qur'an 1:1–7
In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful.  
Praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds,  
The Beneficent, the Merciful.  
Master of the Day of Judgment,  
Thee (alone) we worship; Thee (alone) we ask for help.  
Show us the straight path,  
The path of those whom Thou hast favoured; Not the (path) of those who earn Thine anger nor of those who go astray.
:::

### Reading in context

- Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955 (the "P:" lines). Words in brackets are Pickthall's. His "Thee" and "Thou" are old English forms of "you".
- The two names "the Beneficent, the Merciful" (ar-Rahman, ar-Rahim) appear in the first verse. [Traditional teaching] This first verse ("In the name of Allah…"), called the Basmalah, also opens almost every other chapter of the Qur'an, and many Muslims say it before starting a task.
- Verse 7 mentions "those who earn Thine anger" and "those who go astray". Commentators understand this verse in different ways. The course does not go into these readings; if you want to learn more, ask a teacher within the tradition.
- The Vietnamese page includes the translator's own footnotes, as its licence requires the text to be kept whole.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any study that evaluates slow reading of Al-Fatihah itself.
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 97 studies (7,181 people) on psychotherapy that integrates the client's religious faith found that this integration reduced distress as much as standard therapy, and increased spiritual well-being. (Captari et al. 2018)
- [Verified] A patient-level meta-analysis of three trials (266 people in Turkey, Pakistan and the UK) compared a faith-adapted depression therapy for Muslims (BA-M) with standard treatment. The BA-M group's PHQ-9 depression scores fell by about 2.7 points more, and they stayed in treatment more. (Mir et al. 2026)
- [Inference] Both are therapies led by professionals, for people with mental health problems and usually for believers. Reading on your own for 10 minutes a day has not been tested; any benefit is only inferred.

Limits: the study by Mir et al. is still small. Reading scripture is learning about a tradition, not treatment.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 10–12 minutes a day, the same passage all week.

1. **Settle (1 minute).** Sit still and take a few slow breaths.
2. **Read slowly (3 minutes).** Read the seven verses once. Read them a second time very slowly, pausing for a moment after each verse. On the first day also read the notes above.
3. **Choose one word (2 minutes).** Notice a word or phrase that makes you stop, for example "Merciful", "help", "the straight path".
4. **Write one line (3 minutes).** Write: "Today the word … reminds me of …". It need not be right or beautiful, only honest.
5. **Sit quietly (1–2 minutes).** No need to think further.
6. **Mark** your calendar. The goal is at least 5 of 7 days.

Muslims can do step 2 in the way they are used to. Those who are not Muslim read it as one reads a classic text: to understand, not to pray on someone else's behalf.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Walking the straight path"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Stick a straight line of tape about 3 metres long on the floor (or lay out a row of slippers).
2. The child puts a small ball or a sweet on a spoon and walks slowly along the tape from one end to the other without dropping it.
3. Try once more, walking very fast. Ask: "Is it easier to keep your balance walking slowly or quickly?"
4. The adult says: "Some things we do better when we go slowly and pay attention to each step."

Play only on a flat floor: no running, no walking on chairs or high steps. Muslim families can read verse 6 of the chapter to their child. Other families just play the balancing game.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Verses about judgement leave you frightened or feeling guilty for a long time. If so, stop and talk with a trusted friend, a teacher in your community, or a mental health professional.
- Taking this course causes tension in your family or with people around you about belief. You can pause; learning here is voluntary.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
