**This week:** read two verses of the Qur'an on how to treat parents in their old age. The exercise is small acts for a parent or an older person in the family: asking after them, helping with one task, speaking one respectful word.

**Measurable goal:** read slowly on at least 5 of 7 days; ask after a parent or an older person at least 3 times (a call, a visit, sitting and talking), at least 10 minutes each, without doing anything else at the same time; one specific act of help in the week; one line in your notebook each evening.

## 1. Traditional teaching

### When parents grow old

[Traditional teaching] In the Qur'an, the teaching to be kind to parents often comes straight after the teaching to worship Allah alone. The two verses below are in chapter 17 (*Al-Isra*).

::: kinh quran-pickthall nguyen-van | Qur'an 17:23–24
Thy Lord hath decreed, that ye worship none save Him, and (that ye show) kindness to parents. If one of them or both of them attain old age with thee, say not "Fie" unto them nor repulse them, but speak unto them a gracious word.  
And lower unto them the wing of submission through mercy, and say: My Lord! Have mercy on them both as they did care for me when I was little.
:::

### Reading in context

- Pickthall's translation, from Project Gutenberg #16955.
- "Fie" is a small sound of irritation. [Inference] The scripture reaches even the smallest words, not only open insults.
- Verse 24 uses the image of "lowering the wing", like a mother bird spreading her wing over her young: when parents grow old, the child becomes the one who shelters.
- [Traditional teaching] The tradition also teaches that respect for parents does not include obeying them when told to do wrong (Qur'an 31:15 speaks of parents who press their child to act against the faith, and still teaches kindness towards them). The lesson only gives the idea, without quoting.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 27 experiments, 4,045 people: acts of kindness to others raise the doer's well-being by a small-to-moderate amount; the studies are often small. (Curry et al. 2018)
- [Inference] The research measures kindness in general, not caring for parents. Long-term care of an elderly parent can be very heavy; this week's exercise is only a few small acts.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 15 minutes a day, plus the visits or calls.

1. **Morning: slow reading (3 minutes).** Read 17:23–24.
2. **Three times asking after someone (in the week, at least 10 minutes each).** Call or visit a parent, grandparent or older relative. Put other phones away and do nothing else at the same time. Ask one specific question: "How have you been sleeping this week?", "What were you doing when you were my age?"
3. **One specific act of help (once in the week).** Ask first what they need: fixing something broken, taking them to an appointment, tidying a corner of the house, setting up their phone again. Do what they need, not what you think they need.
4. **Hold back one small word.** Choose one sigh or habitual sharp remark you use with an older person at home, and replace it with something calm.
5. **Evening: write one line (2 minutes).** "What did I learn today about my parents (or an older person in my life)?"

If your parents have died, or you cannot contact them: write a short paragraph about one good thing they did for you, and give the three times of asking after someone to another older person (a grandparent, an aunt or uncle, a neighbour who lives alone).

**Very important.** This lesson does not ask you to be close to, ask after or obey anyone who has harmed you or is harming you. If your relationship with your parents is not safe, choose another older person, or do only the writing.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

This week uses the activity **"A good deed for someone at home"**: the child chooses one small thing to do for a grandparent, parent or sibling during the day. See the full activity at [Kids' corner – Activity 6](/en/kids-corner/activity-06/).

The adult can say: "When you were tiny, we carried you and fed you. When grandma and grandpa grow old, we look after them. That's how our family takes care of each other."

## 5. Stop practising if

- Asking after a parent stirs up painful family history and you feel it is too much. Stop the visits or calls, keep only the reading and writing, and find someone to talk to.
- You are caring for an elderly parent or a relative who has been ill for a long time and feel exhausted, sleepless or very irritable. This lesson does not ask you to do more; ask others to share the work.
- You feel panic, have flashbacks to trauma, several nights of poor sleep, or thoughts of harming yourself. Stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
