**This week:** practise recognising five states that often get in the way of sitting, which the tradition calls the five hindrances. Sit for 25 minutes a day, and for 30 minutes from day four; note which "guest" visits most.

**Measurable goal:** sit for at least 25 minutes on 5 of 7 days; at the end of the week, write down which hindrance visited most and one thing that helped it settle.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The fourth place in the Discourse on Mindfulness Meditation is principles (*dhammā*). This is the longest part of the discourse, with five groups: the five hindrances, the five aggregates, the six sense fields, the seven awakening factors and the four noble truths. This week covers only the first group. For each hindrance, the discourse teaches four things: knowing whether it is present, knowing how it arises, knowing how it is given up, and knowing how it does not arise again.

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, sections 36.2 and 36.4 (sensual desire)
It’s when a mendicant meditates by observing an aspect of principles with respect to the five hindrances.

It’s when a mendicant who has sensual desire in them understands: ‘I have sensual desire in me.’ When they don’t have sensual desire in them, they understand: ‘I don’t have sensual desire in me.’ They understand how sensual desire arises; how, when it’s already arisen, it’s given up; and how, once it’s given up, it doesn’t arise again in the future.
:::

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, section 36.5 (ill will)
When they have ill will in them, they understand: ‘I have ill will in me.’ When they don’t have ill will in them, they understand: ‘I don’t have ill will in me.’ They understand how ill will arises; how, when it’s already arisen, it’s given up; and how, once it’s given up, it doesn’t arise again in the future.
:::

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, section 36.7 (restlessness and remorse)
When they have restlessness and remorse in them, they understand: ‘I have restlessness and remorse in me.’ When they don’t have restlessness and remorse in them, they understand: ‘I don’t have restlessness and remorse in me.’ They understand how restlessness and remorse arise; how, when they’ve already arisen, they’re given up; and how, once they’re given up, they don’t arise again in the future.
:::

The other two hindrances, dullness and drowsiness (section 36.6) and doubt (section 36.8), are described in exactly the same pattern.

### Reading it in context

- "Principles" here does not simply mean "the teaching". Bhikkhu Sujato translates *dhammā* as "principles"; other translators use "phenomena" or "mental qualities". Scholars read this word in different ways.
- The traditional names are sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and remorse, and doubt. This course uses everyday words to keep it simple.
- The discourse treats knowing that a hindrance "is present" as part of meditation, not a sign of sitting wrongly.
- The other four groups of "principles" are kept for later courses: the four truths were covered in [Buddhism 1 – Week 2](/en/traditions/buddhism/buddhism-1-week-02/); the five aggregates belong to Buddhism 3.
- Doubt here means the hesitation that stalls practice, not asking questions. [Buddhism 1 – Week 6](/en/traditions/buddhism/buddhism-1-week-06/) reads a discourse that encourages testing for yourself.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A review of 47 randomised trials (3,515 people) found that mindfulness meditation reduced anxiety and depression by small to moderate amounts, and was not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
- [Verified] About 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience (review of 83 studies); it can happen to people with no history of mental health problems. (Farias et al. 2020)
- [Inference] We have not found any research synthesis on contemplating the five hindrances. There is no direct evidence for this week.

Limits: these figures are averages across many studies. Sitting longer is not better for everyone.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 25–35 minutes a day.

1. **Slow reading (3 minutes).** Read the three passages.
2. **Sit with the breath (10 minutes).**
3. **Recognise the guest (12–17 minutes).** When sitting gets hard, ask: "Which guest is visiting?" The five guests: *wanting* (wanting food, your phone, something pleasant); *irritation* (annoyed at someone, at noise, at sitting itself); *dullness* (heavy eyelids, a slow mind); *restlessness* (wanting to get up, regret over the past); *doubt* ("what's the point of this?"). Name the guest, then return to the breath.
4. **Four questions (after sitting, 2 minutes).** For today’s most frequent guest: When did it come? What fed it? What helped it settle? What could make it visit less tomorrow? Write one line.
5. **Some common approaches.** [Expert judgement] Dullness: open your eyes, sit up straighter, or alternate standing and sitting. Restlessness: make the out-breath longer than the in-breath a few times. If you are drowsy because you are genuinely short of sleep, the best remedy is sleep.
6. **Tick the day.** Write down your minutes of sitting and the main guest each day.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Five guests"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Draw five animals on five sheets of paper: Craving Cat (wanting), Grumpy Hedgehog (irritation), Sleepy Bear (dullness), Fidgety Squirrel (restlessness), Wondering Owl (doubt).
2. Describe a situation, for example: "You're doing homework but you keep wanting to run off and watch cartoons. Which guest is visiting?"
3. The child picks an animal. Think together of a way to see the guest out: for Craving Cat, "finish homework, then watch"; for Sleepy Bear, "stand up and stretch".
4. The adult tells of a time a guest visited them.

Do not use this game to criticise the child ("you're being Grumpy Hedgehog again"). Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: [keeping promises](https://daynentang.com/khoa-hoc/gia-dinh-2-8/tuan-10/g10-giu-loi-hua/) (in Vietnamese).

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Sitting longer makes anxiety, restlessness or regret grow and it does not ease when you return to the breath or open your eyes. If so, go back to 15–20 minutes.
- If there are serious warning signs, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
