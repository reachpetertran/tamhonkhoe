**This week:** mindfulness of the body is not only for sitting. Each day, walk slowly for 10 minutes, sit for 15 minutes feeling the body through four elements (firmness, moisture, warmth, movement), and choose one routine task to do with awareness.

**Measurable goal:** walk slowly for 10 minutes and sit for 15 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; do one routine task with awareness each day (for example brushing your teeth or washing up) on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] After the breath, the Discourse on Mindfulness Meditation lists several more ways of observing the body. This week we read three: knowing your posture, being aware in every activity, and seeing the body in terms of four elements. The tradition teaches that mindfulness should be present all day, not only on the cushion.

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, sections 6.1–6.2 (the postures)
Furthermore, when a mendicant is walking they know: ‘I am walking.’ When standing they know: ‘I am standing.’ When sitting they know: ‘I am sitting.’ And when lying down they know: ‘I am lying down.’ Whatever posture their body is in, they know it.
:::

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, section 8.1 (situational awareness)
Furthermore, a mendicant acts with situational awareness when going out and coming back; when looking ahead and aside; when bending and extending the limbs; when bearing the outer robe, bowl and robes; when eating, drinking, chewing, and tasting; when urinating and defecating; when walking, standing, sitting, sleeping, waking, speaking, and keeping silent.
:::

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, sections 12.1–12.2 (the elements)
Furthermore, a mendicant examines their own body, whatever its placement or posture, according to the elements: ‘In this body there is the earth element, the water element, the fire element, and the air element.’
:::

### Reading it in context

- The list of activities includes going to the toilet. In this tradition, no task is too ordinary to be done mindfully.
- The four elements are a traditional way of looking at the body: earth is what is solid, water what is moist and binding, fire warmth, air movement. This is a way of contemplating in meditation, not a modern biological explanation.
- The section on the body has two more contemplations: examining 31 parts of the body (head hair, body hair, nails, teeth…) as unattractive, and comparing one’s body to a corpse at stages of decay. [Traditional teaching] The tradition uses them to reduce attachment to the body. This course does not practise them, because they can be strongly upsetting, do not suit people struggling with body image or eating or who are grieving, and in the tradition are often learned with a teacher.
- Leaving those two parts out is a choice of this self-study course, not a judgement that they are wrong.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 29 studies of the MBSR programme (2,668 healthy adults) found that the programme reduced stress, anxiety and depression by a moderate amount. The studies varied a lot, so it is not known which parts of the programme have an effect. (Khoury et al. 2015)
- [Verified] A 2018 critical review pointed out that many mindfulness studies rely on self-report questionnaires and lack good control groups. (Van Dam et al. 2018)
- [Inference] We have not found a research synthesis specifically on walking meditation or on contemplating the four elements. There is no direct evidence for each exercise this week.

Limits: these figures are averages across many studies and do not predict what will happen for any one person.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 25 minutes a day, plus one routine task.

1. **Slow reading (2 minutes).** Read the three passages.
2. **Slow walking (10 minutes).** Choose a path of 10–15 steps indoors or in a yard. Walk more slowly than usual; know the foot lifting, moving forward, being placed down. At the end of the path, stop, know "standing", then turn back. Look at the ground ahead, not at your feet.
3. **Sitting with the four elements (15 minutes).** Spend the first few minutes with the breath. Then about 2–3 minutes for each element: where the body meets the chair or floor (firmness: earth); saliva in the mouth (moisture: water); warmth in the hands (warmth: fire); the belly rising and falling with the breath (movement: air). End by knowing the whole body sitting.
4. **One routine task.** Choose something you do every day, such as brushing your teeth. Do it while knowing each movement: picking up, lifting, brushing, rinsing. When your mind moves elsewhere, come back to what you are doing.
5. **Tick the day.** Write down the minutes of walking and sitting, and whether you did your routine task with awareness.

If walking is not easy for you, you can sit and notice your hand lifting and lowering instead of slow walking.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Walk like a cat"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The adult and child walk very slowly and softly together, like a cat that does not want to wake anyone.
2. The adult whispers: "Lift… forward… down." Walk about 10 steps, then stop.
3. Stand still like a statue and count to 5. Ask: "Does the floor feel cool or warm under your feet? Hard or soft?"
4. Swap roles: the child is the "mother cat" who leads.

Clear things off the floor before you play. If the child wants to run and jump, let them, and play again later. Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: [Walking slowly, eating slowly](https://daynentang.com/khoa-hoc/gia-dinh-2-8/tuan-9/g9-di-cham-an-cham/) (in Vietnamese).

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Paying attention to sensations in the body brings growing discomfort or dizziness that does not ease when you open your eyes and look around the room.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
