**This week:** read three passages of the discourse to the Kālāmas (AN 3.65) slowly, sit with the breath for 5 minutes a day, and use your own notes from the past five weeks to see which habits really help you.

**Measurable goal:** write down one belief you have tested in your own experience, with what you recorded; rate the five habits and choose two to keep.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The Kesamutti Sutta, also called the Kālāma Sutta, belongs to the Numbered Discourses (Aṅguttara Nikāya). The Kālāmas of the town of Kesamutta were confused because many teachers passed through, each praising his own doctrine and disparaging the others. The Buddha does not tell them simply to believe him; he gives them a way to examine things for themselves.

::: kinh an3-65-sujato nguyen-van | AN 3.65, sections 3.1–4.3
“There are, sir, some ascetics and brahmins who come to Kesamutta.
They explain and promote only their own doctrine, while they attack, badmouth, disparage, and smear the doctrines of others.
Then some other ascetics and brahmins come to Kesamutta.
They too explain and promote only their own doctrine, while they attack, badmouth, disparage, and smear the doctrines of others.
So, sir, we’re doubting and uncertain:
‘I wonder who of these respected ascetics and brahmins speaks the truth, and who speaks falsehood?’”

“It is enough, Kālāmas, for you to be doubting and uncertain.
Doubt has come up in you about an uncertain matter.

Please, Kālāmas, don’t go by oral transmission, don’t go by lineage, don’t go by testament, don’t go by canonical authority, don’t rely on logic, don’t rely on inference, don’t go by reasoned train of thought, don’t go by the acceptance of a view after deliberation, don’t go by the appearance of competence, and don’t think ‘The ascetic is our respected teacher.’
But when you know for yourselves:
‘These things are unskillful, blameworthy, criticized by sensible people, and when you undertake them, they lead to harm and suffering’, then you should give them up.
:::

::: kinh an3-65-sujato nguyen-van | AN 3.65, sections 26.2–26.3
But when you know for yourselves:
‘These things are skillful, blameless, praised by sensible people, and when you undertake them, they lead to welfare and happiness’, then you should acquire them and keep them.
:::

Near the end of the discourse, the Buddha speaks of someone whose heart is free of enmity, and of the consolations they have whether or not there is a life to come:

::: kinh an3-65-sujato nguyen-van | AN 3.65, sections 43.1–44.1
When that noble disciple has a mind that’s free of enmity and ill will, uncorrupted and purified,
they’ve won four consolations in this very life.
‘If it turns out there is another world, and good and bad deeds have a result, then—when the body breaks up, after death—I’ll be reborn in a good place, a heavenly realm.’ This is the first consolation they’ve won.
‘If it turns out there is no other world, and good and bad deeds don’t have a result, then in this very life I’ll keep myself free of enmity and ill will, untroubled and happy.’ This is the second consolation they’ve won.
:::

### Reading it in context

- [Expert judgement] The discourse is often quoted as "believe nothing, trust only yourself". Read in full, it says something different: the test includes whether "sensible people" criticise or praise a thing, and what is being tested is greed, hate, delusion and harming others, not every question of belief.
- The discourse speaks to people confused by many teachers who disparage each other. It does not discuss the scriptures of other religions, and this course does not use it to rank traditions.
- The third and fourth consolations (not quoted here) follow the same pattern: whether or not bad deeds bring bad results, someone with no ill intent can be at ease. At the end, the Kālāmas go for refuge to the Buddha, the teaching and the Saṅgha.
- "Skillful" and "unskillful" translate *kusala* and *akusala*: whether something brings benefit or not.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any study that evaluates the way of self-examination in the Kālāma Sutta as such.
- [Verified] Even scientific research on mindfulness needs checking: the 2018 critical review mentioned in Week 1 argued that the hype runs ahead of the evidence, that many studies are methodologically weak and rely heavily on self-report. (Van Dam et al. 2018)
- [Inference] Your own notes are self-report too: when we hope a habit helps, we tend to see it helping. So in the practice below, count the days you recorded, and also write down what did not change.

Limits: testing for yourself over six weeks tells you what suits you now; it does not prove the same holds for everyone.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 10–15 minutes a day.

1. **Slow reading (2 minutes).** Read one passage a day, rotating through the three.
2. **Sit with the breath (5 minutes)** as in Week 1.
3. **Days 1–3: look over your notes (5 minutes).** Open your notes from Weeks 1–5. For each habit, count the days you did it and reread what you wrote:
   - sitting with the breath;
   - the four-question frame;
   - true and gentle speech;
   - one act of non-harm;
   - loving-kindness.
4. **Days 4–5: the discourse’s questions (5 minutes).** Choose two habits. Ask: Is it skilful (does it bring benefit)? Is it blameworthy? What would a sensible person you trust say? When I do it, does it lead to welfare or harm, for me and for others?
5. **Day 6: write one tested belief.** One sentence on this pattern: "I tried ___ for ___ days; I found ___; what I am still unsure about is ___." Include what did not change.
6. **Day 7: rate and choose.** Rate each habit 0–3 (0 = does not suit me, 3 = suits me very well). Choose two habits to keep for the coming month.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Guess, then test"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Get a bowl of water and five safe objects that are not too small: a leaf, a spoon, a plastic bottle cap, a lime, a wooden toy block.
2. For each object, the child guesses first: "Float or sink?" The adult writes the guesses down.
3. Drop each one in the water to test. Compare with the guess.
4. Ask: "Did you guess any wrong? How can we know for sure?" To finish: "We know by trying."

An adult stays close the whole time the child plays with water; do not use small objects a child could swallow.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Looking over your notes makes you blame yourself for "not doing enough". This course does not grade you; a low count of days is useful information too.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
