**This week:** read two passages of the Discourse on Rolling Forth the Wheel of Dhamma (SN 56.11) slowly, rewrite the four noble truths in your own words, then each day use a four-question frame to look at one small thing that bothers you. Keep sitting with the breath for 5–10 minutes as in Week 1.

**Measurable goal:** rewrite the four points in at most four sentences; use the four-question frame on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In the Buddhist tradition, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta is the Buddha’s first teaching after his awakening, given to five ascetics in the deer park near Varanasi. It opens with the middle way and then sets out the four noble truths.

::: kinh sn56-11-sujato nguyen-van | SN 56.11, sections 2.1–2.4 (the middle way)
Mendicants, these two extremes should not be cultivated by one gone forth.
What two?
Indulgence in sensual pleasures, which is low, crude, ordinary, ignoble, and pointless. And indulgence in self-mortification, which is painful, ignoble, and pointless.
Avoiding these two extremes, the Realized One understood the middle way of practice, which gives vision and knowledge, and leads to peace, direct knowledge, awakening, and extinguishment.
:::

::: kinh sn56-11-sujato nguyen-van | SN 56.11, sections 4.1–4.10 (the four noble truths)
Now this is the noble truth of suffering.
Rebirth is suffering; old age is suffering; illness is suffering; death is suffering; being coupled with the disliked is suffering; separation from the liked is suffering; not getting what you wish for is suffering. In brief, the five grasping aggregates are suffering.

Now this is the noble truth of the origin of suffering.
It’s the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights. That is,
craving for sensual pleasures, craving for continued existence, and craving for nonexistence.

Now this is the noble truth of the cessation of suffering.
It’s the fading away and cessation of that very same craving no residue left behind; giving it away, letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it.

Now this is the noble truth of the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering.
It is simply this noble eightfold path, that is:
right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion.
:::

### Reading it in context

- [Traditional teaching] "Suffering" (dukkha) in the discourse is wider than pain: it also covers what is unsatisfying, unstable and incomplete. The list begins with rebirth because this tradition holds to a cycle of rebirths; craving "that leads to future lives" belongs to the same belief.
- "Self-mortification" is the second extreme. This tradition does not teach harming the body as a path of practice. Nor does this week’s exercise: no eating less, no sleeping less, no forcing yourself to endure.
- The eight factors of the path are, in order: right view, right purpose (often "right intention"), right speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, right immersion (often "right concentration"). Weeks 3 and 4 of this course practise two of them: speech and action.
- A later part of the discourse (not quoted here) says the Buddha understood each truth in three rounds: knowing what it is, knowing what is to be done with it, knowing it has been done. [Traditional teaching] In this tradition the four truths are understood gradually through long practice, not simply by reading.
- [Expert judgement] The four-question frame in the practice below only borrows the structure of the four truths for small everyday matters. It does not replace the full meaning of the teaching, which points towards liberation.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any study that evaluates learning about or reflecting on the four noble truths as such.
- [Verified] The closest evidence comes from acceptance-based therapies. A review of 20 meta-analyses on acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) found it effective for anxiety, depression, chronic pain and substance use, on a par with cognitive behavioural therapy. (Gloster et al. 2020)
- [Inference] ACT teaches noticing how we cling to an experience or try to push it away, which is close to the idea that "craving is the origin of suffering". But ACT is a guided therapy lasting many weeks; its results do not show that the 15-minute writing exercise here has the same effect.

Limits: this is reading and self-observation, not therapy. If your suffering right now is large (a loss, a serious illness, trauma), do not use this exercise for it; please find someone who can help.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 10–15 minutes a day.

1. **Day 1: rewrite the four points (15 minutes).** Read the two passages slowly. Then rewrite the four truths in your own words, one sentence each, four sentences at most. It need not be word-perfect; it needs to be a sentence you understand.
2. **Other days: sit with the breath (5–10 minutes)** as in Week 1.
3. **The four-question frame (5 minutes).** Pick one small thing that bothered you today, for example a traffic jam, an unanswered message, a meal that wasn’t good. Write four lines:
   - *What is bothering me?*
   - *What am I wanting to be different?*
   - *If I let go of that wanting a little, how do I feel?*
   - *What is one small, sensible thing I can do?*
4. **Tick the day.** In the evening, tick the days you used the four questions. The goal is at least 5 of 7.

Choose only small matters. The third question does not mean accepting what is wrong: if someone is harming you, the sensible thing is to find help.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Just enough"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Get a cup, a small jug of water and a cloth; play at the sink or outside.
2. The child pours very little water: "Is that enough to drink?" Then fills the cup until it spills: "Can you hold it?" Then pours just enough.
3. The adult says: "Too little isn’t enough, too much spills over. Just enough is just right."
4. Ask: "What did you do just enough of today? Playing, eating, watching TV?"

Do not use this game to comment on how much the child eats. If the child gets bored, stop.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Reading about suffering makes you feel low, stays with you as sadness, or leads to thoughts like "life is nothing but suffering". Then drop the writing and keep only sitting with the breath, or pause.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
