**This week:** practise sorting thoughts into two kinds: those that lead to harm and those that do not. Sit for 30 minutes a day, reviewing one part of the course each day. At the end of the week, rate yourself and choose how to practise after the course.

**Measurable goal:** sit for 30 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; each evening, write down how many "harm basket" thoughts you noticed; at the end of the week, complete the 8-week self-rating and write a practice plan for the next 4 weeks.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The Dvedhāvitakka Sutta belongs to the Middle Discourses. In it, the Buddha recalls how he worked with thoughts before his awakening. The tradition teaches that what we often think about gradually becomes the mind’s inclination, so choosing which thoughts to nourish is part of practice.

::: kinh mn19-sujato nguyen-van | MN 19, sections 2.2–2.6
‘Why don’t I meditate by continually dividing my thoughts into two classes?’ So I assigned sensual, malicious, and cruel thoughts to one class. And I assigned thoughts of renunciation, good will, and harmlessness to the second class.
:::

::: kinh mn19-sujato nguyen-van | MN 19, sections 6.1 and 11.1
Whatever a mendicant frequently thinks about and considers becomes their heart’s inclination.
:::

::: kinh mn19-sujato nguyen-van | MN 19, sections 12.1–12.4
Suppose it’s the last month of summer, when all the crops have been gathered within a village, and a cowherd must take care of the cattle. While at the root of a tree or in the open he need only be mindful that the cattle are there. In the same way I needed only to be mindful that those things were there.
:::

### Reading it in context

- The sentence in the second passage appears twice in the discourse: once before the part on the mind inclining to bad thoughts (6.1), and once before the part on the mind inclining to skilful thoughts (11.1).
- Between them there is another simile: at the end of the rainy season, when the crops are still in the fields, the cowherd has to use a staff to keep the cattle out of the crops (section 7). The two cowherd similes show two stages: at first close attention is needed, later it is enough to know "they are there".
- The discourse also says that thinking even skilful thoughts for too long tires the body and stresses the mind, so the Buddha settled his mind in immersion (sections 8.8–8.11). Practising a lot does not mean practising tensely.
- The later part of the discourse describes the absorptions and three knowledges gained on the night of awakening, including recollection of past lives. [Traditional teaching] These are central beliefs of the tradition; this course does not ask learners to accept them, and does not go into that part.
- "Renunciation" (*nekkhamma*) in a broad sense means letting go of some sensual desire; for lay people, it may be the times you choose "enough" instead of "more".

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A 2016 individual patient data meta-analysis (9 trials, 1,258 people with recurrent depression in remission) found that mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) reduced the risk of relapse within 60 weeks compared with not receiving MBCT (hazard ratio 0.69) and compared with other active treatments (0.79). (Kuyken et al. 2016)
- [Verified] A review of 47 randomised trials (3,515 people): mindfulness meditation reduced anxiety and depression by small to moderate amounts, and was not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
- [Inference] MBCT has trained teachers and is for people with a history of depression. This self-study course is not MBCT and does not replace treatment for depression. These findings suggest regular practice may help in the long run; they do not tell us how much this course helps.

Limits: if you are being treated for depression, do not stop medication or treatment on your own because you are meditating; talk to your doctor.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 30–35 minutes a day.

1. **Slow reading (3 minutes).** Read the three passages.
2. **Review one part each day (the first 10 minutes of sitting).** Day 1: the breath. Day 2: the four elements. Day 3: feelings. Day 4: the second arrow. Day 5: mind weather. Day 6: the five guests. Day 7: your choice.
3. **Two baskets (15 minutes).** When a thought appears, put it in a basket: the "harm basket" (strong craving, ill will, wanting to hurt someone) or the "no-harm basket" (letting go, good will, harmlessness). Neutral thoughts (to-do lists, plans) go outside. No need to deal with them, just sort. If a "harm basket" thought keeps coming back, use the approaches from Week 7.
4. **Like the cowherd in the dry season (last 5 minutes).** Relax, stop watching closely; just know "the breath is there, the body is there".
5. **End-of-week self-rating.** Rate each practice from 0 to 3 (0 = could not practise it, 3 = practised regularly and found it useful): the breath; slow walking and the four elements; feelings; the second arrow; mind weather; the five guests; the four approaches to thoughts; the two baskets. Keep the two with the highest scores.
6. **A 4-week plan.** Write down: your daily sitting time, the number of minutes (20–30), the two practices you keep, and one day a week to reread a passage from the discourses.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Two baskets"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Set out two baskets or boxes: one with a heart on it (the "helping basket"), one with a thorn on it (the "hurting basket").
2. Write or draw a few thoughts on cards: "I want to share my cake with my little brother", "I want to grab my friend's toy", "I want to help Mum", "I want to tease my friend until they cry".
3. The child reads (or hears the adult read) each card and puts it in the right basket.
4. Say: "Everyone sometimes has a thought from the hurting basket. We notice it, then choose to act from the helping basket."

Do not use cards about serious fighting or about times the child has been hurt. Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: [Secret good deeds](https://daynentang.com/khoa-hoc/gia-dinh-2-8/tuan-3/g3-viec-tot-bi-mat/) (in Vietnamese).

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Sorting thoughts into the "harm basket" leaves you feeling bad or guilty for a long time. If so, drop the sorting and just sit with the breath.
- If there are serious warning signs, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

Before you sleep, recall one "no-harm basket" thought you had today, and stay with it for one more minute.
