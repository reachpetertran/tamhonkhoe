**This week:** when a thought of wanting or irritation keeps coming back while you sit, try the four approaches from the discourse in turn. Sit for 30 minutes a day (25 minutes if 30 is still too long) and note which approach helped.

**Measurable goal:** sit for at least 25 minutes on 5 of 7 days; record at least 3 times during the week that you tried the approaches, and which one helped the thought settle.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The Vitakkasaṇṭhāna Sutta belongs to the Middle Discourses. After knowing the mind and the hindrances (Weeks 5–6), this discourse teaches what to do when an unskilful thought, connected with greed, hate or delusion, keeps coming back. The tradition presents five approaches in order: each later one is used only when the earlier one has not worked.

::: kinh mn20-sujato nguyen-van | MN 20, sections 2.1 and 3.1–3.4 (approach 1: replace the thought)
“Mendicants, a mendicant committed to the higher mind should focus on five subjects from time to time.

Take a mendicant who is focusing on some subject that gives rise to bad, unskillful thoughts connected with desire, hate, and delusion. That mendicant should focus on some other subject connected with the skillful. As they do so, those bad thoughts are given up and come to an end. Their mind becomes stilled internally; it settles, unifies, and becomes immersed in samādhi. It’s like a deft mason or their apprentice who’d knock out or extract a large peg with a finer peg.
:::

::: kinh mn20-sujato nguyen-van | MN 20, sections 4.1–4.2, 5.1 and 5.4–5.5 (approaches 2 and 3)
Now, suppose that mendicant is focusing on some other subject connected with the skillful, but bad, unskillful thoughts connected with desire, hate, and delusion keep coming up. They should examine the drawbacks of those thoughts: ‘So these thoughts are unskillful, they’re blameworthy, and they result in suffering.’

Now, suppose that mendicant is examining the drawbacks of those thoughts, but bad, unskillful thoughts connected with desire, hate, and delusion keep coming up. They should try to forget and ignore them. … Suppose there was a person with clear eyes, and some undesirable sights came into their range of vision. They’d just close their eyes or look away.
:::

::: kinh mn20-sujato nguyen-van | MN 20, sections 6.1, 6.4–6.8 and 6.21 (approach 4: calming step by step)
Now, suppose that mendicant is ignoring and forgetting about those thoughts, but bad, unskillful thoughts connected with desire, hate, and delusion keep coming up. They should focus on stopping the formation of those thoughts.

Suppose there was a person walking quickly. They’d think: ‘Why am I walking so quickly? Why don’t I slow down?’ So they’d slow down. … And so that person would shun successively coarser postures and adopt more subtle ones.
:::

### Reading it in context

- The simile in approach 4 continues: walking slowly, then standing still, then sitting down, then lying down (sections 6.9–6.20). The point is calming step by step, not slamming on the brakes.
- In approach 2, the discourse has a simile of a carcass hung around the neck to convey revulsion at bad thoughts (section 4.5). This course does not quote it.
- Approach 5 (sections 7.1–7.2), used only when the first four have not worked: with teeth clenched and tongue pressed to the roof of the mouth, crush mind with mind. [Traditional teaching] The tradition places it last, as a final resort. This self-study course does not practise approach 5, because forcing thoughts down can make them return more often (see the science section), and it is better learned with a teacher.
- The discourse is about "unskilful" thoughts connected with greed, hate and delusion. It is not about traumatic memories or unwanted intrusive thoughts. Those need other approaches, usually with professional guidance.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A 2001 meta-analysis of controlled studies found that trying to suppress a thought produces a small to moderate "rebound": the thought comes back more often. The size of the effect depended on the kind of thought and how it was measured. (Abramowitz, Tolin & Street 2001)
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 29 trials (2,535 people) of MBCT found that the programme reduced rumination by a moderate amount. (Wei et al. 2025)
- [Inference] Approach 1 (replacing the thought) and approach 4 (calming step by step) are closer to redirecting attention than to suppression. Approach 3 ("try to forget and ignore") can be read as not feeding the thought, like the person who looks away; if it turns into forcing the thought down, it may rebound. No study has tested the five approaches of the discourse directly.

Limits: most research on thought suppression was done in laboratories, with assigned thoughts (for example "don't think of a white bear"), not real-life thoughts.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 30–35 minutes a day.

1. **Slow reading (3 minutes).** Read the three passages.
2. **Sit with the breath (10 minutes).**
3. **When a thought of wanting or irritation keeps returning (the remaining 15 minutes).** Try these in order, a few breaths each:
    1. *Replace:* put your attention on something skilful, for example a good wish for the very person who annoyed you, or return to the breath with clearer attention.
    2. *Look at the drawbacks:* ask silently: "Does this thought harm me, others, or both?"
    3. *Pay it no attention:* like looking away. Let the thought be there, but do not answer it or follow it.
    4. *Calm it step by step:* ask "Where does this thought come from?", then calm down in stages, like the person walking fast who slows down, then stands, then sits: breathe out more slowly, let the shoulders and jaw loosen.
4. **Write it down (2 minutes).** What was the thought? Which approaches did you try? Which helped?

If no thought keeps coming back, just sit with the breath. There is no need to go looking for bad thoughts to practise on.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Change the channel"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. The adult says: "Our heads are like a TV. Sometimes the 'grumpy' channel keeps playing."
2. Draw three "happy channels" with the child: a happy memory, a favourite toy, someone they love.
3. Tell a small story: "A friend took your crayons, and you stay grumpy for ages." The child picks a happy channel to "switch to" and says one sentence about it.
4. Ask: "After changing the channel, do you feel more grumpy or less?"

Changing the channel does not mean the upsetting thing does not matter: if something real happened, the adult still listens and helps sort it out. Daynentang has a lesson for families with children aged 2–8: [Owning and fixing mistakes](https://daynentang.com/khoa-hoc/gia-dinh-2-8/tuan-6/g6-nhan-loi-va-sua-loi/) (in Vietnamese).

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The returning thought is a traumatic memory, an unwanted frightening image, or an urge to do something over and over. Do not use this lesson to push them down; seek a professional.
- The harder you try, the more the thought returns and the more tense you become.
- If there are serious warning signs, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
