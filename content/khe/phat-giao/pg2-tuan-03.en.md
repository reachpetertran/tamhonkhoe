**This week:** practise noticing the "tone" of experience: pleasant, painful or neutral. Sit for 20 minutes a day; during the day, pause 3 times to name the tone of that moment.

**Measurable goal:** sit for 20 minutes on at least 5 of 7 days; pause 3 times a day to name the feeling tone on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] The second place in the Discourse on Mindfulness Meditation is feeling (*vedanā*). Buddhism teaches that every experience has a tone that is pleasant, painful or neutral, and that greed or aversion often arises right after that tone. Knowing a feeling as it first appears is a place where the mind need not be carried away.

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, sections 32.1–32.4
And how does a mendicant meditate observing an aspect of feelings? It’s when a mendicant who feels a pleasant feeling knows: ‘I feel a pleasant feeling.’ When they feel a painful feeling, they know: ‘I feel a painful feeling.’ When they feel a neutral feeling, they know: ‘I feel a neutral feeling.’
:::

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, sections 32.5–32.6
When they feel a pleasant feeling of the flesh, they know: ‘I feel a pleasant feeling of the flesh.’ When they feel a pleasant feeling not of the flesh, they know: ‘I feel a pleasant feeling not of the flesh.’
:::

The next four sentences (sections 32.7–32.10) follow the same pattern for painful and neutral feelings, of the flesh or not of the flesh.

::: kinh mn10-sujato nguyen-van | MN 10, sections 33.2–33.3
With respect to feelings, they meditate observing the liability to originate, to vanish, and to originate and vanish. Or mindfulness is established that feelings exist, to the extent necessary for knowledge and mindfulness. They meditate independent, not grasping at anything in the world.
:::

### Reading it in context

- "Feeling" here is not emotion (joy, sadness, anger). It is the simpler tone that comes with every experience: pleasant, unpleasant, or neither. Anger belongs to the mind, which comes in Week 5.
- "Of the flesh" (*sāmisa*) and "not of the flesh" (*nirāmisa*): commentators usually take these as feelings tied to worldly sensual pleasures, and feelings that arise from spiritual practice, such as the joy of a settled mind. Other translations say "worldly" and "spiritual".
- [Traditional teaching] The tradition does not teach that you must give up pleasant feelings or push painful ones away; only that you know them clearly and see them arise and pass.

## 2. What science finds

- [Verified] A review of 47 randomised trials (3,515 people) found that mindfulness meditation reduced anxiety, depression and pain by small to moderate amounts, and was not better than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)
- [Verified] About 8% of meditators reported an adverse experience in a review of 83 studies, most often anxiety or depression. (Farias et al. 2020)
- [Inference] We have not found a research synthesis specifically on noting feelings as pleasant, painful or neutral. This week’s exercise rests on the teaching, without direct evidence.

Limits: these figures are averages across many studies and do not predict what will happen for any one person.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 20–25 minutes a day, plus three short pauses.

1. **Slow reading (2 minutes).** Read the three passages.
2. **Sit with the breath (5 minutes).** Let the mind settle.
3. **Noting feelings (15 minutes).** Let your attention widen to the whole body. When a sensation appears (an itch, warmth, a numb leg, traffic outside, a pleasant smell), silently note its tone: "pleasant", "painful" or "neutral". There is no need to work out the cause. Then notice how it changes or fades.
4. **When the tone is unclear.** "Neutral" is enough. Most sensations in a day are neutral; noticing that is a discovery too.
5. **Three pauses during the day (1 minute each).** For example before lunch, when reading a message, before bed. Ask: "Is this moment pleasant, painful or neutral?" Write one word in your notebook.
6. **Tick the day.** Write down your minutes of sitting and the number of pauses.

If a painful sensation in the body grows strong (for example a leg going numb and sore), change position. Noting feelings does not mean sitting through pain.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Three boxes"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Draw three faces on three sheets of paper: a smiling face, a frowning face, an ordinary face.
2. Gather a few safe objects: a soft cloth, the rough side of a washing-up sponge, a spoon, an orange.
3. The child closes their eyes, touches or smells each one, then puts it next to the face that matches how it felt.
4. There is no right answer: the same thing can feel pleasant to one person and ordinary to another.

Do not use anything hot, icy or sharp. If the child does not want to close their eyes, let them keep them open.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Paying attention to an unpleasant sensation makes it worse and it does not ease when you open your eyes, change position or move your attention to your feet.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.
