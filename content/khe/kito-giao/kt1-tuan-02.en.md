**This week:** read slowly the seven "Blessed are…" lines at the start of the Sermon on the Mount, lingering each day on one line.

**Measurable goal:** read in four steps on at least 5 of 7 days, and write one line in a notebook after each reading.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In the Gospel of Matthew, Jesus goes up a mountain, sits down and teaches his disciples. The sermon opens with lines that begin "Blessed are…", usually called the Beatitudes. The tradition teaches that God sees and stands with those whom ordinary life counts as weak: the poor, those who mourn, the gentle, those who hunger for justice, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers.

::: kinh web-bible nguyen-van | Matthew 5:3–9
“Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.

Blessed are those who mourn, for they shall be comforted.

Blessed are the gentle, for they shall inherit the earth.

Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall be filled.

Blessed are the merciful, for they shall obtain mercy.

Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God.

Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God.
:::

### Reading it in context

- "Poor in spirit" is commonly understood as people who know they need God. Luke's version (6:20) says simply "the poor". The churches read the two together.
- Verses 10–12, right after, speak of people persecuted for righteousness. This week reads only the first seven lines. (The quotation mark at the start stays open in the source because the sermon continues.)
- [Inference] The Beatitudes are promises and invitations, not orders to go looking for suffering. "Blessed are those who mourn" does not mean your sorrow is something good to hold on to.
- [Expert judgement] Each line has two halves: who is blessed, and why. Reading each half slowly helps you see that shape.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any study that evaluates slow reading of the Beatitudes, or lectio divina in general.
- [Verified] The closest evidence is still the meta-analysis of 97 studies on therapy that integrates the client's faith (see Week 1): the added religious part did not reduce distress more than standard therapy, but increased spiritual well-being. (Captari et al. 2018)
- [Inference] Writing one line after reading lets you look back at which line you stopped on across the week. It is a note-taking habit, not a tested intervention.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 12–15 minutes a day, with the same four steps as Week 1.

1. **Settle (1 minute).**
2. **Read (3 minutes).** Read all seven lines once. The second time, read slowly the line for today: day 1 verse 3, day 2 verse 4… day 7 verse 9.
3. **Meditate (4 minutes).** Who do you know who "mourns", is "gentle", "makes peace"? Where are you in this line?
4. **Respond (2 minutes).** Christians: pray in your own words. Others: write one sentence in response.
5. **Be still (2 minutes).**
6. **Write one line (1 minute).** For example: "Verse 7: thought of my mother, who is merciful." It does not need to be good, only true.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- The line about mourning or hungering for righteousness stirs up a grief or an injustice you are living with. This reading does not replace finding someone to help.
- The reading makes you feel guilty for "not being gentle enough, not pure enough".
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
