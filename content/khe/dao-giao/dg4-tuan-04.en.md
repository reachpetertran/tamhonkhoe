**This week:** read a short passage from chapter XIX of the *Zhuangzi* about a gambler, and paragraph 3 of chapter 64 of the *Tao Te Ching* about ruining things when they are nearly done. Carry both into a working day: before, during and after one important task.

**Measurable goal:** one minute of "putting the stake down" before an important task, on at least 5 of 7 days; two minutes checking the last step of that task, on at least 5 of 7 days.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] In chapter XIX of the *Zhuangzi*, the disciple Yen Yüan tells of a boatman who crosses a rapid with great skill. Confucius explains with the example of a gambler: the skill stays the same, but the bigger the stake, the shakier the hand. Chapter 64 of the *Tao Te Ching* adds another place where work goes wrong: not at the start, but near the end.

::: kinh zhuangzi-giles nguyen-van | chapter XIX, Confucius on the gambler
"A man who plays for counters will play well. If he stakes his girdle, he will be nervous; if yellow gold, he will lose his wits. His skill is the same in each case, but he is distracted by the value of his stake. And every one who attaches importance to the external, becomes internally without resource."
:::

::: kinh ddk-legge nguyen-van | chapter 64, paragraph 3
3\. He who acts (with an ulterior purpose) does harm; he who takes hold of a thing (in the same way) loses his hold. The sage does not act (so), and therefore does no harm; he does not lay hold (so), and therefore does not lose his hold. (But) people in their conduct of affairs are constantly ruining them when they are on the eve of success. If they were careful at the end, as (they should be) at the beginning, they would not so ruin them.
:::

Giles’s footnote is left out.

### Reading it in context

- According to Giles’s note, the girdle is where people kept their loose cash. [Expert judgement] The Chinese names three different stakes (tiles, a belt buckle, gold); Giles translates freely. The point is the same: the more precious the stake, the more the player loses his composure.
- Confucius here is again a character in the *Zhuangzi*’s parables (see Week 1). The course quotes only the passage about the gambler and not the part about the boatman who treats "an upset as an ordinary cart accident", so that it is not read as advice to take danger lightly.
- The passage uses gambling as an image; the course does not encourage gambling.
- Words in round brackets in chapter 64 are Legge’s additions to make the English clear.
- [Inference] "Putting the stake down" does not mean caring less about responsibilities. You still care about the result; the exercise only separates the fear of the result from the hands doing the work. For work with real danger (driving, machinery, health care, electricity), your trade’s safety procedures always come first.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any systematic review that evaluates "putting the stake down" or "checking the last step" as exercises.
- [Verified] A meta-analysis of 86 publications (38,124 workers): people who detach from work when off duty report less exhaustion and sleep better. This is a correlation. (Wendsche & Lohmann-Haislah 2017)
- [Inference] This result relates to step 4 (stepping back when the work is done); it does not prove anything about steps 2 and 3.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 10 minutes a day, spread through the working day.

1. **Slow reading (3 minutes, morning).** Read both passages. Choose one important task for today: a meeting, a report, a class, a difficult call, a household job that needs care.
2. **Put the stake down (1 minute, just before the task).** Name the stake in a short phrase: "afraid of criticism", "afraid of losing the client", "afraid the boss won’t be pleased". Take three slow breaths. Say silently: "The skill is the same." Then work as you would when practising.
3. **Careful at the end (2 minutes, before calling it done).** Check the last step as carefully as the first: the email goes to the right person with all the attachments; the figures have been checked; the stove is off; the door is locked.
4. **Step back when it is done (2 minutes, end of the working day).** Write down what is unfinished for tomorrow, then make a sign that work has ended: turn off work notifications, put the laptop away, change your clothes.
5. **End of the week.** Look back: which stake makes your hands shake the most?

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Paper balls, light hands"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. Scrunch five balls out of scrap paper. Put a basket two steps away.
2. Round 1: "practice round", throw all five.
3. Round 2: the adult says playfully: "This is the golden round!" Throw again. Ask: "How do your hands feel?"
4. Round 3: take one breath together, say "paper balls are still paper balls", then throw as in the practice round.
5. To finish: "When we’re not worried, our hands are lighter."

No rewards, no punishments, no counting who loses. If round 2 makes the child really anxious, skip it and just play for fun.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- "Checking the last step" turns into checking again and again without end. Then check once only, from a short list written in advance.
- Fear before work is so strong that you lose sleep for several nights or avoid work altogether. Then it is worth talking to a professional.
- If the signs are serious, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
