**This week:** read the story of the *li* tree in chapter IV of the *Zhuangzi* slowly, do one 30-minute session each day (sitting, or sitting then slow walking), and spend 10 minutes doing something not aimed at achieving anything.

**Measurable goal:** one 30-minute session (30 minutes sitting, or 20 minutes sitting and 10 minutes slow walking) on at least 5 of 7 days; one 10-minute stretch of "not being useful" on at least 5 of 7 days.

**A reminder:** this is the longest week of the course. If you felt unsteady while sitting last week, keep to 20–25 minutes. This lesson is about letting go of the need to "be useful"; it does not say that anyone is useless.

## 1. Traditional teaching

[Traditional teaching] Near the end of chapter IV of the *Zhuangzi* is the story of a carpenter who passes a huge, sacred *li* tree. He does not bother to look, because its wood is good for nothing. That night the tree appears in his dream and tells him that it is precisely by being useless that it has lived so long. The chapter closes with one sentence: everyone knows the use of useful things, but not the use of useless things.

::: kinh zhuangzi-giles nguyen-van | chapter IV, the li tree
A certain artisan was travelling to the Ch'i State. On reaching Ch'ü-yüan, he saw a sacred *li* tree, large enough to hide an ox behind it, a hundred spans in girth, towering up ten cubits over the hill top, and carrying behind it branches, many tens of the smallest of which were of a size for boats. Crowds stood gazing at it, but our artisan took no notice, and went on his way without even casting a look behind. His apprentice however gazed his fill, and when he caught up his master, said, "Ever since I have handled an adze in your service, I have never seen such a splendid piece of timber as that. How was it that you, sir, did not care to stop and look at it?"

"It's not worth talking about," replied his master. "It's good for nothing. Make a boat of it,—'twould sink. A coffin,—'twould rot. Furniture,—'twould soon break down. A door,—'twould sweat. A pillar,—'twould be worm-eaten. It is wood of no quality, and of no use. That is why it has attained its present age."

When the artisan reached home, he dreamt that the tree appeared to him in a dream and spoke as follows:—"What is it that you compare me with? Is it with the more elegant trees?—The cherry-apple, the pear, the orange, the pumelo, and other fruit-bearers, as soon as their fruit ripens are stripped and treated with indignity. The great boughs are snapped off, the small ones scattered abroad. Thus do these trees by their own value injure their own lives. They cannot fulfil their allotted span of years, but perish prematurely in mid-career from their entanglement with the world around them. Thus it is with all things. For a long period my aim was to be useless. Many times I was in danger, but at length I succeeded, and so became useful as I am to-day. But had I then been of use, I should not now be of the great use I am. Moreover, you and I belong both to the same category of things. Have done then with this criticism of others. Is a good-for-nothing fellow whose dangers are not yet passed a fit person to talk of a good-for-nothing tree?"
:::

::: kinh zhuangzi-giles nguyen-van | chapter IV, last sentence
All men know the use of useful things; but they do not know the use of useless things.
:::

### Reading it in context

- [Expert judgement] The tree's name in the Chinese is 櫟 (*li*), which Giles notes is a worthless species of oak; the place is Ch'ü-yüan (曲轅). The course leaves out Giles's notes.
- The story goes on briefly: the apprentice asks why, if the tree wanted to be useless, it let itself be worshipped; the master replies that being sacred was only a way of escaping the axe. The course's last sentence comes at the very end of the chapter, in the song of the eccentric Chieh Yü as he passes Confucius's door in the State of Ch'u.
- [Inference] "Useless" here means useless by the carpenter's measure, that is, not good as timber to sell. The story does not tell readers to stop working or stop helping others; it questions the yardstick of "usefulness" we tend to apply to everything, including ourselves.
- The story mentions a coffin as one of the things wood might be made into; the lesson does not go into the subject of death.

## 2. What science finds

- We have not found any study that evaluates doing things "not meant to be useful", as in this week's exercise.
- [Verified] Longer sittings can bring difficult experiences: a review of 83 studies found that about 8% of meditators had adverse experiences (about 4% in experimental studies). (Farias et al. 2020)
- [Verified] An interview study of Western meditators recorded many factors that influence whether a difficult experience becomes more or less severe, including factors in the practitioner, in the practice, in relationships and in health habits. (Lindahl et al. 2017)
- [Verified] Mindfulness meditation programmes reduce anxiety and depression by a small-to-moderate amount (47 trials, 3,515 people), no more than other active treatments. (Goyal et al. 2014)

Limits: a 30-minute session is practice, not treatment. Sitting longer does not mean practising better.

## 3. Practice for adults

About 35 minutes a day, plus 10 minutes of "not being useful".

1. **Slow reading (3 minutes).** Read the story once. On the second reading, pause at "Have done then with this criticism of others".
2. **A 30-minute session.** Choose one of two ways:
    - *Sit for 30 minutes:* begin with listening (as in Week 5) for about 5 minutes, then just sit with the fewest instructions (as in Week 4). If your legs go numb or your back aches, shift position slowly; do not force yourself to stay still.
    - *Sit for 20 minutes, walk slowly for 10:* walk very slowly in a room or a yard, feeling your feet touch the ground, turning at the end of your path.
    - At the end: open your eyes, put your feet on the floor, look around, and name three things before you stand up.
3. **Ten minutes of "not being useful".** Do something that is not meant to achieve anything: look at a tree, listen to birds, watch clouds, sit on the porch. No photos, no notes, and do not turn it into "training" to get better at something.
4. **Write one line (2 minutes).** When did I hear "what's the use of this?" in my head? What did it say?
5. **Tick the day.** The goal is at least 5 of 7 days for both.

## 4. Kids' corner 6+

**Game: "Sitting with a tree"** (5–10 minutes, with an adult)

1. With the child, find a tree in the yard or a park, or a potted plant indoors.
2. Sit next to it for 3 minutes. Look together: are the leaves moving, are there any ants or birds, where is the tree's shadow?
3. Ask: "Does the tree need to do anything? What is it giving us?" (Shade, a place for birds to perch, something green to look at.)
4. To finish: "A tree only needs to be a tree."

Choose a safe spot away from traffic. No climbing, no breaking branches. Do not use the word "useless" with the child.

## 5. Stop practising if

- You feel panic, have flashbacks of trauma, have a lasting sense that you or the world are "not real", lose sleep for several nights, or have thoughts of harming yourself.
- Thoughts like "I'm useless" or "I'm a burden to everyone" keep coming back for several days. This is the time to talk with someone you trust or to get help now, not the time to sit longer.
- A sense of being detached from your body, or of everything seeming strange, stays after you get up; if it lasts more than a day, see a doctor.
- Your legs, back or joints hurt a lot during long sitting. Then sit on a chair, or choose 20 minutes of sitting and 10 minutes of slow walking.
- If so, stop and see [Get help now](https://daynentang.com/en/get-help/).
- If you have a history of psychosis, bipolar disorder or severe PTSD, ask a doctor before sitting for longer than 20 minutes.

## 7. Practice for today

When you hear "what's the use of this?" in your head, try answering: "It doesn't need to be useful. It only needs to be here."
